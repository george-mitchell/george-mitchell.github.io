Chapter 4
Generalized linear models

Lectures 7–8 · probabilities and counts: links, logistic and Poisson regression, and the fairness of a risk score

The linear model of Chapter 3 says that an outcome scatters in a bell shape around a straight line. That is a fine description of heights and log earnings. It is a poor description of whether a defendant is re-arrested (a yes or a no), how many incidents an airline has in fifteen years (a count, never negative, often zero), or which of several options a voter chooses. For outcomes like these the normal distribution is the wrong likelihood, and a straight line for the mean will sooner or later predict impossible values: probabilities above one, negative counts.

The generalized linear model (GLM) keeps everything that made the linear model useful (a linear predictor built from the variables you choose, priors on its coefficients, a causal graph that says which variables belong) and changes two things: the likelihood, which is chosen to respect the kind of outcome you have, and a link function, which bends the linear predictor so that it always lands in the range the outcome allows. Logistic regression for yes-or-no outcomes and Poisson regression for counts are the two most important members of the family, and between them they cover a very large share of applied research.

The price of the link function is that coefficients no longer mean what they meant in Chapter 3. A coefficient of 0.8 is no longer "0.8 more of the outcome"; it is 0.8 on the scale of log-odds or log-rates, and what that means for the outcome depends on everything else. Learning to translate coefficients back to the outcome scale, and to report results there, is most of the skill of using GLMs well.

  • Lecture 7 is about probabilities. We will build logistic regression from the link function up, set priors on the log-odds scale, and fit it to the data at the centre of the most famous recent controversy in applied statistics: the COMPAS criminal risk score, analysed by ProPublica in 2016. We will learn to report effects as differences in probability, and to check whether a model's probabilities can be trusted.
  • Lecture 8 is about fairness and counts. We will see why the COMPAS debate could not be settled by better statistics, and what that teaches about choosing an estimand. Then we turn to counts, with fifteen years of incidents at 56 airlines, and meet exposure, overdispersion, the negative binomial and regression to the mean.

Think About It: Before You Start

A risk tool flags defendants as "high risk". Among those it flags, 65% of Black defendants and 59% of white defendants are re-arrested within two years: the flag means roughly the same thing for both groups. Yet among defendants who are not re-arrested, the tool flagged 42% of Black defendants but only 22% of white defendants. Is the tool fair? Write down your answer and your reasons; Section 4.7 shows why both facts can be true at once, and why no tool could avoid a version of this.

Lecture 7 — Probabilities

4.1 When a straight line breaks

Suppose yi is 1 if defendant i is re-arrested within two years and 0 otherwise. The quantity we want to model is the probability pi=Pr(yi=1). The simplest idea is to make pi a straight line in a predictor such as the number of prior arrests. This linear probability model is common in economics and often harmless near the middle of the data, but it has a fatal flaw: lines go on forever, and probabilities do not. A line steep enough to describe the difference between zero and five prior arrests will predict a probability above 1 for someone with thirty.

The GLM solution has three ingredients, and you have already met the reasoning behind each.

  1. A likelihood that matches the outcome. A yes-or-no outcome with probability pi is a Bernoulli variable; a count of yeses out of n independent tries is binomial. Section 2.8 showed these are the maximum-entropy distributions for binary outcomes with a given mean, so they assume nothing beyond the probability itself.
  2. A linear predictor, exactly as in Chapter 3: ηi=α+β1x1i+⋯+βKxKi, which can be any real number.
  3. A link function connecting the two. For probabilities, the standard choice is the logit, the logarithm of the odds: logit(pi)=log⁡pi1−pi=ηi⟺pi=11+e−ηi. The right-hand function, the inverse logit or logistic function, squeezes the whole real line into the interval from 0 to 1.

Definition: Generalized Linear Model

A generalized linear model for an outcome y has three parts: a likelihood from the exponential family with mean parameter θi (for example yi∼Bernoulli(pi) or yi∼Poisson(λi)); a linear predictor ηi=α+∑kβkxki; and a link function g with g(θi)=ηi. Logistic regression uses the Bernoulli or binomial likelihood with the logit link. Poisson regression uses the Poisson likelihood with the log link, log⁡λi=ηi. The linear model of Chapter 3 is the special case with a normal likelihood and the identity link.

The logistic function is S-shaped: nearly flat when η is very negative (the probability is close to 0 and hard to move), steepest at η=0 (where p=1/2), and nearly flat again when η is large. So a coefficient no longer has a single meaning on the probability scale. A change of one unit in x always changes the log-odds by β, but it changes the probability a lot when p is near one-half and very little when p is near 0 or 1. Explore this below.

From a line to an S-curve

The linear predictor is η=α+βx. The solid curve is the probability p=logit−1(η). The dashed line is the straight line that touches the curve at x=0, as a linear probability model would draw it. The amber dot and tick mark show where the curve is steepest.

Two facts from the widget are worth memorising. First, the steepest slope of the probability curve, reached where p=1/2, is exactly β/4. This gives the divide-by-4 rule (Gelman, Hill and Vehtari, 2020): a logistic coefficient divided by 4 is an upper bound on the change in probability per unit of x. A coefficient of 0.8 means "at most about 20 percentage points per unit". Second, far from p=1/2 the effect on probability is much smaller, so the same coefficient implies different changes in probability for different people. Logistic regression has interactions built in: the effect of one predictor on the probability depends on the values of all the others.

History: Population Curves and Parole Boards

The logistic curve was invented in the 1830s by the Belgian mathematician Pierre-François Verhulst to describe a population that grows quickly and then levels off as it approaches a limit. It was rediscovered for biology in the 1920s, and the "logit" was named by Joseph Berkson in 1944, who championed it against the then-standard "probit" (based on the normal distribution). The first statistical risk tool in criminal justice came even earlier: in 1928 the Chicago sociologist Ernest Burgess studied 3,000 parolees, scored each on 21 factors, giving one point for each factor associated with success, and showed that the total predicted success on parole well. Illinois used versions of his scale for decades. COMPAS is a direct descendant, with more questions, more statistics and the same basic question underneath: what is the probability that this person will be arrested again?

A logistic regression has a coefficient of 0.8 on a predictor. Using the divide-by-4 rule, what is the largest change in probability that a one-unit increase in the predictor can produce? (Give a proportion, such as 0.15.)

The steepest point of the logistic curve has slope β/4.

0.8/4=0.2: at most about 20 percentage points per unit, reached for people whose probability is near one-half. For people with probabilities near 0 or 1 the change is much smaller.

4.2 A risk score and its data

Courts across the United States use risk assessment instruments to inform decisions about bail, sentencing and parole. One of the most widely used is COMPAS, sold by the company Northpointe (now Equivant). A defendant answers a questionnaire of 137 items, and the software produces a "decile score" from 1 (lowest risk) to 10 (highest) for the risk of committing another crime. In 2016 the news organisation ProPublica obtained the scores of more than 7,000 people arrested in Broward County, Florida, in 2013 and 2014, linked them to criminal records, and recorded whether each person was arrested again within two years. Their article, "Machine Bias" (Angwin et al., 2016), argued that the scores were biased against Black defendants. The data and code were published on GitHub, and they have since become a standard test case for fairness in prediction.

We load the data with exactly the filters ProPublica used: keep people whose score was computed within 30 days of their arrest, whose two-year outcome is known, whose charge was an ordinary felony or misdemeanour, and whose score is valid.

Research Practice: Ask What the Outcome Measures

The outcome here is not "committed another crime"; it is "was arrested for another crime in Broward County within two years". Arrests depend on behaviour, but also on where police patrol, whom they stop and which offences they pursue. If policing is more intensive in some neighbourhoods, the recorded outcome (and the recorded prior arrests used as a predictor) will be higher there for the same behaviour. Machine-learning researchers call this label bias; measurement theorists call it a problem of construct validity. Every paper should state precisely what its outcome measures, how it was recorded, and what it does not capture, and should discuss how measurement could differ between the groups being compared. Many published disagreements about COMPAS turn out to be disagreements about this one sentence.

4.3 Priors on the log-odds scale

Priors for a logistic regression live on the log-odds scale, where intuition is weak. Section 2.9 warned that a "vague" prior on one scale can be strongly informative on another. Here is the classic example: the prior Normal(0, 10) on a log-odds intercept, which looks agnostic, and the prior Normal(0, 1.5), which looks restrictive. What do they say about the probability?

R
set.seed(1)
prior_p <- tibble(sd = rep(c(10, 1.5), each = 1e4)) |>
  mutate(alpha = rnorm(n(), 0, sd), p = plogis(alpha),
         prior = factor(paste0("alpha ~ Normal(0, ", sd, ")"),
                        levels = c("alpha ~ Normal(0, 10)", "alpha ~ Normal(0, 1.5)")))
prior_p |> group_by(prior) |> summarise(`share below 0.01 or above 0.99` = mean(p < 0.01 | p > 0.99))
ggplot(prior_p, aes(p)) +
  geom_histogram(binwidth = 0.025, boundary = 0, fill = harbour[["sky"]], alpha = 0.7) +
  facet_wrap(~ prior) + labs(x = "implied prior probability p", y = "draws")
output
# A tibble: 2 x 2
  prior                  `share below 0.01 or above 0.99`
  <fct>                                             <dbl>
1 alpha ~ Normal(0, 10)                            0.644 
2 alpha ~ Normal(0, 1.5)                           0.0023

The "vague" prior puts about two-thirds of its weight on probabilities below 1% or above 99%: a very strong and silly opinion about re-arrest. The Normal(0, 1.5) prior is close to flat on the probability scale, which is what most people mean by "I have no idea". The lesson generalises: in a logistic regression, priors with standard deviations of about 1 to 1.5 on the intercept and on coefficients of standardised predictors are weakly informative, and anything much wider pushes predictions toward 0 and 1.

4.4 Fitting a logistic regression

Our first model predicts re-arrest from three things a court knows about every defendant: age, number of prior arrests and sex. Before fitting, think about the scales. Age enters in decades from 35, so the coefficient is the change in log-odds per ten years. Prior arrests are very skewed (most people have none or a few; some have thirty), and the difference between 0 and 3 priors plausibly matters more than the difference between 20 and 23, so we use log⁡(1+priors). The model is

yi∼Bernoulli(pi)
logit(pi)=α+βAagei−3510+βPlog⁡(1+priorsi)+βMmalei
α∼Normal(0,1.5),βA,βP,βM∼Normal(0,1).
R
compas <- compas |>
  mutate(age10 = (age - 35) / 10, lpriors = log1p(priors), male = as.integer(sex == "Male"))
m_lr <- brm(recid ~ 1 + age10 + lpriors + male, data = compas, family = bernoulli(),
            prior = c(prior(normal(0, 1.5), class = Intercept),
                      prior(normal(0, 1), class = b)),
            seed = 4, refresh = 0, file = "fits/ch4_logit")
fixef(m_lr) |> round(2)
exp(fixef(m_lr)[-1, -2]) |> round(2)        # odds ratios with 95% intervals
output

A detail: where brms puts the intercept prior

By default brms centres the predictors before sampling, and the prior on class = Intercept applies to the intercept of the centred model: the log-odds for a defendant with average values of every predictor. That is usually what you want, because you have more prior knowledge about an average case than about a case with every predictor at zero. The Intercept reported by fixef() is translated back to the uncentred scale: here, the log-odds for a 35-year-old woman with no prior arrests. To put a prior directly on that uncentred intercept, write the formula as recid ~ 0 + Intercept + ... and give the prior with class = b, coef = Intercept.

Every coefficient is far from zero, with narrow intervals. Read them on three scales.

  • Log-odds (the raw coefficients): ten years of age lower the log-odds of re-arrest by about 0.46; each unit of log⁡(1+priors) raises it by about 0.85; being male raises it by about 0.32. Direction and relative size are visible here, but not much else.
  • Odds ratios (eβ): ten more years of age multiply the odds of re-arrest by about 0.63; being male multiplies them by about 1.38. Odds ratios are standard in medicine and epidemiology, and Section 4.5 explains why they often mislead.
  • Probabilities: the scale people care about. To get there, compute predicted probabilities from every posterior draw and summarise them.
R
grid_p <- expand_grid(priors = 0:20, age = c(20, 35, 50)) |>
  mutate(age10 = (age - 35) / 10, lpriors = log1p(priors), male = 1)
pred <- posterior_epred(m_lr, newdata = grid_p)            # draws x rows of grid_p
grid_p <- grid_p |>
  mutate(p = colMeans(pred), lo = apply(pred, 2, quantile, 0.025),
         hi = apply(pred, 2, quantile, 0.975), age = factor(age))
ggplot(grid_p, aes(priors, p, colour = age, fill = age)) +
  geom_ribbon(aes(ymin = lo, ymax = hi), colour = NA, alpha = 0.25) +
  geom_line(linewidth = 1) +
  scale_colour_manual(values = c(`20` = harbour[["rose"]], `35` = harbour[["amber"]], `50` = harbour[["sky"]])) +
  scale_fill_manual(values = c(`20` = harbour[["rose"]], `35` = harbour[["amber"]], `50` = harbour[["sky"]])) +
  scale_y_continuous(labels = scales::percent, limits = c(0, 1)) +
  labs(x = "prior arrests", y = "Pr(re-arrest within 2 years)", colour = "age", fill = "age")

A man of 20 with no prior arrests has about a 42% chance of re-arrest within two years; a man of 50 with none, about 15%. The first few prior arrests raise the probability steeply; after ten or so, extra priors change it little, because of the logarithm and because probabilities near the top of the S-curve are hard to move. These are the statements a reader can act on, and none of them can be read off a coefficient table.

4.5 Reporting on the scale people care about

"How much does age matter?" now has many answers, one for each defendant. The standard summary is the average comparison (also called the average marginal effect): for every defendant in the data, predict the probability of re-arrest as they are and as they would be ten years older, take the difference and average over defendants. Doing this for every posterior draw gives a posterior distribution for the average difference.

R
set.seed(4)
p_now   <- posterior_epred(m_lr, ndraws = 1000)                                     # as observed
p_older <- posterior_epred(m_lr, ndraws = 1000, newdata = mutate(compas, age10 = age10 + 1))
avg_diff  <- rowMeans(p_older - p_now)                     # average change in probability
avg_ratio <- rowMeans(p_older) / rowMeans(p_now)           # ratio of average probabilities
rbind(`risk difference` = quantile(avg_diff, c(0.5, 0.025, 0.975)),
      `risk ratio`      = quantile(avg_ratio, c(0.5, 0.025, 0.975))) |> round(3)
output
                   50%   2.5%  97.5%
risk difference -0.094 -0.112 -0.075
risk ratio       0.793  0.757  0.833

Being ten years older lowers the probability of re-arrest by about 9 percentage points on average (95% interval 7.5 to 11.2), or multiplies it by about 0.79. Compare that with the odds ratio of 0.63. The odds ratio suggests a 37% reduction; the risk actually falls by about 21%. This gap is not an error. When an outcome is common (here 45% of people are re-arrested), odds and probabilities are very different things, and odds ratios exaggerate the corresponding ratios of probabilities. Only when the outcome is rare (below about 10%) are the two close.

Research Practice: Report Effects on the Outcome Scale

Reviewers and readers understand differences in probability far better than coefficients on the log-odds scale. For each effect your argument depends on:

  • report a risk difference (in percentage points) or a risk ratio, with a credible interval, and say for which population it is averaged (all defendants, defendants with no priors and so on);
  • if you report odds ratios (some journals require them), never describe them as "times as likely", and add the risk difference when the outcome is common;
  • show predicted probabilities for a few meaningful profiles, as in the figure above.

Computing these from posterior draws, as in the chunk above, is straightforward with posterior_epred(); the marginaleffects package automates it (avg_comparisons(m_lr, variables = list(age10 = 1))) and works with brms models.

Check Yourself

A study reports that a programme "doubles the odds" of finding a job (odds ratio 2.0). In the comparison group, 60% found a job. What happens to the probability?

Doubling the odds is not doubling the probability; a probability cannot exceed 1.

Compute the odds 0.6/0.4, double them, and convert back.

The odds in the comparison group are 0.6/0.4=1.5. Doubling gives 3, and odds of 3 correspond to a probability of 3/(1+3)=0.75. The risk ratio is only 0.75/0.6=1.25, although the odds ratio is 2. Common outcomes make odds ratios look much larger than risk ratios.

4.6 Can the probabilities be trusted?

A model that outputs probabilities makes a promise: among people given a probability of 30%, about 30% should experience the outcome. A model that keeps this promise is calibrated. Calibration is the first thing to check in any probability model, and it is easy to check: group people by their predicted probability and compare the predicted and observed rates in each group.

We can also ask how well the model discriminates: whether people who were re-arrested got higher probabilities than people who were not. The standard summary is the area under the ROC curve (AUC): the probability that a randomly chosen person who was re-arrested has a higher predicted probability than a randomly chosen person who was not. An AUC of 0.5 is a coin toss; 1 is perfect.

This makes possible a revealing comparison. COMPAS uses 137 items and a proprietary algorithm. Our model uses three facts available in any court record. Which predicts better? We fit a second logistic regression using only the COMPAS decile score, so that both are on the same probability scale.

R
m_dec <- update(m_lr, formula = recid ~ 1 + decile, newdata = compas,
                seed = 4, refresh = 0, file = "fits/ch4_decile")
auc <- function(p, y) {                       # probability that a random positive outranks a random negative
  r <- rank(p); n1 <- sum(y); n0 <- sum(1 - y)
  (sum(r[y == 1]) - n1 * (n1 + 1) / 2) / (n1 * n0)
}
compas <- compas |>
  mutate(p_model = colMeans(posterior_epred(m_lr)), p_compas = colMeans(posterior_epred(m_dec)))
compas |> summarise(auc_model = auc(p_model, recid), auc_compas = auc(p_compas, recid),
                    brier_model = mean((p_model - recid)^2), brier_compas = mean((p_compas - recid)^2))
output

  auc_model auc_compas brier_model brier_compas

R
calib <- compas |>
  pivot_longer(c(p_model, p_compas), names_to = "model", values_to = "p") |>
  mutate(model = recode(model, p_model = "age + priors + sex", p_compas = "COMPAS decile only")) |>
  group_by(model) |> mutate(bin = ntile(p, 10)) |>
  group_by(model, bin) |>
  summarise(pred = mean(p), k = sum(recid), n = n(), .groups = "drop") |>
  mutate(obs = k / n, lo = qbeta(0.025, k + 1, n - k + 1), hi = qbeta(0.975, k + 1, n - k + 1))
ggplot(calib, aes(pred, obs, colour = model)) +
  geom_abline(linetype = "dashed", colour = harbour[["muted"]]) +
  geom_pointrange(aes(ymin = lo, ymax = hi), position = position_dodge(width = 0.015)) +
  scale_colour_manual(values = c(harbour[["sky"]], harbour[["amber"]])) +
  coord_equal(xlim = c(0, 1), ylim = c(0, 1)) +
  labs(x = "predicted probability", y = "observed re-arrest rate", colour = NULL)

Both models are well calibrated: within each bin, the observed rate is close to the predicted probability. And the three-variable model discriminates slightly better than the COMPAS score (AUC 0.73 against 0.71), with a slightly lower Brier score (0.208 against 0.215; the Brier score is the mean squared difference between probability and outcome). An AUC of about 0.7 means that if you pick one person who was re-arrested and one who was not, the model ranks them correctly about 70% of the time. That is useful but far from certain, and both approaches are doing it with essentially the same information: how young a person is and how many times they have been arrested before.

History: Can Untrained People Beat the Algorithm?

In 2018 Julia Dressel and Hany Farid, at Dartmouth, ran a simple experiment on the same Broward County data. They showed short descriptions of defendants (sex, age, the charge and criminal history) to 400 people recruited online, with no training in criminal justice, and asked them to predict re-arrest. The crowd was about as accurate as COMPAS (67% against 65%), and a logistic regression with just two variables, age and number of prior convictions, matched COMPAS too. Their paper, in Science Advances, did not show that risk tools are useless; it showed that this one added little to information any court already has, which matters when the tool is proprietary and cannot be inspected. A good baseline is the most important comparison in any predictive paper.

Research Practice: Evaluate Predictions Properly

For any model whose purpose is prediction, report: calibration (a calibration plot, overall and in the subgroups that matter); discrimination (AUC for binary outcomes); a proper scoring rule such as the Brier score or the log score, which rewards honest probabilities; and a comparison with a simple baseline. Evaluate on data the model did not see (a held-out sample or cross-validation, Chapter 10); with four parameters and 6,000 people the in-sample numbers above are close to out-of-sample ones, but with many predictors they can be badly optimistic. The TRIPOD guidelines (Collins et al., 2015) list what a prediction-model paper must report.

Key Takeaways: Lecture 7

  • A GLM chooses a likelihood that respects the outcome and a link function that maps the linear predictor onto the outcome's range. Logistic regression uses the Bernoulli or binomial likelihood and the logit link.
  • Set priors on the log-odds scale with the probability scale in mind: Normal(0, 1.5) is near-flat on probabilities; Normal(0, 10) is not vague at all.
  • Coefficients are changes in log-odds. Use the divide-by-4 rule for a quick bound, and report average comparisons and predicted probabilities computed from posterior draws.
  • Odds ratios exaggerate risk ratios when the outcome is common.
  • Check that probabilities are calibrated, measure discrimination, and compare with a simple baseline.

Lecture 8 — Fairness, counts and exposure

4.7 What would a fair risk score be?

ProPublica's central finding is easy to state. Call a defendant "flagged" if their COMPAS decile score is 5 or more (medium or high risk). Among defendants who were not re-arrested, a much larger share of Black than white defendants had been flagged: the tool's false positive rate was higher for Black defendants. Among those who were re-arrested, a larger share of white defendants had been missed: the false negative rate was higher for white defendants. Northpointe replied (Dieterich, Mendoza and Brennan, 2016) that the score was fair in a different sense: at every score, Black and white defendants were re-arrested at about the same rate, so a score meant the same thing whoever received it. That is calibration within groups, the property of Section 4.6, checked separately for each group. Both claims can be checked in the data, with Bayesian uncertainty.

R
two <- compas |> filter(race %in% c("African-American", "Caucasian")) |>
  mutate(race = recode(race, `African-American` = "Black", Caucasian = "white"),
         flagged = decile >= 5)
counts <- two |> group_by(race) |>

Why can't a score have both properties? The reason is arithmetic, and it was proved independently in 2016–17 by Jon Kleinberg, Sendhil Mullainathan and Manish Raghavan, and by Alexandra Chouldechova (Kleinberg, Mullainathan and Raghavan, 2017; Chouldechova, 2017). If two groups have different base rates of the outcome (here 52% against 39% re-arrested), then no imperfect score can simultaneously be calibrated within both groups and have equal false positive and false negative rates in both. A calibrated score has to give higher scores, on average, to the group with the higher base rate, and with any fixed cut-off that group will then contain more flagged people who turn out not to be re-arrested. The only escapes are a perfect predictor or equal base rates. The widget lets you try to beat the theorem.

Try to make the score fair in every sense

The 5,278 Black and white defendants, each with a probability of re-arrest from our three-variable model (age, priors, sex) or their COMPAS decile score. Choose a cut-off for each group: people at or above it are flagged. Bars show each group's false positive rate (FPR), false negative rate (FNR) and positive predictive value (PPV, the share of flagged people who were re-arrested).

With a single cut-off, PPV is similar for the two groups but the error rates differ. Press "equalise false positive rates": the cut-off for Black defendants rises until the FPRs match, but now the PPVs and FNRs differ, and a higher cut-off is being applied to one group, which is itself a form of unequal treatment. There is no setting that equalises everything. Which kind of fairness should a court prefer? That is not a statistical question; it is a question about which errors are worse, and for whom. What statistics can do is make the trade-off explicit, with uncertainty.

The coefficient's meaning depends on which arrows are real. If recorded prior arrests reflect both behaviour and the intensity of policing, then "adjusting for prior arrests" partly adjusts away a pathway through policing, and a near-zero coefficient cannot be read as "no racial disparity in the system". It says only that, given the recorded history, recorded re-arrest rates are similar: exactly the calibration property again. And because the outcome is itself an arrest, the path R→P→Y affects what counts as "re-arrest" in the first place. Whether the coefficient is a direct effect (Section 3.7), a descriptive conditional comparison, or nothing interpretable at all depends on an estimand you must state and on arrows you must defend. Race is also not a treatment that can be assigned, which is why careful studies frame questions around manipulable things: a policing practice, a policy, the use of a tool.

Research Practice: Writing About Fairness and Group Differences

  • Name the fairness criterion you evaluate (calibration within groups, equal error rates, equal flag rates) and justify why it matters for the decision at hand. Report several, because the impossibility result guarantees they will disagree.
  • Report group-specific results with uncertainty: calibration curves, error rates and their differences, as posterior distributions or intervals, not just point estimates.
  • Be explicit about measurement (Section 4.2): if outcome or predictors are recorded by a process that may differ between groups, say so, and consider sensitivity analyses.
  • Do not interpret a demographic coefficient causally without an estimand and a graph. Mitchell, Potash, Barocas, D'Amour and Lum (2021) give a careful review of fairness definitions, the assumptions behind them and how to write about them.

4.8 Counts and exposure: airline safety

The second great family of GLMs is for counts: numbers of events such as accidents, arrests, emergency calls, citations or infections. Counts are whole numbers, never negative, often zero, and their spread grows with their mean. The natural likelihood is the Poisson distribution, the limit of the binomial when there are many opportunities for an event, each with a small probability, so that it inherits the binomial's maximum-entropy justification (Section 2.8). Its single parameter λ is both its mean and its variance. Because λ must be positive, the link is the logarithm: log⁡λi=ηi.

History: Horse Kicks and the Law of Small Numbers

In 1898 the Russian-German statistician Ladislaus von Bortkiewicz published a small book called The Law of Small Numbers. Its most famous table counted the soldiers killed by horse kicks in 14 corps of the Prussian cavalry over 20 years: 280 corps-years, with 0 deaths in 144 of them, 1 in 91, 2 in 32, 3 in 11 and 4 in 2. These counts match a Poisson distribution with mean 0.7 almost exactly. Bortkiewicz's point was general: rare events, from many independent opportunities, follow the Poisson law, whatever they are. The distribution itself had been derived by Siméon Denis Poisson in 1837, in a book about the probability of wrong verdicts in criminal trials.

Our data are from a 2014 FiveThirtyEight analysis by Nate Silver: for 56 major airlines, the number of safety incidents (events in which an aircraft is damaged or someone is injured) in 1985–1999 and in 2000–2014, together with each airline's capacity in available seat-kilometres per week. The question is one every traveller has asked: is an airline's safety record a lasting trait? If an airline had many incidents in the past, should you expect more from it in the future?

The model must compare rates, incidents per unit of flying, and the GLM does this with an offset. If airline i has exposure Ei (billions of seat-kilometres) and an incident rate ri per billion, then its expected count is λi=Eiri, so

log⁡λi=log⁡Ei+log⁡ri=log⁡Ei+α+βxi.

The term log⁡Ei is part of the linear predictor with its coefficient fixed at 1: an offset. Everything else models the rate. Definition: Offset and Exposure

When counts are collected over different amounts of time, space, population or activity (the exposure Ei), a Poisson model for the rate per unit of exposure is yi∼Poisson(Eiri) with log⁡ri=α+∑kβkxki. The term log⁡Ei, added to the linear predictor with coefficient 1, is called an offset. Coefficients are then changes in the log rate, and eβ is a rate ratio. In brms, write y ~ x + offset(log(E)). Typical exposures: person-years in epidemiology, population in crime and emergency-service studies, miles driven in traffic safety.

4.9 Overdispersion and the negative binomial

The Poisson distribution has a rigid property: its variance equals its mean. Real counts are almost always more variable than that, because the units differ in ways the model does not capture (airlines differ in fleet age, regulators, training, weather and routes, beyond what their past record reveals). Extra variation of this kind is called overdispersion, and ignoring it makes a model too confident. A posterior predictive check shows whether we have it.

R
m_nb <- brm(incidents_00_14 ~ 1 + past_z + offset(log(exposure)), data = air,
            family = negbinomial(),
            prior = c(prior(normal(-5, 2), class = Intercept), prior(normal(0, 1), class = b),
                      prior(exponential(0.2), class = shape)),
            seed = 4, refresh = 0, file = "fits/ch4_nb")
set.seed(8)
y <- air$incidents_00_14
chisq <- function(fit, model) {             # Pearson discrepancy, for observed and replicated data
  mu   <- posterior_epred(fit, ndraws = 1000)
  yrep <- posterior_predict(fit, ndraws = 1000)
  tibble(model, observed = rowSums(sweep(mu, 2, y)^2 / mu), replicated = rowSums((yrep - mu)^2 / mu))
}
ppc <- bind_rows(chisq(m_pois, "Poisson"), chisq(m_nb, "negative binomial"))
ppc |> group_by(model) |> summarise(`Pr(replicated >= observed)` = mean(replicated >= observed))

Which statistic should the check use? Simple summaries such as the spread of the raw counts or the number of airlines with zero incidents turn out to be reproduced well by the Poisson model, because differences in exposure and past record already create a lot of variation between airlines. The statistic that targets overdispersion directly compares each airline's count with its own expected value: the χ2 discrepancy ∑i(yi−μi)2/μi, computed under each posterior draw for the real data and for data simulated from that draw (Gelman, Meng and Stern, 1996). Under the Poisson model the real counts are far more dispersed than the model's own simulations in every one of the 1,000 draws. The negative binomial (also called gamma-Poisson) model fixes this by letting each airline's true rate vary around the regression line, following a gamma distribution, before the counts are drawn. It has one extra parameter, a shape ϕ: the variance of the counts is μ+μ2/ϕ, so small ϕ means a lot of extra variation and very large ϕ gives back the Poisson.

Poisson and negative binomial with the same mean

Bars: the probability of each count under a Poisson distribution (blue) and a negative binomial (amber), both with the mean you choose. The shape ϕ controls the negative binomial's extra spread.

The substantive conclusion survives, but the uncertainty changes. Under the negative binomial, the coefficient on past record is about the same (0.52 against 0.47), but its 95% interval is about 1.5 times as wide. The Poisson model was not wrong about the direction; it was overconfident about the precision, because it treated every airline's count as if its rate were known exactly given the past record. In a paper, the Poisson intervals would have overstated the evidence, and with a weaker effect they could have turned noise into a "finding".

Research Practice: Counts Need Exposure and Dispersion

  • Always model rates with an offset when units differ in exposure, and report rate ratios with intervals. Comparing raw counts between neighbourhoods of different populations, or hospitals of different sizes, is one of the commonest errors in applied work.
  • Check for overdispersion with a posterior predictive check, and default to the negative binomial for real-world counts. Report the dispersion parameter.
  • Beware of rankings based on extreme counts. The units with the highest observed rates are usually the ones with the most luck as well as the highest true rates, especially when exposure is small. Rank by posterior estimates, with intervals, not by raw rates.

Key Takeaways: Lecture 8

  • Calibration within groups and equal error rates are different fairness criteria, and with different base rates no imperfect score can satisfy both. Report several criteria, with uncertainty.
  • A demographic coefficient has no causal meaning without an estimand and a graph, and outcomes and predictors recorded by the same process can hide the bias being studied.
  • Counts use the Poisson likelihood and the log link; differing exposure enters as an offset, and coefficients become rate ratios.
  • Real counts are usually overdispersed: check with posterior predictive checks and use the negative binomial, or your intervals will be too narrow.
  • Extreme past counts partly reflect luck, so they regress toward the mean; rank units by posterior estimates, not raw rates.

For white defendants, a cut-off of 4 gives a false positive rate of 33%, a false negative rate of 38% and a PPV of 55%. Equalising false positive rates (here the false negative rates also happen to match) requires holding Black defendants to a higher cut-off, and leaves a flag that means re-arrest 68% of the time for one group and 55% for the other: the trade-off of Section 4.7 in numbers.

  5. Calibration by group for our model. Repeat the calibration plot of Section 4.6 for the three-variable model separately for Black and white defendants (five bins each). Is the model calibrated within both groups?

    Filter compas to the two groups, then group_by(race) before ntile().

    Within each group, the observed re-arrest rates in the five bins are close to the predicted probabilities: for Black defendants 25%, 39%, 51%, 66% and 80% observed against 25%, 39%, 52%, 64% and 77% predicted; for white defendants 17%, 27%, 38%, 46% and 67% against 14%, 27%, 37%, 50% and 68%. The model is roughly calibrated within both groups, even though race is not a predictor, because the predictors it uses (age and recorded priors) account for the difference in recorded re-arrest rates. As Section 4.7 explains, calibration within groups then implies different error rates at any common cut-off.

  6. Do incidents predict fatal accidents?. Fit the negative binomial model with fatal_accidents_00_14 as the outcome and the same predictor (past incident rate) and offset. Report the rate ratio per standard deviation, with its interval. Does a record of non-fatal incidents predict future fatal accidents?

    Use update(m_nb, formula = fatal_accidents_00_14 ~ 1 + past_z + offset(log(exposure)), newdata = air).

    The rate ratio for fatal accidents in 2000–14 per standard deviation of the past incident rate is about 2.35 (95% interval 1.63 to 3.43). Airlines with more non-fatal incidents in 1985–99 had substantially higher rates of fatal accidents fifteen years later. With only 37 fatal accidents in 2000–14, at 24 of the 56 airlines, the interval is wide, but it is well above 1. This matches the conclusion of Silver's article: incidents are a more informative record of safety culture than the rare fatal accidents themselves.

  7. Interval widths. Compute the ratio of the widths of the 95% intervals for the past-record coefficient under the negative binomial and Poisson models. Then refit the negative binomial with a Normal(0, 0.5) prior on the coefficient. How much does the prior matter here, with 56 airlines?

    Width is Q97.5 - Q2.5 in the output of fixef().

    The negative binomial interval for the coefficient is 1.53 times as wide as the Poisson interval (0.32 to 0.75, against 0.34 to 0.62). With a Normal(0, 0.5) prior the estimate becomes 0.51 (0.30 to 0.72), against 0.52 (0.32 to 0.75) with Normal(0, 1). The prior barely matters: the coefficient is estimated from 56 airlines with a clear signal, and both priors allow the plausible range of effects. Stating that you checked is still worth a sentence in a paper.

  8. A short report. Write the results paragraph (four or five sentences) for the airline analysis, as you would in a paper: the estimand, the model and priors, the main estimate as a rate ratio with interval, the check that motivated the negative binomial, and one limitation.

    Follow the model results paragraph at the end of Chapter 3.

    A model answer: "We estimated how strongly an airline's incident rate in 1985–1999 predicts its incident rate in 2000–2014, for 56 major airlines, using incidents per billion available seat-kilometres. Counts in 2000–2014 were modelled with a negative binomial likelihood and a log link, with log exposure as an offset and the standardised log past rate as the predictor; priors were Normal(−5, 2) for the intercept, Normal(0, 1) for the coefficient and Exponential(0.2) for the shape. A Poisson model was rejected by a posterior predictive check of overdispersion (posterior predictive probability of the χ² discrepancy below 0.001), whereas the negative binomial was consistent with the data (0.59). An airline with a past rate one standard deviation above average had an expected future rate 1.69 times higher (95% credible interval 1.37 to 2.11), and extreme past records regressed substantially toward the mean. Exposure was available only for 2014 and was assumed constant across both periods."
