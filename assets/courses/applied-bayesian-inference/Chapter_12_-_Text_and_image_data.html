Chapter 12
Text and image data

Lectures 24–25 · naive Bayes and shrinkage for words, topic models, calibration, detecting AI-written text, and Bayesian models of pixels

Text and images are the data of modern machine learning, and they look nothing like the tables of the previous chapters. A document is a sequence of words of any length; an image is a grid of thousands of pixel intensities. Yet the Bayesian toolkit carries over with surprisingly little change. Turn a document into counts of words, and classifying it is a regression with many predictors (Chapter 10); discovering its themes is a mixture model (Chapter 11). Turn an image into a vector of pixels, and the same logistic regression applies. What changes is scale, and with it the importance of two old ideas: shrinkage, because most words and most pixels carry little information, and calibration, because a classifier that is confidently wrong does real harm.

  • Lecture 24 is about classifying text. We ask whether an answer to a finance question was written by a person or by ChatGPT, using the Human ChatGPT Comparison Corpus. We build naive Bayes as a genuinely Bayesian model, fit a horseshoe logistic regression on word counts, and look hard at what the classifiers actually learn.
  • Lecture 25 is about topics, calibration and images. We fit a topic model with a sampler we write ourselves, check whether the detectors' probabilities can be trusted, test them on a different kind of question, and then classify clothing from pixels with a model whose uncertainty you can probe by drawing on a canvas.

Think About It: Before You Start

A detector of AI-written text reports 95% accuracy on a test set. A university plans to use it on student essays, flagging any essay it rates above 90% likely to be AI-written. Before agreeing, what would you want to know about the test set, about the essays the detector will see, and about the students who write them? Write down three questions. Section 10.2's warning, that a good prediction model can be a poor guide to anything else, is about to become very concrete.

Lecture 24 — Classifying text

12.1 Who wrote this answer?

In the weeks after ChatGPT's release in late 2022, Biyang Guo and colleagues collected questions from existing public question-answering data sets, each with one or more answers written by people, and asked ChatGPT to answer the same questions (Guo et al., 2023). The result, the Human ChatGPT Comparison Corpus (HC3), pairs human and machine answers to the same questions in several domains, and it became one of the first benchmarks for detecting AI-generated text. We use the finance subset: 3,933 questions about personal finance and investing, each with a human answer and a ChatGPT answer. A second subset, open-domain questions, will serve in Section 12.7 as a test of what happens when the kind of question changes.

The question is the one behind the AI-text detection project: given an answer, how probable is it that a machine wrote it? It is a prediction question, and we will judge every method on answers it did not see in training.

All 3,933 finance questions have a usable answer of each kind, so we have 7,866 answers, half of them ChatGPT's. Two differences between the classes are visible before any modelling. ChatGPT's answers are longer: a median of 206 words against 129. And 90.2% of ChatGPT's answers contain a full stop followed directly by a capital letter with no space, as in "dealership.To determine" in the second answer above, against 0.5% of the human answers. The human answers have quirks of their own: the first one above is stitched together from fragments that begin with dashes, which is how it was stored in the source data set the authors used.

We split by question, not by answer: 1,000 questions, with both their answers, form the training set, and the other questions the test set. Splitting answers at random would put the human answer to a question in the training set and the ChatGPT answer to the same question in the test set, and a classifier could then score points by recognising the topic rather than the author. Any grouping in the data (patients, schools, questions) must be respected by the split.

To give a classifier something to work with, we reduce each answer to a bag of words: lower-case it, split it at anything that is not a letter or apostrophe, and count how often each word in a fixed vocabulary occurs. Word order is thrown away. The vocabulary is the 2,000 words that occur in the most training answers, chosen without looking at the labels (Chapter 10's lesson about outcome-based filtering).

12.2 What a detector can learn

The causal sketch for a detection problem is short, and it is the most important part of the analysis. The author (person or ChatGPT) shapes the answer in many ways: word choice, tone, length, structure. The question shapes it too, through its topic. And the pipeline that collected, cleaned and stored the answers can leave its own marks, which differ between the two sources because the two kinds of answer were collected differently.

A classifier does not know which path a feature came through. Style is what we hope it learns. Length is a real difference between the authors in this data set (ChatGPT's answers are longer), but it would vanish if someone asked ChatGPT to be brief. The glued full stops are pure pipeline: in HC3, the line breaks in ChatGPT's answers were removed when the answers were stored, so sentences that began on a new line now follow the previous full stop with no space. A detector that learns this has learned how a data set was scraped, not how a model writes. A bag of words splits text at punctuation, so our classifiers cannot see the seams directly, but Exercise 1 shows how far a one-feature "detector" gets with them.

There is a second, more uncomfortable lesson. Liang et al. (2023) showed that several commercial GPT detectors classified more than half of a set of essays written by non-native English speakers as AI-generated, while classifying essays by native speakers almost perfectly. Simpler vocabulary and more formulaic phrasing, common in writing in a second language, look like machine text to a model trained on fluent native writing. Whatever a detector learns, it learns relative to the people in its training data.

Check Yourself

A detector trained and tested on HC3 reaches 99% accuracy. Which conclusion is justified?

The test set came from the same collection process as the training set.

High accuracy does not identify which features were used.

Test-set accuracy is a statement about a population like the test set: the same questions, the same version of ChatGPT, the same scraping, the same human writers. Every one of those can change in deployment. The causal sketch says which differences between the two classes are fragile (length, formatting) and which might generalise (style), and only new data can say which ones the detector relies on.

It separates HC3's ChatGPT answers from HC3's human answers very well, by some combination of style, length, topic and collection artefacts; how it does on other text, other models or other writers has to be tested separately.

12.3 Naive Bayes is Bayesian

The oldest statistical text classifier is a direct application of Bayes' rule. Suppose each class c (human or ChatGPT) has its own probability distribution over the vocabulary, θc=(θc1,…,θcV), and that the words of a document are drawn independently from the distribution of its class. Then a document with word counts x=(x1,…,xV) has likelihood ∏vθcvxv under class c, and Bayes' rule gives the posterior log-odds that it is ChatGPT's:

log⁡Pr(ChatGPT∣x)Pr(human∣x)=log⁡π1−π+∑v=1Vxvlog⁡θ1vθ0v,

where π is the prior probability of ChatGPT (one half here, by design). Each word contributes a weight, its log probability ratio, once for each time it appears. The independence assumption is plainly false (the word "credit" makes "card" more likely), which is why the method is called naive Bayes. It nonetheless classifies well, for reasons explained by Domingos and Pazzani (1997): classification needs only the sign of the log-odds to be right, not its size.

The θ's must be estimated. The naive estimate, each word's share of the tokens in its class, fails badly for rare words: a word seen twice in ChatGPT's training answers and never in the human ones gets θ^0v=0 and a weight of +∞, and any document containing it is declared ChatGPT's with certainty. The Bayesian fix is a prior. With a symmetric Dirichlet prior, θc∼Dirichlet(α,…,α), which is conjugate to the multinomial, the posterior is Dirichlet too, with posterior mean

𝔼[θcv∣data]=ncv+αnc+Vα,

where ncv is the count of word v in class c and nc the total number of tokens in class c. This is additive smoothing, invented as a fix ("add one to every count") and revealed by Bayes as a posterior mean; α=1 is Laplace's rule of succession, from the eighteenth century. Rare words are pulled toward equal probability in the two classes, so their weights shrink toward zero, while common words, whose counts swamp α, barely move. The widget shows it for words from our training set.

Smoothing word weights

R
metrics <- function(p, y) {                   # accuracy, log loss, and share of near-certain predictions
  p <- pmin(pmax(p, 1e-12), 1 - 1e-12)        # floating point: probabilities can round to exactly 0 or 1
  c(accuracy = mean((p > 0.5) == y), log_loss = -mean(y * log(p) + (1 - y) * log(1 - p)),
    near_certain = mean(p < 0.001 | p > 0.999))
}
nb_weights <- function(alpha, X = X_train, y = train$ai) {
  n1 <- colSums(X[y == 1, ]); n0 <- colSums(X[y == 0, ])
  log((n1 + alpha) / (sum(n1) + alpha * ncol(X))) - log((n0 + alpha) / (sum(n0) + alpha * ncol(X)))
}
nb_logit <- function(alpha, X = X_test) as.vector(X %*% nb_weights(alpha))   # prior odds 1:1

A word occurs 3 times in 10,000 tokens of ChatGPT training text, and the vocabulary has 2,000 words. With a Dirichlet(1) prior, what is the posterior mean probability of the word in ChatGPT text, expressed per 10,000 tokens?

(ncv+α)/(nc+Vα) with ncv=3, nc=10,000, V=2,000, α=1.

With V=2,000, the prior adds 2,000 pseudo-tokens spread evenly over the vocabulary, a noticeable amount when the class has only 10,000 tokens. The strength of a "flat" Dirichlet prior grows with the size of the vocabulary, which is worth remembering when V is 50,000.

12.4 Shrinkage on words

Naive Bayes models how documents are generated from classes. The alternative is to model the class given the document directly: a logistic regression of the label on the word counts. This drops the independence assumption, since correlated words now share their weight instead of each adding a full vote, but with a full vocabulary it has thousands of coefficients and only a few thousand documents, which is Chapter 10's problem exactly. We use the regularised horseshoe on the 300 most widespread words (again chosen without labels), each entered as log⁡(1+count) and standardised, with a prior guess of 30 relevant words. On one core the fit takes about nine minutes.

R
v300 <- vocab[1:300]

History: The Federalist and the Spam Filter

The first large Bayesian analysis of text settled a historical argument. Twelve of the Federalist papers, published anonymously in 1787–88 to persuade New York to ratify the US Constitution, were claimed by both Alexander Hamilton and James Madison. Frederick Mosteller and David Wallace (1963, 1964) counted how often each man used common function words in writings of known authorship ("upon" was a famous marker: Hamilton used it often, Madison almost never), modelled the counts with Poisson and negative binomial distributions under carefully chosen priors, and computed posterior odds for each disputed paper. All twelve came out strongly for Madison, the conclusion most historians now accept. Thirty-five years later, Mehran Sahami and colleagues at Stanford and Microsoft Research (Sahami et al., 1998) applied naive Bayes to junk e-mail, and Paul Graham's essay "A Plan for Spam" (2002) showed programmers how well it worked, setting off a wave of Bayesian spam filters. Mosteller and Wallace's function words and Graham's spam tokens are the ancestors of every feature in the figure above.

Key Takeaways: Lecture 24

  • Split text data by the unit that generates it (here, the question), so that the test set measures what deployment will face.
  • Draw the causal sketch before trusting accuracy: detectors learn style, length, topic and collection artefacts alike, and only the first is the target.
  • Naive Bayes is Bayes' rule with a multinomial model per class; additive smoothing is the posterior mean under a Dirichlet prior, and it shrinks the weights of rare words.
  • A horseshoe logistic regression on word counts drops the independence assumption: it is as accurate as naive Bayes, with a log loss less than half as large, and honest uncertainty about each word.

Lecture 25 — Topics, calibration and images

12.5 Topic models

Classification needs labels. Often we have only documents and want to know what they are about. Latent Dirichlet allocation (LDA; Blei, Ng and Jordan, 2003) answers with a generative story that is a mixture model with one twist. There are K topics, each a probability distribution ϕk over the vocabulary. Each document d has its own topic proportions θd. Each word of the document is generated by first choosing a topic z∼Categorical(θd) and then a word w∼Categorical(ϕz), with Dirichlet priors on both:

θd∼Dirichlet(α),ϕk∼Dirichlet(β), zdi∼Categorical(θd),wdi∼Categorical(ϕzdi).

The twist is that the group label belongs to each word, not to each document. The penguins of Chapter 11 each belonged to one species; a document is a mixed-membership object, partly about mortgages and partly about taxes. Population geneticists had found the same model three years earlier: Pritchard, Stephens and Donnelly (2000) described each person's genome as a mixture of ancestral populations, with each stretch of DNA drawn from one of them. Documents are people, words are genetic markers and topics are populations.

The posterior is usually computed by collapsed Gibbs sampling (Griffiths and Steyvers, 2004). The Dirichlet priors are conjugate, so θ and ϕ can be integrated out, leaving only the topic assignments z. Each word's assignment is then resampled in turn from

Pr(zdi=k∣z−di,w)∝nk,wdi+βnk+Vβ(nd,k+α),

where the counts exclude the current word: nk,w is how often word w is assigned to topic k across the corpus, nk the total assigned to topic k, and nd,k the number of words in document d assigned to topic k. The first factor says "this word is common in topic k"; the second says "this document already uses topic k". The widget runs this sampler on a toy corpus.

LDA on a toy corpus

Twelve short documents mixing three themes (money, sport and weather). Each sweep of the collapsed Gibbs sampler reassigns every word's topic. Left: each topic's five most probable words. Right: each document's topic proportions. The sampler does not know the themes; it starts from random assignments.

After a few sweeps the topics usually separate into the three themes, and documents that mix themes get mixed proportions. Small α pushes each document toward one dominant topic; large α spreads documents across topics. Restart a few times and the topics come out in a different order: the label switching of Chapter 11 again, harmless as long as we never average topic numbers across runs.

For the HC3 answers we write the same sampler in C++ through Rcpp, about twenty lines, and run it on all the finance answers (topic models use no labels, so the test answers can be included). We drop words of two letters or fewer, words in fewer than ten answers, and the very common words that appear in more than 15% of answers, and fit eight topics with α=0.5 and β=0.05 for 300 sweeps. It takes a few seconds.

R
cppFunction('
List lda_gibbs(IntegerVector w, IntegerVector d, int K, int V, int D, double alpha, double beta, int iters) {
  // collapsed Gibbs sampler for LDA; w and d are 0-based word and document ids, one entry per token
  int N = w.size(); IntegerVector z(N); IntegerMatrix nwk(V, K), ndk(D, K); IntegerVector nk(K);
  for (int i = 0; i < N; i++) { int k = (int)(R::runif(0.0, 1.0) * K); z[i] = k; nwk(w[i], k)++; ndk(d[i], k)++; nk[k]++; }
  std::vector<double> p(K);
  for (int it = 0; it < iters; it++) for (int i = 0; i < N; i++) {
    int k = z[i]; nwk(w[i], k)--; ndk(d[i], k)--; nk[k]--;          // remove the current word
    double tot = 0;
    for (int j = 0; j < K; j++) { p[j] = (nwk(w[i], j) + beta) / (nk[j] + V * beta) * (ndk(d[i], j) + alpha); tot += p[j]; }
    double u = R::runif(0.0, 1.0) * tot; int kn = 0; double c = p[0];
    while (u > c && kn < K - 1) { kn++; c += p[kn]; }
    z[i] = kn; nwk(w[i], kn)++; ndk(d[i], kn)++; nk[kn]++;          // add it back with its new topic
  }
  return List::create(_["nwk"] = nwk, _["ndk"] = ndk);
}')
tok_all <- map(tokenize(fin$text), ~ .x[nchar(.x) > 2])
df_all <- table(unlist(map(tok_all, unique)))
lda_vocab <- names(df_all)[df_all >= 10 & df_all <= 0.15 * nrow(fin)]
ids <- map(tok_all, ~ { m <- match(.x, lda_vocab); m[!is.na(m)] })
set.seed(12)
lda <- lda_gibbs(unlist(ids) - 1L, rep(seq_along(ids), lengths(ids)) - 1L, K = 8L,
                 V = length(lda_vocab), D = length(ids), alpha = 0.5, beta = 0.05, iters = 300L)
phi <- sweep(lda$nwk + 0.05, 2, colSums(lda$nwk) + 0.05 * length(lda_vocab), "/")
theta <- (lda$ndk + 0.5) / rowSums(lda$ndk + 0.5)
c(vocabulary = length(lda_vocab), tokens = length(unlist(ids)))
tibble(topic = 1:8, top_words = map_chr(1:8, ~ paste(lda_vocab[order(-phi[, .x])][1:8], collapse = " ")))

Check Yourself

What is the essential difference between the Gaussian mixture of Chapter 11 and LDA?

Both use latent group labels and Dirichlet priors.

Both suffer from label switching.

LDA is a mixed-membership model: the mixture happens inside each document, with document-specific weights θd. That is why it suits documents (and genomes, and tumours made of several cell types) better than a model that forces each unit into one group. Its topics are still exchangeable, so label switching and multimodality carry over unchanged.

In a mixture, each observation (a penguin) belongs to one group; in LDA, each document has its own proportions over all topics, and the topic label belongs to each word. A document can be 60% about mortgages and 40% about taxes.

12.6 Can we trust the probabilities?

A classifier's probabilities are calibrated if, among all the documents it rates 80% likely to be ChatGPT's, about 80% are. Accuracy cannot reveal miscalibration, because it only uses which side of 50% a probability falls on. The reliability diagram does: group the test predictions into bins of predicted probability and plot the observed share of ChatGPT answers in each bin against the bin's average prediction. A calibrated classifier lies on the diagonal.

The usual repair for an overconfident classifier is temperature scaling (Guo et al., 2017): divide every log-odds by a constant T>1 chosen to minimise the log loss on a held-out calibration set, which leaves the ranking (and the accuracy) unchanged and softens every probability. For naive Bayes, the right temperature has a meaning: if words were in effect redundant copies of each other, T is roughly how many times each piece of evidence was counted. Check Yourself

A detector flags 95% of AI-written essays and wrongly flags 5% of human-written ones. If 10% of submitted essays are AI-written, what fraction of flagged essays are actually AI-written?

Bayes' rule: Pr(AI∣flag)=0.95×0.100.95×0.10+0.05×0.90.

0.095/(0.095+0.045)=0.68. Nearly one flagged essay in three is a false accusation, even with a detector that is 95% accurate on both classes. With 2% AI-written essays the share falls to about 28%. The base rate is the first thing to ask about before acting on any detector, and, as Section 12.7 shows, the false-positive rate itself may not survive a change of text.

Research Practice: Reporting a Detector

  • Describe the data generation for both classes: sources, generating models and versions, prompts, and every cleaning step. Check for artefacts that differ between classes (formatting, length, encoding) and report how the detector does when they are removed.
  • Split by the unit that generates the text (question, author, assignment) and say so.
  • Report performance on out-of-distribution text: other domains, other generating models, other human writers. In-distribution accuracy is an upper bound.
  • Report calibration (a reliability diagram and the log loss), and the false-positive rate on human text at the decision threshold, broken down by relevant groups of writers (for example, native and non-native speakers; Liang et al., 2023).
  • Translate error rates into consequences with explicit base rates: what share of flagged texts would be human-written in the intended setting?

12.8 From words to pixels

An image is another bag of numbers. Fashion-MNIST (Xiao, Rasul and Vollgraf, 2017) is a drop-in replacement for the handwritten digits of MNIST (LeCun et al., 1998): 70,000 greyscale photographs of clothing from the online retailer Zalando, 28 by 28 pixels, in ten classes. Digits have become too easy; clothing is harder, and two of its classes, T-shirts and shirts, are genuinely confusable even for people. We take 1,000 training images and 2,000 test images of those two classes, average each 2 × 2 block of pixels to get 14 × 14 = 196 predictors, and fit a Bayesian logistic regression with a normal prior whose scale is learned from the data (the learned-scale ridge of Chapter 10).

Project Connections: Detection, Topics and Mixed Membership

This chapter is the core of the AI-generated text detection project. Three lessons transfer directly. First, audit the data for artefacts that separate the classes for the wrong reasons: HC3's glued full stops and length difference are the template, and the project should report a detector's performance after normalising formatting and matching on length. Second, evaluate across domains and generators: the finance-to-open-domain comparison of Section 12.7 is what a reviewer will ask about, and newer models than the ChatGPT of late 2022 must be included. Third, report calibrated probabilities and base-rate consequences, not accuracy alone; a horseshoe logistic model gives both a calibrated score and a short list of telltale words with uncertainty. For the AI tools in university mathematics project, the same detector logic applies to student work, and the base-rate check of Section 12.7 should come before any detector is used on real students. For the cancer gene-expression project, LDA's mixed membership is the right idea for tumours made of several cell populations, and the population-genetics model of Pritchard et al. (2000) is a direct template (genes replace words, cell types replace topics). For clustering hostile web traffic, request logs are documents: a topic model on tokenised request paths gives each client a mixture of behaviours rather than a single label.

Key Takeaways: Lecture 25

  • LDA is a mixed-membership mixture: each document has its own topic proportions, and the collapsed Gibbs sampler integrates out the Dirichlet-distributed parameters analytically.
  • Accuracy hides calibration. Naive Bayes, which counts correlated evidence as independent, is badly overconfident; temperature scaling repairs the probabilities without changing the ranking.
  • In-domain accuracy is an upper bound: test a detector on a different kind of text before trusting it, and look at its errors on human writing in particular.
  • Pixels are predictors too: a Bayesian logistic regression on pixels predicts tolerably, but its posterior shows that no single pixel coefficient is well determined. Good prediction does not imply interpretable parameters.

Exercises

  1. A one-feature detector. Classify an answer as ChatGPT's if and only if it contains a "seam", a lower-case letter, a full stop and a capital letter with no space between them. What are its accuracy and log loss on the finance test set (use probabilities 0.99 and 0.01) and on the open-domain answers?

    str_detect(text, "[a-z]\\.[A-Z]").

    On the finance test set the one-feature "detector" is 94.8% accurate, with a log loss of 0.247, better than the 0.287 of naive Bayes on 2,000 words, because 90.2% of ChatGPT's finance answers have a seam and only 0.5% of the human answers do. On the open-domain answers it is 46.5% accurate, worse than a coin, with a log loss of 2.47: there the seam appears in only 0.2% of ChatGPT's answers and in 7.1% of the human ones. A feature produced by how one subset was scraped can look like the best predictor in the data and carry no information, or the wrong information, anywhere else. Any detector built on HC3 should be evaluated after removing all whitespace and punctuation differences.

  2. Length as a confounder. Split the finance test answers into thirds by length (number of words). Within each third, what share is ChatGPT's, and how accurate are naive Bayes and the horseshoe model?

    cut(len, quantile(len, 0:3 / 3), include.lowest = TRUE).

    The thirds have very different mixes of authors: among the shortest answers (up to 140 words) only 19.5% are ChatGPT's, against 71.9% in the middle third (141 to 220 words) and 58.9% in the longest. Within each third the classifiers remain accurate: naive Bayes 91.5%, 97.4% and 98.2%, and the horseshoe 92.7%, 96.2% and 97.7%. So length is a confounder of the overall comparison but not the whole story: the word-based classifiers separate the authors among answers of similar length too. Accuracy is lowest for short answers, where there are fewer words to go on.

  3. Presence or count. McCallum and Nigam (1998) compared two event models for naive Bayes: the multinomial (word counts) and the Bernoulli (word present or absent). Fit the Bernoulli version with Beta(1, 1) priors on each word's presence probability and compare it with the multinomial on the test set.

    The log-odds is ∑v[bvlog⁡(p1v/p0v)+(1−bv)log⁡((1−p1v)/(1−p0v))], where bv indicates presence and pcv=(docs with v+1)/(docs+2).

    The Bernoulli model is slightly more accurate (96.5% against 95.7%) but even more overconfident: 95.3% of its predictions are beyond 0.001 or 0.999, and its log loss is worse (0.369 against 0.287). Every vocabulary word now contributes evidence whether or not it appears: the words present through their presence, and the far more numerous absent words through their absence; with 2,000 words, the absences add up. McCallum and Nigam (1998) found that the multinomial model is usually better with large vocabularies, as here for the log loss.

  4. Topics as predictors. Use the logarithms of the eight topic proportions of Section 12.5 as the only predictors in a logistic regression of authorship, trained on the training questions and tested on the others. How much of the authorship signal do the topics carry?

    glm(ai ~ ., binomial, data = ...) on log(theta).

    With only the eight topic proportions, a logistic regression classifies 92.2% of the test answers correctly, with a log loss of 0.218: less accurate than naive Bayes on 2,000 words, but with a lower log loss. The figure in Section 12.5 shows where it most likely comes from: the conversational topic, which the topic model found without labels, is nine times as prominent in human answers. This is a useful warning for the detection project: "unsupervised" features can carry the label very strongly when the classes differ in style.

  5. Honest temperature scaling. Choose the naive Bayes temperature on a random half of the test set (minimising log loss) and evaluate it on the other half. What is T, and how much does the log loss improve?

    optimize() over T∈(1,500).

    On one half of the test set, the best temperature is T=1.8. On the other half it lowers the log loss from 0.343 to 0.214, without changing the accuracy (95.3%). Even after scaling, 84.5% of the predictions remain beyond 0.001 or 0.999, so a single temperature only partly repairs naive Bayes. The reliability diagram of Section 12.6 shows why: about two-thirds of naive Bayes' errors are made in its two most extreme bins, where it is nearly certain, and no common divisor can soften those without also softening the far more numerous confident correct predictions. Fitting T on the same answers used to report the log loss would overstate the improvement.

  6. The other direction. Train multinomial naive Bayes on all the open-domain answers and test it on the finance test set. Compare with Section 12.7.

    Reuse count_words() with the finance vocabulary, so the two directions use the same features.

    Trained on open-domain answers and tested on finance, naive Bayes is 56.7% accurate, with a log loss of 6.98, and 80.3% of its predictions are beyond 0.001 or 0.999. The failure is symmetric with Section 12.7 and, if anything, worse: confident and nearly useless. The two subsets differ in topic, in the length and register of the human answers, and in their formatting artefacts, and a model trained on one learns the other's quirks as if they were authorship.

  7. An easy pair. Refit the image model of Section 12.8 to sneakers (class 7) versus ankle boots (class 9). Compare accuracy, the share of uncertain predictions and the learned prior scale sb with the shirt model.

    update(m_img, newdata = ...) avoids recompiling.

    Sneakers and ankle boots are far easier: 95.5% accuracy, a log loss of 0.124, and only 11.4% of predictions between 0.2 and 0.8 (against 82.6%, 0.380 and 35.6% for shirts). The learned prior scale is about twice as large, sb=2.02 (95% interval 1.44 to 2.71) against 0.96: when the classes differ strongly in shape (a boot has a tall shaft), the data support larger coefficients, and the hierarchical prior lets them grow. The prior scale is learned, not fixed, and it adapts to how separable the classes are.

  8. What a flag means. For the horseshoe model at a threshold of 0.9, compute the sensitivity (share of ChatGPT answers flagged) and false-positive rate (share of human answers flagged) on the finance test set and on the open-domain answers.     PPV=sπ/(sπ+f(1−π)).

    On finance, at a threshold of 0.9, the horseshoe model flags 85.0% of ChatGPT's answers and 0.55% of the human ones. If 10% of submissions were ChatGPT's, 94.5% of flagged texts would be; if 2%, 76.1%, so about one flag in four would be a false accusation. On open-domain answers it flags only 29.7% of ChatGPT's answers and 1.1% of the human ones: with 10% ChatGPT, 75.1% of flags would be correct, and with 2%, only 35.6%, so nearly two flags in three would accuse a human. A detector's false-positive rate, and therefore what a flag means, depends on the kind of text and on the base rate, and both must be known before acting on it.
