Chapter 14
Bayesian deep learning and causal AI

Lectures 30–33 · Bayesian neural networks as causal models, transformers as Bayesian machines, learning causes instead of shortcuts, and teaching AI to model causation

Chapter 13 ended with three requirements for any learner that is to model causation rather than association: causal assumptions, written down; variation that comes from interventions or from several environments; and honest uncertainty that widens where the data cannot speak. This final chapter takes the three into deep learning, the technology behind modern AI, and asks how far each can be met.

The answer is more encouraging than one might fear and less magical than one might hope. Neural networks can be fitted the Bayesian way, and when they are used as the structural equations of a causal model their flexibility is a real asset. Transformers, the architecture of large language models, turn out to be machines that can learn Bayesian inference, and even causal inference, from simulated worlds. But no architecture removes the need for the assumptions of Chapter 13. A network trained on the wrong prior, or on data with a hidden confounder, learns the wrong answer confidently. The chapter's thesis is that making AI smarter about causes means building the causal assumptions into what it is trained on, how it is trained and what it is asked to report.

Lecture 30 — Bayesian neural networks for causal questions

14.1 Networks as priors over functions

A neural network with one hidden layer of H units computes

f(x)=a+∑h=1Hvhϕ(bh+wh⊤x),

where ϕ is a fixed nonlinearity such as tanh, and the weights wh, biases bh and output weights vh are parameters. Deep networks stack several such layers. Machine learning usually fits the weights by minimising a loss. The Bayesian alternative puts a prior on the weights, and since every draw of the weights is a function, a prior on the weights is a prior on functions. Radford Neal (1996) showed what that prior looks like for a wide network: if the output weights have prior standard deviation proportional to 1/H, then as H→∞ the prior over functions becomes a Gaussian process (Chapter 8), by the central limit theorem applied to the sum over units. A finite network's prior over functions is close to a Gaussian process but not exactly one: it has extra, non-Gaussian structure. The widget draws functions from the prior.

Functions from a network prior

Eight functions, each from one draw of all the weights of a one-hidden-layer network with H units, input weights wh∼Normal(0,sw), biases bh∼Normal(0,sw) and output weights vh∼Normal(0,1/H). Widen the network and the functions settle into a Gaussian process; change the input scale and they change from smooth to wiggly.

The posterior over the weights is hard to work with. It is high-dimensional, and it has exact symmetries: swapping two hidden units, or flipping the signs of a tanh unit's input and output weights, leaves the function unchanged. So every posterior mode has H!2H copies, and different chains land in different ones. This is the label switching of Chapter 11, multiplied. The practical consequence is that the convergence of a network's chains must be judged on functions (predictions and effects), which are identified, not on the weights, which are not.

There are four common ways to approximate the posterior, and they differ mainly in how much of its spread they capture.

  • Hamiltonian Monte Carlo (Neal, 1996), feasible for small networks such as the one we fit below.
  • Deep ensembles (Lakshminarayanan, Pritzel and Blundell, 2017): train the network several times from different random starting weights and treat the trained networks as draws from the posterior. Each run lands in a different mode, which captures the multimodality, but not the spread within each mode.
  • The Laplace approximation (MacKay, 1992; Daxberger et al., 2021): a Gaussian around one trained network, cheap and surprisingly effective.
  • Variational inference (Chapter 9) and Monte Carlo dropout (Gal and Ghahramani, 2016), which is approximately variational, scale to the largest networks and tend to be overconfident.

The networks agree where there are data and disagree in the gap and beyond the ends, so the ensemble's spread grows where it should. But the band is often too narrow in the gap compared with what a Gaussian process with the same smoothness would give, and with few networks it varies from one ensemble to the next. Ensembles describe the disagreement between plausible functions, which is a large part of epistemic uncertainty, but not all of it.

14.2 A Bayesian network for individual effects

Chapter 13 used BART as the outcome model in g-computation and scored it on the IHDP benchmark (Hill, 2011; replication 1 of those distributed by Louizos et al., 2017), where every child's true effect is known. A neural network can play the same role. A good architecture for the job is the two-headed TARNet of Shalit, Johansson and Sontag (2017): a shared hidden layer learns a representation Φ(x) of the covariates, and two separate output heads predict the outcome under control and under treatment, μ0(x)=h0(Φ(x)) and μ1(x)=h1(Φ(x)). Each head is trained only on the units that received its treatment, while the representation is shared. The individual effect is τ(x)=μ1(x)−μ0(x). The causal graph does not change: the network is a flexible structural equation for Y given T and X, and identification still rests on no unmeasured confounding and on overlap.

We write the two-headed network directly in Stan, with 8 hidden tanh units shared by both heads, normal priors scaled by 1/fan-in, and sample it with HMC: 226 weights and biases in all, plus the noise standard deviation. On two cores it takes about three minutes.

tarnet_code <- "
data { int<lower=1> N; int<lower=1> P; int<lower=1> H; matrix[N, P] X; vector[N] t; vector[N] y; }
parameters {
  matrix[P, H] W; vector[H] b;                    // shared representation layer
  vector[H] v0; vector[H] v1; real a0; real a1;   // two heads: outcome under control and under treatment
  real<lower=0> sigma;
}
model {
  matrix[N, H] Z = tanh(X * W + rep_matrix(b', N));
  to_vector(W) ~ normal(0, 1 / sqrt(P)); b ~ normal(0, 1);
  v0 ~ normal(0, 1 / sqrt(H)); v1 ~ normal(0, 1 / sqrt(H)); a0 ~ normal(0, 2); a1 ~ normal(0, 2);
  sigma ~ exponential(1);
  y ~ normal(t .* (Z * v1 + a1) + (1 - t) .* (Z * v0 + a0), sigma);   // each head sees only its own units
}
generated quantities {
  vector[N] tau;                                  // individual effect: difference of the two heads
  { matrix[N, H] Z = tanh(X * W + rep_matrix(b', N)); tau = Z * (v1 - v0) + a1 - a0; }
}"

Check Yourself

Four HMC chains of a Bayesian neural network report R^=1.8 for the input weights and R^=1.00 for every predicted treatment effect. What should you conclude?

R-hat for non-identified parameters is not informative.

Weights are not interpretable one by one.

A network's weights are not identified: H!2H weight vectors give the same function. Chains that disagree on weights but agree on every function of interest have converged for every quantity we report. If they disagreed on predictions, that would be a real problem (different functional modes), as in the mixture models of Chapter 11.

The chains are in different but equivalent modes of the weight posterior (hidden units relabelled or sign-flipped), which give the same functions. Convergence should be judged on identified quantities such as predictions and effects, and these agree.

History: Bayesian Networks Before Deep Learning

Neural networks were Bayesian earlier than most people realise. David MacKay's 1991 Caltech thesis and his 1992 papers fitted networks with a Laplace approximation and used the evidence to choose their size and regularisation. Radford Neal's 1995 Toronto thesis, published as Bayesian Learning for Neural Networks (1996), sampled network weights with Hamiltonian Monte Carlo, which he had brought from physics into statistics a few years earlier, and proved that wide networks become Gaussian processes. Neal and Jianguo Zhang's Bayesian networks won the NIPS 2003 feature-selection challenge (Chapter 10). Then the field turned to ever larger networks trained by stochastic gradient descent, where full Bayesian inference was out of reach. The Bayesian ideas returned in approximate forms: dropout read as variational inference (Gal and Ghahramani, 2016), deep ensembles (2017), and Laplace approximations for large networks (Daxberger et al., 2021). In causal inference, the representation-learning networks TARNet (Shalit et al., 2017) and Dragonnet (Shi, Blei and Veitch, 2019) adapted network architectures to the structure of treatment-effect problems.

Key Takeaways: Lecture 30

  • A prior on a network's weights is a prior on functions; wide networks become Gaussian processes.
  • Network posteriors have huge numbers of equivalent modes: judge convergence on predictions and effects, never on weights.
  • Used as the outcome model in g-computation, a small Bayesian network estimated individual effects on IHDP at least as well as BART; the DAG and the identification assumptions are unchanged.
  • Deep ensembles capture disagreement between plausible functions cheaply, but their intervals are only as good as the number and diversity of members, and should be checked for coverage.

Lecture 31 — Transformers as Bayesian machines

14.3 Attention is kernel regression

The transformer (Vaswani et al., 2017) is the architecture behind every large language model. Its central operation, attention, is older and simpler than its reputation. Given a query q and a set of items, each with a key ki and a value vi, attention returns a weighted average of the values, with weights from a softmax of query–key similarities:

Attention(q)=∑iαivi,αi=exp⁡(s(q,ki))∑jexp⁡(s(q,kj)).

In a transformer, queries, keys and values are learned linear projections of the token representations, and s is a scaled dot product. Statisticians will recognise the formula: with s(q,ki)=−β(q−ki)2 it is exactly the Nadaraya–Watson kernel regression estimator of 1964, a locally weighted average with a Gaussian kernel of bandwidth 1/2β. And a dot product of suitably augmented features can represent this squared distance exactly, since −β(q−k)2=2βqk−βk2−βq2, and the last term is the same for every item and cancels in the softmax. A single attention head is a kernel smoother with a learned kernel. A transformer stacks many such heads, interleaved with small feed-forward networks, so that each layer re-weights and recombines the items in context.

Kernel smoothers are close relatives of the Gaussian processes of Chapter 8: the GP posterior mean is also a weighted sum of the observed outcomes, with weights from the kernel. The difference is that the GP's weights account for how the observed points relate to each other (two nearby points share their weight rather than each taking a full vote), and they shrink toward the prior mean when no data are nearby. Plain attention does neither. A common addition helps with the second: an extra "sink" item with a fixed key and a value of zero, which absorbs the attention when nothing in the context is relevant, so that the output falls back toward zero. 14.4 Learning to do inference

The widget's attention head was not tuned by hand. Its two parameters came from fitting it, in Stan, to simulated tasks: 400 data sets, each with twelve noisy observations of a different function drawn from a Gaussian-process prior and one held-out query point whose value the model must predict from the twelve. The model sees nothing but the twelve context points of each task; it has no notion of a GP. Trained to predict well across many tasks drawn from the prior, the attention head learns an approximation to the posterior predictive distribution under that prior, and it can then be applied to a new data set instantly, without any fitting. This is the idea of prior-fitted networks (Müller et al., 2022): to make a network do Bayesian inference, train it on data simulated from the prior. TabPFN (Hollmann et al., 2025), a transformer trained on millions of simulated tabular data sets drawn from a prior over structural causal models, predicts on small real data sets in a single forward pass, competitively with tuned gradient-boosted trees. Ours is the same idea with one attention head and three parameters (bandwidth, sink and a value scale), small enough to fit by HMC so that we also get a posterior over the attention parameters themselves.

R
set.seed(14)
sim_tasks <- function(M, K = 12, ell = 0.15, noise = 0.1) {   # M tasks: functions from a GP prior on [0, 1]
  xs <- matrix(runif(M * (K + 1)), M); ys <- matrix(0, M, K + 1)
  for (m in 1:M) { S <- exp(-outer(xs[m, ], xs[m, ], "-")^2 / (2 * ell^2)) + diag(1e-8, K + 1)
    ys[m, ] <- t(chol(S)) %*% rnorm(K + 1) + rnorm(K + 1, 0, noise) }
  list(xc = xs[, 1:K], yc = ys[, 1:K], xq = xs[, K + 1], yq = ys[, K + 1])
}
attn_code <- "
data { int<lower=1> M; int<lower=1> K; matrix[M, K] xc; matrix[M, K] yc; vector[M] xq; vector[M] yq; }
parameters { real log_beta; real b0; real<lower=0> c; real<lower=0> sigma; }
model {
  log_beta ~ normal(3, 2); b0 ~ normal(0, 3); c ~ normal(1, 1); sigma ~ exponential(2);
  for (m in 1:M) {
    vector[K + 1] s; vector[K + 1] v;
    for (k in 1:K) { s[k] = -exp(log_beta) * square(xq[m] - xc[m, k]); v[k] = c * yc[m, k]; }
    s[K + 1] = b0; v[K + 1] = 0;                        // the sink: attended to when no context point is close
    yq[m] ~ normal(dot_product(softmax(s), v), sigma);
  }
}"
train_tasks <- sim_tasks(400); test_tasks <- sim_tasks(2000)
fit_attn <- stan(model_code = attn_code, data = c(list(M = 400, K = 12), train_tasks), chains = 4, iter = 1000, seed = 14, refresh = 0)

The posterior is tight: log⁡β=5.65 (95% interval 5.38 to 5.92), so β≈280, a kernel bandwidth of about 0.04, much narrower than the GP's length-scale of 0.15: with little noise, the best smoother leans heavily on the nearest points. The sink score is b0=−8.0, so the sink takes over when the nearest context point is farther than about 8/280≈0.17, roughly the GP's length-scale, which is where the GP posterior mean also starts to fall back to the prior mean of zero. The value scale is 0.99. On 2,000 new tasks the attention head predicts with an RMSE of 0.330, against 0.235 for the exact GP posterior mean, 0.349 for the same head without a sink, and 0.996 for always predicting zero; its predictions correlate 0.97 with the GP posterior mean. A three-parameter head has learned most of what Bayesian inference under this prior does. The remaining gap is what a kernel smoother cannot represent: the GP accounts for how the context points relate to each other, sharing weight within clusters, and it continues trends a little way beyond the data. Those are the things that more heads and more layers let a transformer learn.

One head is not a transformer. With PyTorch, the same idea can be tried at scale: a transformer with three layers of four attention heads (about 159,000 parameters) is trained by stochastic gradient descent on freshly simulated tasks from the same Gaussian-process prior, each with twelve context points and eight query points, to output a mean and a standard deviation for every query. Following Müller et al. (2022), every token may attend only to the context, so each query is predicted from the context alone. Training uses 6,000 batches of 256 tasks, about 1.5 million simulated data sets, and takes a few minutes on two CPU cores; the trained weights are cached. We then evaluate it on the same 2,000 test tasks as the Stan model.

Why should training on simulated tasks produce Bayesian inference? Because the expected squared error over tasks drawn from the prior is minimised by the posterior mean under that prior: the best possible prediction of yq from the context is 𝔼[yq∣context], averaged over the prior's functions. A model trained to minimise that error, if flexible enough, converges to it. The same argument with the log loss gives the whole posterior predictive distribution. Xie et al. (2022) used a related argument to explain in-context learning in large language models, their ability to pick up a task from a few examples in the prompt: if the training text is a mixture of documents generated by many latent "concepts", next-word prediction forces the model to infer the concept from the context, which is implicit Bayesian inference. On this view a language model's pretraining data is its prior, a perspective that will matter in Section 14.8.

History: From Kernel Smoothers to Transformers

Kernel regression was proposed independently by Èlizbar Nadaraya and Geoffrey Watson in 1964. Attention as a neural-network mechanism appeared fifty years later in machine translation: Dzmitry Bahdanau, Kyunghyun Cho and Yoshua Bengio (2014; published at ICLR 2015) let a recurrent network "look back" at a weighted average of the source sentence's words when producing each word of the translation. In 2017 Ashish Vaswani and colleagues at Google removed the recurrence altogether in a paper titled "Attention is all you need"; the resulting transformer could be trained in parallel on enormous data sets and became the basis of the large language models of the 2020s. The Bayesian reading came soon after: in-context learning as implicit Bayesian inference (Xie et al., 2022), transformers trained as prior-fitted networks (Müller et al., 2022), and in 2025 a transformer trained entirely on simulated data, TabPFN, published in Nature as a foundation model for small tabular data sets.

  • Training a model on data simulated from a prior makes it approximate the posterior predictive under that prior (prior-fitted networks); one attention head fitted this way came close to the exact GP posterior mean, and a small transformer trained the same way matched it almost exactly, uncertainty included.   • In-context learning can be read as implicit Bayesian inference, with the pretraining data as the prior.
  • Uncertainty in language models is best measured on meanings (semantic entropy), not on tokens.

Lecture 32 — Learning causes, not shortcuts

14.5 Shortcuts are causal failures

A classifier trained to minimise error uses whatever predicts the label in its training data. Some predictive features are causes or reliable consequences of the label; others are accidents of how the data were collected. Geirhos et al. (2020) called the habit of relying on the second kind shortcut learning, and collected examples: image classifiers that recognise cows by the green pasture behind them, pneumonia detectors that learned which hospital's scanner took the X-ray, and language models that answer reading-comprehension questions from superficial word overlap. Chapter 12 added one of our own: in the finance answers of HC3 (Guo et al., 2023), a formatting artefact separated ChatGPT from people almost perfectly.

In causal terms, a shortcut is a feature connected to the label by a path that runs through the data-collection process, a path that need not exist, or may point the other way, in new data. The causal graph of Chapter 12 already said this. What it adds is a remedy: if the mechanism by which the true causes produce the label is the same everywhere, while the shortcuts vary from one data source to another, then features whose relation to the label is stable across sources are more likely to be causal, and a model built on them is more likely to keep working on a source it has never seen. This is the principle of invariance.

HC3 lets us test it. Besides finance and open-domain questions, it has medical questions (1,246 with both kinds of answer) and computer-science questions from Wikipedia (842). We treat the four subsets as four environments: different questions, different human sources and different collection details, but the same kind of author on each side.

14.6 Invariance across environments

Peters, Bühlmann and Meinshausen (2016) turned the invariance principle into a method, invariant causal prediction: if a set of predictors contains the causes of the outcome, the conditional distribution of the outcome given those predictors is the same in every environment, and sets that fail this test can be rejected. Arjovsky et al. (2019) proposed a version for deep networks, invariant risk minimisation, which looks for a representation on which the optimal classifier is the same across environments. Later work found that invariant risk minimisation can fail in simple settings, especially with few environments (Rosenfeld, Ravikumar and Risteski, 2021). The principle is sound; turning it into a reliable algorithm is hard. The widget shows the principle in its simplest form.

Pooled or invariant?

A binary label with a causal feature (same strength everywhere) and a spurious feature whose association with the label differs by environment. Two training environments are pooled; the test environment can reverse the spurious association. Bars show the test accuracy of a classifier trained on both features (pooled) and one trained on the causal feature only (invariant). Accuracies are computed exactly for Gaussian features.

When the spurious association is the same in both training environments, nothing in the training data distinguishes it from a cause, and the pooled classifier uses it, with a large gain in accuracy if the test environment resembles the training ones and a large loss if it reverses. Only when the training environments disagree does the spurious feature reveal itself. Invariance needs variation: environments that differ in the shortcuts and agree in the mechanism.

We apply a simple, transparent version to HC3. The training environments are finance and medicine; open-domain and Wikipedia computer-science answers are held out entirely as test environments. The candidate features are the 300 most widespread words (as log counts, standardised) plus two non-lexical features, the glued full stop ("seam") and the log length of the answer. For each feature and each training environment we fit a one-feature Bayesian logistic regression (normal(0, 1) prior, Laplace approximation as in Chapter 9). We call a feature stable if its association has the same sign in both environments, is clearly non-zero in both (posterior mean more than two standard deviations from zero) and differs in size by less than a factor of two. Then we compare four detectors, each a Bayesian logistic regression on standardised features, trained on finance and medicine together: all features; all features except the two non-lexical artefacts; the stable features only; and, for reference, all features trained on finance alone.

Check Yourself

A team training a detector of AI-written essays has data from one university only. Why can't invariance methods help them, and what could?

The amount of data is not the issue.

A held-out set from the same source shares the same shortcuts.

Random splits of one source share every shortcut, so every feature looks stable. Environments must differ in the ways deployment will differ. When they are not available, the alternative is to use domain knowledge to remove known artefacts (as removing the seam did here), or to create variation deliberately, for example by counterfactual editing.

Invariance is learned from differences between environments; with one environment there is no variation to separate stable from unstable features. They need data from several sources that differ in the likely shortcuts (courses, prompts, generators, student populations), or causal knowledge about which features are artefacts.

Two further tools make text models more causal. Counterfactual data augmentation (Kaushik, Hovy and Lipton, 2020) asks people to make minimal edits that flip a document's label and adds the edited documents to the training data. A model trained on such pairs cannot rely on features that the edits left unchanged, because those features no longer predict the label. For AI-text detection, the analogue is paraphrasing machine text into a human register, and human text into a machine register. When text is a confounder, treatment or outcome in a causal analysis, as in "does the tone of a complaint affect how fast it is answered?", Veitch, Sridhar and Blei (2020) and Egami et al. (2022) show how to adjust for text with learned representations, and how not to: fitting the representation and estimating the effect on the same documents invites overfitting that looks like an effect. The common thread is causal representation learning (Schölkopf et al., 2021): finding variables in raw data, such as pixels or words, that behave like the variables of a causal graph, with stable mechanisms between them.

Research Practice: Claims About Robustness and Causal Ability

  • Report out-of-distribution performance on environments that were held out entirely (not used for selecting features, tuning or stopping), and say how they differ from the training environments.
  • Report uncertainty for deep models: the approximation used (HMC, ensemble, Laplace, VI), calibration on held-out data, and coverage where the truth is known (semi-synthetic benchmarks).
  • For treatment-effect estimates from networks, state the identification assumptions separately from the architecture, and show overlap: flexibility does not repair a design.
  • For claims that an AI system "reasons causally", say which rung of the ladder is tested, how contamination of the benchmark by training data was ruled out, and how sensitive results are to paraphrasing the question.
  • Report negative and mixed results: methods such as invariant risk minimisation are known to fail in some settings, and a paper that shows where is as useful as one that shows where they work.

Key Takeaways: Lecture 32

  • Shortcut learning is a causal failure: the model uses a path through the data-collection process that will not hold elsewhere.
  • Invariance: causal mechanisms are stable across environments, shortcuts are not. Exploiting it needs environments that genuinely differ.
  • On HC3, the formatting artefact reversed between environments and was rejected by the stability screen. Removing artefacts improved out-of-domain accuracy, but every detector remained far less accurate on new kinds of question than on the training kinds.
  • Counterfactual augmentation, careful text adjustment and causal representation learning are complementary ways to give models causal structure.

14.7 Learning causal inference from simulated worlds

Section 14.4 trained a model on simulated data sets to make it do Bayesian prediction. The same trick can be pointed at causal questions: simulate many worlds from a prior over structural causal models, record each world's data and its true causal effect, and train a network to map data to effect. Applied to a real data set, the network returns an approximation to the posterior of the effect under that prior over worlds, in a single forward pass. This is amortised causal inference: the cost of inference is paid once, in training, and then spread ("amortised") across every future data set. Do-PFN (Robertson et al., 2025) applies it with a transformer pretrained on synthetic causal models, including their interventions, and reports accurate effect estimates from observational data on synthetic and semi-synthetic benchmarks without being told the causal graph.

It is worth asking what such a network can possibly learn, and a small example makes the answer clear. Each simulated world is the confounded triangle of Chapter 13: a covariate C affects treatment T with coefficient a and outcome Y with coefficient b, and T affects Y with the causal effect τ. The prior draws a,b∼Normal(0,1), τ∼Normal(0,1) and noise standard deviations between 0.5 and 1.5, and each world has 200 observations. We summarise each world's data by its sample covariances and train small networks (20 hidden units, nnet) on 20,000 worlds to predict τ. One network sees all six covariances of (C,T,Y); the other sees only the three covariances of (T,Y), as if C were unmeasured. Both are compared, on 2,000 fresh worlds from the same prior, with the regressions of Chapter 13: Y on T and C (the back-door adjustment), and Y on T alone (naive).

R
set.seed(1414)
sim_scm <- function(R, n = 200, conf_sd = 1) {          # R worlds from a prior over linear SCMs: C -> T, C -> Y, T -> Y
  map_dfr(1:R, function(r) {
    a <- rnorm(1, 0, conf_sd); b <- rnorm(1, 0, conf_sd); tau <- rnorm(1)
    C <- rnorm(n); Tr <- a * C + rnorm(n, 0, runif(1, 0.5, 1.5)); Y <- tau * Tr + b * C + rnorm(n, 0, runif(1, 0.5, 1.5))
    S <- cov(cbind(C, Tr, Y))
    tibble(vC = S[1, 1], cCT = S[1, 2], cCY = S[1, 3], vT = S[2, 2], cTY = S[2, 3], vY = S[3, 3], tau = tau,
           adjusted = (S[1, 1] * S[2, 3] - S[1, 2] * S[1, 3]) / (S[1, 1] * S[2, 2] - S[1, 2]^2),   # Y ~ T + C
           naive = S[2, 3] / S[2, 2])                                                             # Y ~ T
  })
}
train_worlds <- sim_scm(20000); test_worlds <- sim_scm(2000)

fit_net <- function(d, inp) nnet(as.matrix(d[, inp]), d$tau, size = 20, linout = TRUE, decay = 1e-3, maxit = 2000, trace = FALSE)
net_obs <- fit_net(train_worlds, inp_obs); net_hid <- fit_net(train_worlds, inp_hid)
rmse <- function(est, truth) sqrt(mean((est - truth)^2))

  adjusted_regression = rmse(d$adjusted, d$tau), net_C_hidden = rmse(predict(net_hid, as.matrix(d[, inp_hid])), d$tau),
  naive_regression = rmse(d$naive, d$tau), prior_mean = rmse(0, d$tau))

Three lessons follow, and each is a lesson of Chapter 13 in a new costume. First, with the confounder observed, the network learned to adjust: it approximates the back-door estimate without ever having been told the back-door criterion, because the simulated worlds contained the information that adjustment is the right thing to do. Second, with the confounder hidden, no amount of training helps: the network does a little better than the naive regression, because it has learned how much confounding to expect on average under the prior, but the effect is not identified, and the network cannot extract information the data do not contain. Third, the network is only as good as its prior over worlds: outside the range of confounding it was trained on, it degrades, while the regression, which encodes the identification argument directly, does not. In a prototype, a network trained on worlds with weak confounding (standard deviation 0.2) and tested on strong confounding (standard deviation 2) had an RMSE of 0.68, nearly as bad as the naive regression (0.83) and eight times the adjusted regression's (0.08): it had never seen confounding that strong, so it had never learned to remove it. Amortisation buys speed and flexibility, not identification.

14.8 Can language models climb the ladder?

Large language models answer causal questions fluently. Asked whether altitude causes temperature or temperature causes altitude, they answer correctly; asked what would have happened had a patient not taken a drug, they produce plausible counterfactuals. Kıcıman et al. (2023) found GPT-4 answering pairwise "which causes which" questions with 97% accuracy and counterfactual-reasoning questions with 92%, well above earlier algorithms, and argued that language models carry a large store of causal knowledge extracted from text: the kind of domain knowledge that analysts have always used to draw DAGs.

Knowledge is not reasoning. The pairwise questions concern variables whose causal relation is written about at length, so a model can answer them from association with the text describing them. Zečević et al. (2023) called such models causal parrots: they repeat causal facts that appear in their training data, which is association at the level of text. CLadder (Jin et al., 2023) tests the distinction directly with more than 10,000 questions generated from formal causal graphs and queries on all three rungs, including stories whose causal relations are deliberately anti-commonsensical or nonsensical, so that memorised facts cannot help and only the stated graph and probabilities can. GPT-4 answered about 62% correctly, where 50% is chance; a prompting strategy that walks the model through the formal steps (extracting the graph, choosing the estimand, applying the do-calculus, then computing) raised it to 70%.

Since CLadder is public, we can run a small version of its test ourselves. We take 450 questions, 50 from each rung in each of the three kinds of story (commonsensical, anti-commonsensical and nonsensical), balanced between "yes" and "no" answers, and put them to two small open language models, Qwen2.5-Instruct with 0.5 and 1.5 billion parameters (Qwen Team, 2024), small enough to run on two CPU cores. Each model reads the question and we compare the probability it gives to answering "Yes" with the probability of "No". These models are about a thousand times smaller than GPT-4, so the test is a demonstration of method, not a measure of the state of the art.

14.9 Interventions inside networks

Causal reasoning can also be turned on the network itself. A trained network is a structural causal model: inputs cause hidden activations, which cause outputs, by known equations. We can therefore intervene on a hidden unit, setting its activation to the value it would have taken on a different input, and observe the effect on the output. This is activation patching, and it is causal mediation analysis (Chapter 13) applied to a network's internal variables: the change in output when a unit is patched is the indirect effect of the input change transmitted through that unit (Vig et al., 2020). Meng et al. (2022) used it to locate where factual associations are stored in GPT-style models; Geiger et al. (2021) used interventions to test whether a network implements a hypothesised high-level causal model. The field is called mechanistic interpretability.

We apply it to the network of Section 14.7 that sees C. Take the worlds where adjustment matters (where the adjusted and naive regressions differ by more than 0.2). For each, compare the network's output on the real input with its output on a counterfactual input in which the two covariances involving C are set to zero, as if there were no confounding; the network then returns roughly the naive answer. The difference is the total effect of the confounding information. Then patch hidden units, one at a time and in groups, with their counterfactual values, and ask how much of the total each carries.

Large models have millions of hidden units and billions of weights, so interpretability research looks for the right variables to intervene on: directions in activation space, or groups of units, that behave like the variables of a simple causal model (Geiger et al., 2021). 14.10 What it would take

Chapter 13 ended with three things that more data from the same source cannot supply. This chapter has shown each of them at work inside deep learning, and they make a short programme for causal AI.

  1. Causal assumptions as structure. TARNet's two heads build the potential-outcomes structure into the architecture; the amortised network learned adjustment only because the simulated worlds encoded which variables were confounders. Assumptions can be built into architectures, priors over worlds, or prompts, but they must be stated somewhere, and they must be the right ones.
  2. Interventional and multi-environment data. Invariance needs environments that differ; counterfactual augmentation creates such differences deliberately; experiments supply them directly. Models trained only on observational data from one source learn its shortcuts.
  3. Honest uncertainty. The Bayesian network's intervals widened, if only a little, where treated and control children did not overlap; semantic entropy flags confabulation; a prior-fitted network's posterior is only as honest as its prior. Uncertainty must be checked against truth where truth is available (semi-synthetic benchmarks, held-out environments) before it is trusted where it is not.

None of these is specific to neural networks, and that is the point of the course's last chapter: the ideas of Bayesian inference and causal reasoning that work for a dozen variables are the ideas that make the largest models more reliable. A network is a flexible likelihood; the rest of the analysis, the estimand, the graph, the prior, the check against reality, is still ours to do.

Project Connections: Deep Models in the Five Projects

For AI-generated text detection, Lecture 32 is the template: collect training data from several sources that differ in prompts, topics, generators and human populations; treat them as environments; report accuracy on sources held out entirely; and remove known artefacts before claiming that a detector has learned "how AI writes". Counterfactual pairs (the same content paraphrased by a person and by a model) are the strongest training data. Key Takeaways: Lecture 33

  • Amortised causal inference trains a network on simulated worlds to output causal effects. It learns adjustment when the confounder is observed, but cannot overcome non-identification, and it fails outside its prior over worlds.
  • Language models hold much causal knowledge from text, useful for proposing assumptions; benchmarks with novel causal structures show that formal causal reasoning remains weak, and small open models were at chance on our sample of CLadder.
  • Activation patching is causal mediation inside a network; even small networks spread a computation across many interacting units.
  • Causal AI needs stated assumptions, interventional or multi-environment data, and checked uncertainty: the same three things as any causal analysis.

    With finance and open-domain answers as the training environments, the seam again flips sign (log-odds per standard deviation 3.53 in finance, −1.65 in open-domain answers) and is rejected; length is again rejected (0.86 against 2.96). The screen keeps 42 features.   5. A broader prior over worlds. Retrain the amortised network with C observed on worlds whose confounding coefficients have standard deviation 2 instead of 1, and test it on the grid of Section 14.7. What did the broader prior buy, and what did it cost?

    sim_scm(20000, conf_sd = 2), then reuse score_worlds() after replacing net_obs.

    Trained on worlds with confounding standard deviation 2, the network's RMSE is 0.129 to 0.151 for test confounding up to 2 and 0.267 at 3, against 0.321 and 0.582 for the network trained with standard deviation 1. It has bought robustness to strong confounding at a price: for weak and moderate confounding (0.25 to 1) its error is 0.13 to 0.14, against 0.09 for the narrower prior. The regression, which encodes the identification argument directly, stays at 0.08 to 0.10 throughout. A broader prior over worlds spreads the network's capacity over more kinds of world; it does not replace knowing which estimator is right.

  6. A sensitivity analysis from a network. The network with C hidden in Section 14.7 returns one number per world. Explain why it cannot be calibrated as a posterior for τ unless the real world's confounding resembles the prior's, and connect this to the tipping-point sensitivity analysis of Chapter 13.

    What determines the spread of τ given the observed covariances of T and Y?

    Given only (T,Y), the effect is not identified: for any τ, there are values of the confounding coefficients that reproduce the observed covariances (the widget in Section 14.7 constructs them). So the posterior of τ given the data is driven by the prior over confounding, not by the data, and no amount of data narrows it. A network trained under one prior reports that prior's implied answer as if it were a finding. The honest alternative is the sensitivity analysis of Chapter 13: report how strong a confounder would have to be to change the conclusion, and let readers judge whether confounding that strong is plausible. A Bayesian analysis can do the same by reporting the posterior under several priors for the confounding, making the dependence on the prior explicit instead of hiding it inside a trained network.

  7. Patching a different variable. In Section 14.9, patch hidden units with values from an input in which only cCY is set to zero (keeping cCT). Is the effect of removing the C–Y information carried by the same units as before?

    Reuse patch() with a new H_cf.

    Removing only the C–Y covariance gives a much larger change in output (mean absolute 2.07 against 0.52), because an input with C correlated with T but not with Y is unusual under the prior. The single-unit effects are only moderately related to those of Section 14.9 (rank correlation 0.36), although the most influential unit (unit 3) is the same in both. The computation is again distributed, if less so: the top unit carries 11% of the effect, the top five 48%, the top ten 80%. Different counterfactual inputs probe different parts of the computation, so a patching result is a statement about a specific intervention, not about "what a unit does".

  8. Reviewing a claim. A preprint claims: "Our 7-billion-parameter language model performs causal inference: on our benchmark of 500 questions about the effects of medical treatments it answers 91% correctly." Using the Research Practice box in Section 14.6, write four questions you would ask the authors.

    Rungs, contamination, novelty, paraphrase, uncertainty.

    Example. (1) Which rung does each question test, and can the correct answers be found in medical text the model was trained on? A question such as "does aspirin reduce the risk of heart attack?" tests recall of published causal facts, not causal inference. (2) How was contamination ruled out: were the questions, or the studies they were drawn from, published before the model's training cutoff? (3) Does accuracy hold on questions with novel or counter-intuitive causal structure (as in CLadder's anti-commonsensical stories), and on paraphrased versions of the same questions? (4) Does the model express uncertainty appropriately: is it less confident on questions where the evidence is weak or conflicting, and how is its calibration measured? A fifth question is about the comparison: how does a simple retrieval baseline (searching the medical literature) do on the same benchmark?
