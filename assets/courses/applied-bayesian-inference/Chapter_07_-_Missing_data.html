Chapter 7
Missing data

Lectures 13–14 · missingness mechanisms, imputation as a model, and how far the answer depends on what we cannot see

Real data sets have holes. A survey respondent skips the income question, a sensor drops out, a hospital stops reporting, a country has no system for registering deaths. The analyst is left with a table that is not rectangular, and the standard software response is to delete every row with a hole and carry on with the rows that survive. That reflex has a name, complete-case analysis, and this chapter is about when it is harmless, when it is wasteful and when it gives the wrong answer.

The Bayesian view of missing data is short enough to state in one sentence: a missing value is an unknown quantity, and unknown quantities get posterior distributions. A missing value becomes a parameter, with a prior given by a model for how such values are distributed, and is estimated along with everything else. Nothing new is needed: no special algorithm, just a model for the missing values. What is new is a question that no algorithm can answer for us: why are the values missing? As in every chapter so far, a causal graph is how we make that question precise.

  • Lecture 13 is about missing outcomes. We will estimate how many people died worldwide because of the COVID-19 pandemic in 2020 and 2021, when excess deaths can be measured in only 86 of the 158 countries with more than a million people. We classify missingness with Rubin's taxonomy, see why the countries that report are a very unrepresentative sample, and build a regression model that imputes the missing countries, with honest uncertainty.
  • Lecture 14 is about missing predictors and missing mechanisms. We impute missing predictors inside the model with brms's mi(), see why the popular shortcuts (filling in the mean, or a single predicted value) mislead, meet Rubin's multiple imputation, and finish with the case that no model can fix, missingness that depends on the missing value itself, and the sensitivity analysis that makes it visible.

Lecture 13 — Missing outcomes

7.1 How many people died?

Counting the dead of a pandemic sounds simple and is not. Official COVID-19 death counts depend on testing, on what doctors write on death certificates, and on whether deaths are registered at all, and all three vary enormously between countries. Demographers therefore use a different measure: excess mortality, the number of deaths from all causes beyond what would have been expected had there been no pandemic. Expected deaths come from projecting each country's pre-2020 trend in deaths forward. Excess mortality counts deaths from COVID-19 that were never diagnosed, and also deaths caused indirectly (delayed treatment, overwhelmed hospitals) or prevented (fewer traffic accidents and less influenza during lockdowns).

Excess mortality needs one thing that many countries do not have: a timely, complete count of all deaths. The World Mortality Dataset (Karlinsky and Kobak, 2021) collected weekly or monthly death counts from every country that publishes them, and Our World in Data distributes the resulting estimates of excess deaths. We combine them with reported COVID-19 deaths and a few country characteristics, and keep the 158 countries with more than a million people.

History: Counting Deaths

The idea of judging an epidemic by deaths "in excess" of the usual number is as old as vital statistics. William Farr, who compiled the statistics of England's General Register Office from 1839 to 1879, used weekly registered deaths to measure the toll of the cholera epidemics of 1849 and 1854 against the deaths expected in an ordinary year. In 1963 Robert Serfling at the US Communicable Disease Center put the idea into a regression model: fit a seasonal curve to deaths in non-epidemic years, and call the deaths above it excess influenza mortality. Versions of Serfling's model are still used for influenza surveillance. COVID-19 made excess mortality front-page news, and also exposed how unequal the world's death registration systems are: in its first global assessment of health information systems (the SCORE report, 2020–21), the World Health Organization estimated that about 4 in 10 deaths worldwide were not registered at all, and that in its African region only about 1 in 10 deaths was registered.

Which brings us to the obvious estimate of the world total, and why it is wrong. Take the population-weighted average excess death rate in the observed countries and multiply it by the world's population:

7.2 Why values go missing

Whether complete-case analysis is harmless depends on why values are missing, and that is a causal question. Add to the causal graph a missingness indicator RY: a variable that equals 1 if Y was observed and 0 if not. The question is what causes RY. Donald Rubin's 1976 taxonomy distinguishes three cases, drawn below for an outcome Y and a fully observed variable X that affects it.

  • Missing completely at random (MCAR). Missingness is unrelated to anything in the problem: a lab sample was dropped, a page of a questionnaire was lost in the post. The observed rows are a random sample of all rows. Complete-case analysis is unbiased, but it wastes the information in the incomplete rows.
  • Missing at random (MAR). Missingness depends only on variables we observe. Here, whether a country can measure excess deaths depends on its income and the age of its population, which we know. The observed rows are a biased sample, so a simple average of them is biased, but the bias is repairable: comparing countries with the same income and age, the observed and missing ones are alike, so a model that conditions on income and age can predict the missing ones.
  • Missing not at random (MNAR). Missingness depends on the missing value itself, or on something unobserved that also affects it. If the countries hardest hit by the pandemic were also the ones whose registration systems broke down, then even among countries with the same income and age, the missing ones would have higher excess deaths than the observed ones. No model of the observed data alone can detect or remove this bias.

The names are confusing (MAR does not mean missingness is random), but the graphs are not. Under MCAR and MAR, the missingness mechanism can be ignored when we fit a model that conditions on the variables that drive it; Rubin called such mechanisms ignorable. Under MNAR it cannot. Whether a mechanism is MAR or MNAR cannot be tested from the data, because the data say nothing about the values we do not have. It has to be argued from knowledge of how the data were collected, exactly like the arrows of any other causal graph.

Different estimates are affected differently, and the widget makes this concrete. It uses the life expectancy and GDP per person of the 154 countries with more than a million people where both are known, deletes some life expectancies according to a mechanism you choose, and compares two quantities computed from the complete cases with their true values: the average life expectancy, and the slope of life expectancy on log GDP.

Three mechanisms, two estimates

Life expectancy against log GDP per person for 154 countries. Filled points are observed; open points have had their life expectancy deleted. The blue line is the regression on the observed points; the grey dashed line is the regression on all points. "Regression imputation" fills each missing life expectancy with the observed-data regression's prediction from that country's GDP, and averages.

Three lessons come out of a few clicks. Under MCAR, the complete cases give unbiased answers for both the mean and the slope; they are just noisier. Under MAR (missingness depending on GDP), the complete-case mean is biased upward, because poor countries with low life expectancy are missing, but the complete-case slope is still right: among countries with the same GDP, those that are observed are a random sample, so the regression of Y on X is unaffected. And regression imputation of the mean repairs the bias, because it uses the observed relationship to predict the missing countries from their GDP. Under MNAR, everything is biased, including the slope and the imputation. That is the situation the world excess-mortality estimate must hope to avoid.

Check Yourself

An online survey asks students how many hours a week they study. Students who study very little are less likely to answer the question. Which mechanism is this?

Missingness is related to the value of the answer.

Is the cause of missingness something we observe?

The probability of non-response depends on the very quantity that is missing (hours of study), so the mechanism is MNAR. If the survey also recorded something that explains non-response, such as course grades, and students with the same grade were equally likely to answer whatever their study hours, the mechanism would be MAR given grades. Recording such auxiliary variables is one of the best defences against MNAR.

Missing not at random: the probability of a missing answer depends on the answer itself.

Definition: Ignorable Missingness

Let Yobs and Ymis be the observed and missing parts of the data and R the missingness indicators. The mechanism is missing at random if Pr(R∣Yobs,Ymis,ϕ)=Pr(R∣Yobs,ϕ): given the observed data, missingness carries no further information about the missing values. If, in addition, the parameters ϕ of the missingness mechanism are unrelated (a priori independent) to the parameters θ of the data model, the posterior for θ can be computed from p(Yobs∣θ)p(θ) alone, ignoring R entirely. This is Rubin's (1976) result, and it is what licenses every method in this chapter except the last.

7.3 Missing outcomes: imputing the world

If the mechanism is MAR given some observed country characteristics, then a regression of excess deaths on those characteristics, fitted to the observed countries, is unbiased (the slope lesson of the widget), and its predictions for the missing countries are the imputations we need. Which characteristics? Three are available for almost every country and plausibly affect both excess deaths and whether they are measured:

  • reported COVID-19 deaths per million, which, imperfect as they are, track the size of each country's epidemic;
  • median age, because the risk of death from COVID-19 rises steeply with age;
  • GDP per person, which affects health care, the ability to work from home and the completeness of reporting (in poorer countries, fewer deaths are tested and reported, so the same number of reported deaths implies more real ones).

The model is a linear regression of excess deaths per 1,000 people on log reported deaths, median age and log GDP. The predictors are centred so the intercept is the excess death rate for a country with 500 reported deaths per million, a median age of 30 and GDP of $10,000 per person. We set aside the six countries with a missing predictor (Section 7.5 brings them back).

R
known <- filter(covid, !is.na(r), !is.na(g))     # countries with complete predictors
m_cc <- brm(y ~ r + a + g, data = filter(known, !is.na(y)),
            prior = c(prior(normal(2, 2), class = Intercept),   # excess per 1,000 for the reference country
                      prior(normal(0, 2), class = b),
                      prior(exponential(1), class = sigma)),
            seed = 7, refresh = 0, file = "fits/ch7_cc")
fixef(m_cc) |> round(2)
posterior_summary(m_cc, variable = "sigma") |> round(2)

All three predictors matter, in the expected directions. Each doubling of reported deaths adds about 0.78×log⁡2≈0.5 excess deaths per 1,000 people. Ten more years of median age, at the same reported deaths and income, adds about 0.8 per 1,000. And each doubling of GDP per person lowers excess deaths by about 1.4×log⁡2≈1 per 1,000 at the same reported deaths and age: richer countries reported a larger share of their real deaths, and probably prevented more. The residual standard deviation is about 1.4 deaths per 1,000, which is large: countries with the same characteristics differ a lot, and any single country's prediction will be uncertain.

Now the imputation itself. For each missing country, draw its excess death rate from the posterior predictive distribution, multiply by its population and add up. Each posterior draw is one completed world; the spread of the totals across draws is our uncertainty about the world total, and it includes both our uncertainty about the regression coefficients and the country-to-country scatter.

R
missing <- filter(known, is.na(y))
set.seed(7)
draws <- posterior_predict(m_cc, newdata = missing)            # draws x missing countries
total_missing <- draws %*% missing$population / 1e9            # millions of deaths, per draw
total_observed <- with(filter(known, !is.na(y)), sum(y * population)) / 1e9
world <- total_observed + total_missing
c(observed = total_observed, quantile(total_missing, c(.5, .025, .975)))
quantile(world, c(.5, .025, .975))
output

The posterior median for the world total is about 14.6 million excess deaths in 2020–21, with a 95% interval of roughly 7.4 to 21 million. That is nearly three times the reported COVID-19 deaths, and far below the naive extrapolation. It sits close to the World Health Organization's published estimate of 14.9 million (Msemburi et al., 2023) and somewhat below the estimate of 18.2 million from the Institute for Health Metrics and Evaluation (IHME; COVID-19 Excess Mortality Collaborators, 2022). Our interval is much wider than either, because those teams also used subnational data, surveys and much richer covariates. A model with three predictors cannot match that; the point is that it lands in the right place, for the right reason, and says honestly how uncertain it is.

The country-level imputations are where the action is. India alone contributes about 4 million excess deaths in our model (about 2.8 per 1,000 people), against about 480,000 reported. The WHO's estimate for India, about 4.7 million, was rejected by the Indian government when it was published in 2022 and became one of the most contested numbers of the pandemic; independent studies using Indian survey and death registration data (for example Jha et al., 2022) also found several times the official count. China is the opposite case: it reported almost no COVID-19 deaths in 2020–21, far outside the range of any observed country, and the model extrapolates to a negative excess (fewer deaths than usual), with a wide interval. Section 7.4 asks whether we should believe that.

Research Practice: Imputation Is Prediction

When the outcome is missing and the mechanism is MAR given the predictors, the complete-case regression is already unbiased, and imputing the missing outcomes adds nothing to the coefficients. What it adds is the ability to answer questions about the whole population: a world total, a national prevalence, a mean over all respondents. Those questions are prediction problems, and they should be treated with the standards of prediction: report predictive (not just credible) intervals, check calibration on held-out data, and be explicit about how far the imputed units are from the observed ones. The excess-mortality literature is a good model: the WHO and IHME papers report validation on held-out countries, not just model fit.

7.4 Checking an imputation by extrapolating

The imputations for the missing countries are predictions for countries unlike any we have observed: younger, poorer, and with fewer reported deaths. We cannot check those predictions directly, but we can check the model's ability to extrapolate in the same direction. Split the observed countries by income, fit the model to the richer half only, and predict the poorer half, whose true values we know.

R
obs_k <- filter(known, !is.na(y))
rich <- filter(obs_k, g >= median(g)); poor <- filter(obs_k, g < median(g))
m_rich <- update(m_cc, newdata = rich, seed = 7, refresh = 0)
set.seed(7)
pp_poor <- posterior_predict(m_rich, newdata = poor)
tibble(countries = nrow(poor),
       mean_error = mean(colMeans(pp_poor) - poor$y),                       # predicted minus actual
       rmse = sqrt(mean((colMeans(pp_poor) - poor$y)^2)),
       coverage_95 = mean(poor$y > apply(pp_poor, 2, quantile, .025) &
                          poor$y < apply(pp_poor, 2, quantile, .975)),
       total_error_millions = sum((colMeans(pp_poor) - poor$y) * poor$population) / 1e9)
output
# A tibble: 1 x 5
  countries mean_error  rmse coverage_95 total_error_millions
      <int>      <dbl> <dbl>       <dbl>                <dbl>
1        42       1.04  2.06       0.857                 1.22

The check is sobering. Fitted to the 42 richer observed countries, the model over-predicts the 42 poorer ones by about 1 excess death per 1,000 on average, with a root mean squared error of about 2 per 1,000, and its 95% predictive intervals contain only 86% of the true values instead of 95%. Extrapolated toward poorer countries, the linear model overshoots, and it is overconfident. That does not mean the imputations for the missing countries are too high by the same amount: the full model is fitted to the poorer observed countries too, so it does not extrapolate as far. But the missing countries are poorer and younger still, and the check is a warning that the model's form, not just its parameters, is being stretched. It points toward a world total somewhat lower than our 14.6 million; the published estimates, which used much more information, came out at or above ours. When two honest checks pull in opposite directions, the right response is the sensitivity analysis of Section 7.7, not a confident single number.

This kind of check does not prove that the imputations for Africa and South Asia are right; nothing observed can. It does tell us whether the model's form survives extrapolation in the relevant direction, and whether its intervals are honest when it does. China is the case that most needs scrutiny: its reported death rate (4 per million) is less than half the lowest observed (New Zealand's, about 10), and its political circumstances (strict elimination of the virus throughout 2020–21, and doubts about the reported numbers) are unlike any observed country's. A careful analyst would report the world total with and without the model's extrapolation for China, as the widget in Section 7.7 lets you do.

Key Takeaways: Lecture 13

  • Ask why values are missing before choosing a method. Draw the missingness indicator R in the causal graph.
  • MCAR: complete cases are a random sample. MAR: missingness depends only on observed variables; condition on them. MNAR: missingness depends on the missing values; no model of the observed data can fix it.
  • With a missing outcome that is MAR given the predictors, the complete-case regression is unbiased; imputation is posterior predictive simulation, and is needed for population totals.
  • Our model puts world excess mortality in 2020–21 at about 14.6 million (7.4 to 21 million), nearly three times reported COVID-19 deaths and far below the naive extrapolation.
  • Imputations are extrapolations. Check the model by extrapolating within the observed data, and report the cases (China) that lie outside it.

Lecture 14 — Missing predictors and missing mechanisms

7.5 Missing predictors: imputation inside the model

Six of the 158 countries were left out of the analysis so far because a predictor was missing: Cuba, Somalia, Syria and North Korea have no GDP figure in the data; North Korea and Turkmenistan, which both claimed no COVID-19 deaths at all, have no credible reported death rate; and Hong Kong has no reported death rate in the file. Complete-case analysis drops them silently, in two ways. Cuba and Hong Kong have observed excess death rates (Cuba's one of the highest in the Americas), and they vanish from the fit; and Somalia, Syria, North Korea and Turkmenistan, with about 72 million people between them, vanish from the world total, as if nobody died there.

The Bayesian remedy is to treat each missing predictor value as a parameter. It needs a prior, and the prior should itself be a model: here, log GDP is predicted from life expectancy and median age (which are known for every country), and log reported deaths likewise. Each missing value is then informed from two directions at once: by its imputation model, which says what values are plausible for a country with that life expectancy and age, and by the outcome model, which says what value would be consistent with the country's observed outcome. In brms, a variable with missing values is marked | mi() on the left of its own formula and enters other formulas as mi(variable). The outcome is marked | mi() too, so the missing excess death rates become parameters as well, and the whole imputation happens in one fit.

R
f_mi <- bf(y | mi() ~ mi(r) + a + mi(g)) +       # outcome model: missing y imputed too
        bf(r | mi() ~ le + a) +                    # imputation model for log reported deaths
        bf(g | mi() ~ le + a) +                    # imputation model for log GDP
        set_rescor(FALSE)
m_mi <- brm(f_mi, data = covid,
            prior = c(prior(normal(2, 2), class = Intercept, resp = y),
                      prior(normal(0, 2), class = b, resp = y),

7.6 Shortcuts that fail, and multiple imputation

Before Bayesian imputation was easy, analysts filled holes in simpler ways, and many still do. Two are especially common.

  • Mean imputation replaces each missing value by the mean of the observed values. It invents data that lie on a single vertical line, which distorts every relationship involving the variable.
  • Single regression imputation replaces each missing value by its prediction from a regression on other variables. Better, but every imputed value lies exactly on the regression line, so the imputed data are less noisy than real data; relationships are exaggerated, and the analysis treats invented values as if they had been measured, so its standard errors are too small.

The fix for the second problem is to impute with noise, from a distribution, and to do it several times. Each completed data set gives an estimate and a standard error; the variation between the completed data sets is the extra uncertainty due to the missing values. This is multiple imputation, and its rules for combining the results are known as Rubin's rules: with M imputations giving estimates q^m with variances Um, report the average q¯=1M∑mq^m with variance

T=U¯+(1+1M)B,U¯=1M∑mUm,B=1M−1∑m(q^m−q¯)2.

The widget compares the four approaches in a setting where the truth is known: life expectancy regressed on log GDP, with some GDPs deleted.

Four ways to handle a missing predictor

Left: life expectancy against log GDP, with the GDPs of some countries deleted (missingness depends on life expectancy: countries with lower life expectancy lose their GDP more often, a MAR mechanism because life expectancy is observed). Open circles show the values filled in by the chosen method; faint crosses show the deleted true values. Right: the estimated slope and 95% interval from each method, against the slope from the full data (dashed). Multiple imputation uses 20 imputations from a Bayesian regression of log GDP on life expectancy, combined by Rubin's rules.

Repeat the deletion 200 times and the pattern is stark. Complete cases give a slope that is too small, because deleting countries by their life expectancy (the outcome here) distorts the relationship. Mean imputation gives a similarly biased slope with a standard error twice as large. Single regression imputation overshoots the slope and reports a standard error smaller than the full data would give: a precision it does not have. Multiple imputation recovers the full-data slope on average, with a standard error only slightly larger than the full data's, which is exactly right, since some information really was lost.

History: Rubin and Multiple Imputation

Donald Rubin's 1976 paper "Inference and missing data" in Biometrika gave the conditions, missing at random, under which the process that causes missing data can be ignored; the terms MCAR and MAR became standard through Statistical Analysis with Missing Data, his 1987 book with Roderick Little (MNAR, for the remaining case, came into common use later). In the late 1970s Rubin, then working on survey non-response with the US Census Bureau and the Social Security Administration, proposed multiple imputation as a way for the producers of public data sets to handle missing values once, properly, so that thousands of downstream users could analyse the completed files with ordinary software and still get honest standard errors. His 1987 book Multiple Imputation for Nonresponse in Surveys set out the combining rules. Rubin described multiple imputation as an approximation to a Bayesian analysis, and in a sense it is one: each imputed data set is a draw from the posterior predictive distribution of the missing values, and Rubin's rules approximate the law of total variance. A fully Bayesian model like m_mi does the imputing and the combining in one step.

In R, the mice package (van Buuren and Groothuis-Oudshoorn, 2011) implements multiple imputation by chained equations: each incomplete variable is imputed from all the others in turn, round and round until the imputations stabilise. It is the standard tool in epidemiology and the social sciences, and brms can fit a model to each completed data set and pool the draws with brm_multiple(). Use the joint Bayesian model when the imputation and analysis models are natural to write together, as here; use mice when there are many incomplete variables of different types, or when the imputed data will be shared with other analysts.

Warning: The Imputation Model Must Include the Outcome

A common mistake is to impute a missing predictor from the other predictors only, leaving out the outcome "to avoid circularity". This biases the estimated relationship between the predictor and the outcome toward zero, because the imputed values carry no information about the outcome. The imputation model should include every variable in the analysis model, including the outcome, plus any auxiliary variables that predict either the missing values or their missingness. In a joint Bayesian model this happens automatically: the outcome model is part of the posterior for each missing predictor, as the widget in Section 7.5 shows.

7.7 Missing not at random: sensitivity analysis

Everything so far assumed MAR: that among countries with the same reported deaths, median age and income, those with excess mortality data and those without had the same excess mortality on average. Is that plausible? There are reasons to doubt it in both directions. Countries whose health systems were overwhelmed may also have been the ones least able to register deaths, which would make the missing countries worse than the model predicts. On the other hand, much of the missing population lives in young, rural populations where transmission and mortality may have been lower than the observed relationships suggest.

The data cannot settle this, so a careful analysis does not pretend to. Instead, it asks how large a departure from MAR would change the conclusion. The simplest version is a delta adjustment: suppose that each missing country's excess death rate is δ per 1,000 higher than the model predicts, recompute the world total for a range of values of δ, and report how the conclusion depends on it. A value of δ=1 per 1,000 is a substantial departure: about the difference between the predicted excess rates of a country with median age 30 and one with median age 42.

R

         median = map_dbl(world, median),
         lo = map_dbl(world, quantile, .025), hi = map_dbl(world, quantile, .975)) |>

Research Practice: Report a Sensitivity Analysis for Missing Data

Guidelines for observational and clinical research (for example Sterne et al., 2009, in the BMJ, and the National Research Council's 2010 report on missing data in clinical trials) ask for the same things: the amount of missing data for each variable; the assumed mechanism and the reasons for believing it; the method of handling it, with the variables in the imputation model; and a sensitivity analysis to departures from MAR. Delta adjustment is the most widely used and the easiest to explain. More elaborate alternatives model the missingness mechanism directly (selection models) or let the distribution of the missing values differ by an unknown amount with its own prior (pattern-mixture models). Whatever you use, show the reader the value of δ (or its equivalent) at which your conclusion would change, and argue whether such a departure is plausible.

7.8 Measurement error: the same idea

A missing value is an extreme case of a measurement error: a measurement so noisy that it carries no information. Most measurements lie between perfect and absent, and the Bayesian treatment is the same throughout. Treat the true value as a parameter, give it a model, and let the noisy measurement inform it through a likelihood that says how noisy it is. Excess deaths are themselves estimates, with standard errors from the projection of expected deaths; reported COVID-19 deaths are a badly biased measurement of COVID-19 deaths; survey responses about income are noisy and sometimes rounded. In brms, an outcome measured with known standard error is written y | se(sd_y, sigma = TRUE) (the meta-analysis syntax of Chapter 6, with the residual variation kept), and a predictor measured with error gets its own formula, bf(x | mi(sd_x) ~ 1), and enters the outcome model as mi(x), which treats the observed value as a noisy measurement of the true one with standard deviation sd_x. Ignoring measurement error in a predictor biases its coefficient toward zero (the classic "regression dilution"), and the measurement-error model corrects that bias by propagating the uncertainty, just as imputation does.

7.9 Missing data in practice

Research Practice: A Checklist for Missing Data

  • Describe the missingness: for each variable, how many values are missing, and for which kinds of units. A table comparing units with and without missing values (like the left panel in Section 7.1) is often the most informative figure in the paper.
  • Draw the missingness indicators in the causal graph and argue for a mechanism. Collect and use auxiliary variables that predict missingness; they make MAR more plausible.
  • Do not use complete cases by default unless you have argued that it is unbiased for your estimand (for example, missing outcomes that are MAR given the predictors, for a regression coefficient). Never use mean imputation or single imputation for inference.
  • Impute with a model that includes the outcome and all analysis variables, either jointly (mi()) or by multiple imputation (mice, with at least 20 imputations, or more when much is missing).
  • Check imputations: compare the distributions of imputed and observed values, and check predictions on held-out observed data, especially in the direction of extrapolation.
  • Report a sensitivity analysis to departures from MAR, and the departure at which the conclusion would change.

Project Connections: Response Times and Surveys

In the New York emergency medical service data, timestamps are sometimes missing (a call with no recorded arrival time, a transport with no recorded hospital arrival), and the incidents most likely to be missing a timestamp may be the most chaotic and severe ones: a plausible MNAR mechanism. Record what predicts missingness (call type, borough, time of day, dispatcher workload), model the missing times with mi() or mice, and run a delta adjustment: how much longer would the missing response times have to be to change the conclusion about neighbourhood disparities? In the study of AI tools in university mathematics, survey non-response is the main missing-data problem, and it is unlikely to be ignorable: students who use AI tools heavily, or who are doing badly, may be less likely to answer. Course grades, attendance and section are natural auxiliary variables; use them in the imputation model, and report how the estimated association between AI use and grades changes under departures from MAR.

Key Takeaways: Lecture 14

  • A missing predictor becomes a parameter with an imputation model as its prior; in brms, bf(x | mi() ~ ...) and mi(x). It is informed both by its imputation model and, when the outcome is observed, by the outcome model.
  • Mean imputation and single imputation distort relationships and understate uncertainty. Multiple imputation with Rubin's rules, or a joint Bayesian model, propagates the uncertainty correctly.
  • The imputation model must include the outcome.
  • MAR is an assumption. A delta-adjustment sensitivity analysis shows how the conclusion depends on departures from it.
  • Missing data and measurement error are the same problem: an unknown true value with a model and a (possibly absent) noisy measurement.

  2. MCAR costs only precision. Using the 154 countries with both life expectancy and GDP, delete 40% of the life expectancies completely at random, 1,000 times.
