Chapter 2
Choosing causal graphs and priors

Lectures 3–4 · where graphs and priors come from, how to learn a graph from data, and how to assume as little as possible

Chapter 1 asked you to begin every analysis with two ingredients: a causal graph, saying what can affect what, and a set of priors, saying what is plausible before the data arrive. It quietly assumed you already had both. Usually you do, at least in part. An epidemiologist draws the graph she has taught for twenty years; a field biologist knows that penguins weigh a few kilograms. Most of the time, both ingredients come from domain knowledge, and the job is to write that knowledge down honestly.

But what if you know little? You may be handed a dataset with a dozen variables and no consensus diagram in any textbook. You may need a prior for a parameter whose plausible size you have never thought about. Making something up is acceptable if the something-up is principled, and this chapter is about the principles. It has two halves.

  • Lecture 3 is about graphs. Every graph makes predictions that data can check. When knowledge is thin, some of the graph can be learned from data by causal discovery algorithms, and we will run them on real measurements from thousands of human immune cells. We will also see, sharply, what observational data can never tell you, and what experiments add.
  • Lecture 4 is about priors. We will watch the choice of prior decide the answer in small real datasets, learn the principle of maximum entropy for assuming as little as possible, see why every prior lives on a particular scale, and set out what a publishable paper must report about its priors.

Think About It: Before You Start

Suppose you collected data on a thousand variables and found that two of them, A and B, are strongly correlated. List every causal story you can think of that would produce that correlation. How many of them could you rule out using more observational data on the same variables? Keep your list; Section 2.3 explains the answer.

Lecture 3 — Where graphs come from

2.1 Where graphs come from

A causal graph is a summary of scientific knowledge, so it comes from wherever scientific knowledge comes from. In practice, four sources do most of the work.

  1. Time order. Causes precede effects. A variable measured at birth cannot be caused by one measured at age forty. Time order alone orients a surprising number of arrows, and it is the most reliable knowledge you have.
  2. Design. If you assigned a treatment at random, nothing points into it (Section 1.6). If you selected units on some variable, that variable is conditioned on, whether you like it or not.
  3. Mechanism and literature. Biology, physics, institutional rules and previous studies tell you which effects are possible. Each arrow in a published graph should be traceable to one of these.
  4. Expert elicitation. When the literature is thin, structured interviews with people who know the system (clinicians, dispatchers, teachers) can fill gaps. Ask experts about missing arrows as well as present ones: "could A affect B by any route other than through C?"

A fifth source, data, is the subject of most of this lecture. It is the weakest of the five on its own, and the most useful as a check on the others.

History: A Network in a Single Cell

In 2005 Karen Sachs, Omar Perez, Dana Pe'er, Douglas Lauffenburger and Garry Nolan published a paper in Science that became the standard test case for causal discovery. Using flow cytometry, which measures the abundance of fluorescently tagged molecules inside individual cells as they stream past a laser, they recorded 11 signalling proteins and lipids in thousands of human T cells (white blood cells of the immune system). Crucially, some batches of cells were treated with chemicals that inhibit or activate specific molecules: experiments on the network. A Bayesian network learned from these data recovered most of the textbook signalling pathway, and made two predictions that the authors then tested by inhibiting the protein Erk: that Erk influences Akt, which the experiment confirmed, and that it has no direct influence on PKA, which was also borne out. The data have been reanalysed in hundreds of methodological papers since, and we will use them throughout this lecture.

2.2 A graph makes predictions you can test

A graph is not just a picture; it is a list of claims about the data. Every missing arrow implies that some pair of variables should be independent once the right other variables are held fixed. These implied conditional independencies follow from the blocking rules of Section 1.6, and they can be checked against data. If a predicted independence clearly fails, the graph is wrong somewhere, and the failure tells you where to look.

Let us check the textbook signalling network for the Sachs data. The sparsebn package distributes both the measurements and the consensus graph assembled from the biological literature. We use the 2,603 cells that received no targeted intervention.

The graph makes 68 testable predictions. Most hold well: about three-quarters of the predicted partial correlations are within 0.05 of zero, which for biological data is remarkably good. But one prediction fails badly. The graph says that Jnk and P38 should be independent once PKA and PKC are held fixed; in the data their partial correlation is 0.28, with a tight interval. With 2,603 cells, that is not noise.

The failure points somewhere specific. Jnk and P38 are both "stress-activated" kinases, and in the cell both are activated by enzymes upstream of them that were not measured in this experiment. An unmeasured common cause, U→Jnk and U→P38, would produce exactly this residual correlation. The test has not just said "the graph is wrong"; it has suggested the repair: add a latent common cause. That is the right attitude to these tests. They are diagnostics for the graph, as posterior predictive checks are diagnostics for the model.

Research Practice: Test Your Graph and Report It

Reviewers rarely ask for this, which is exactly why it impresses them. Before estimating anything, run localTests (or the equivalent for your data type) on your DAG and report how many implied independencies it has and how many clearly fail. Discuss each large failure: does it suggest a missing arrow or an unmeasured common cause, and would changing the graph change your adjustment set? Two cautions: with a large sample, tiny violations become "significant", so judge failures by the size of the correlation, not its p-value; and passing every test does not prove the graph right, because many different graphs make the same predictions (Section 2.3).

2.3 Fingerprints of structure, and what they cannot tell apart

If graphs make testable predictions, can we run the logic backwards and learn the graph from the independencies in the data? Partly. Start with three variables. Chapter 1 met the four elementary structures; here are the three that can connect X and Z through Y (the chain listed in both directions), with the independencies each one implies.

structuregraphX and Z marginallyX and Z given Y
chainX→Y→Zdependentindependent
reverse chainX←Y←Zdependentindependent
forkX←Y→Zdependentindependent
colliderX→Y←Zindependentdependent

Three different causal stories (two chains and a fork) leave exactly the same fingerprint in the data. No amount of observational data on X, Y and Z can tell them apart. They form a Markov equivalence class. The collider, by contrast, has a fingerprint of its own: independence that disappears when you condition. That asymmetry is the only directional information that observational data give for free, and every discovery algorithm is built on it.

Definition: Markov Equivalence

Two DAGs are Markov equivalent if they imply exactly the same set of conditional independencies. Verma and Pearl (1990) proved that this happens exactly when they have the same skeleton (the same pairs of variables joined, ignoring direction) and the same v-structures (colliders X→Y←Z whose two parents are not themselves joined).

An equivalence class is drawn as a CPDAG (completed partially directed acyclic graph): edges that point the same way in every member are drawn as arrows, and edges that differ are drawn without arrowheads.

Explore every DAG on three variables in the widget. Set the three edges, and it shows the independencies your graph implies and every other DAG that implies the same ones.

Markov equivalence on three variables

Choose each edge. The panel lists the conditional independencies the graph implies and draws its whole equivalence class: every DAG that no observational data could distinguish from yours.

Try the chain X→Y→Z: its class has three members. Now reverse one arrow to make the collider X→Y←Z: its class has one member, itself. Then join X and Z as well. With all three pairs joined, no independence is implied at all, and every acyclic orientation is equivalent: six DAGs that observational data cannot separate.

2.4 Learning a graph: the PC algorithm

The PC algorithm, named after its inventors Peter Spirtes and Clark Glymour, turns the fingerprints into a procedure. It assumes that every independence in the data comes from the graph's structure (an assumption called faithfulness, Section 2.6) and that there are no unmeasured common causes. It then works in three phases.

The PC algorithm

  1. Skeleton. Start with every pair of variables joined. For conditioning sets of size 0, then 1, then 2, and so on: if two joined variables are independent given some set of their neighbours, delete the edge, and record that set as their separating set.
  2. Colliders. For every pattern X−Y−Z where X and Z are not joined: if Y is not in the separating set of X and Z, orient it as a collider X→Y←Z. (If Y were a chain or fork node, conditioning on it would have been what separated X and Z.)
  3. Propagation. Orient any remaining edge whose direction is forced: if X→Y−Z with X and Z not joined, the edge must be Y→Z, because Y←Z would create a collider that phase 2 did not find. Repeat (these are Meek's rules), and avoid creating cycles.

The output is a CPDAG: the equivalence class, not a single graph.

Step through the algorithm on a six-variable graph. To see the logic clearly, the widget uses a perfect "oracle" for each independence test, read off the true graph. Real tests on finite data make mistakes, which we will meet in Section 2.5.

The PC algorithm, one step at a time

The true graph (left) is hidden from the algorithm, which sees only the answers to independence questions. The right panel shows its current guess. Press next step to run one test or one orientation.

Notice three things. First, the algorithm never asks more than a handful of questions per edge, because it only conditions on current neighbours; that is what makes it fast enough for hundreds of variables. Second, the single collider at D orients not just its own two edges but, through propagation, every edge downstream of it. Third, the edge A−B is left undirected. Both A→B and A←B produce exactly the same independencies, so the algorithm honestly reports that the data cannot decide.

The PC algorithm is constraint-based: it builds the graph from yes/no answers to independence tests. The other main family is score-based. These algorithms define a score for each candidate graph, such as the Bayesian information criterion or the marginal likelihood of the data under the graph, and search for the highest-scoring graph, typically by adding, removing and reversing one arrow at a time (hill climbing). The fully Bayesian version treats the graph itself as unknown, puts a prior on all graphs, and returns a posterior distribution over graphs, from which you can read the probability that each edge exists. That is the most honest output a discovery method can give, but the number of DAGs grows super-exponentially (there are about 4×1018 DAGs on 10 nodes), so exact posteriors are possible only for small problems, and approximations use MCMC over graphs or, as we will do next, the bootstrap.

2.5 Discovery on real cells

Now the real test. Run PC on the 2,603 observational cells, and compare its output with the consensus graph. A short helper counts how many consensus edges were found, how many learned edges are not in the consensus, and how many were oriented each way.

R
edge_key <- function(a, b) paste(pmin(a, b), pmax(a, b))

score_graph <- function(g) {
  a <- as_tibble(arcs(g))
  undirected <- filter(a, paste(to, from) %in% paste(a$from, a$to))
  directed   <- anti_join(a, undirected, by = c("from", "to"))
  learned <- unique(edge_key(a$from, a$to))
  truth   <- edge_key(consensus$from, consensus$to)
  tibble(edges     = length(learned),
         found     = sum(truth %in% learned),          # of 20 consensus edges
         extra     = sum(!learned %in% truth),
         right_way = sum(paste(directed$from, directed$to) %in% paste(consensus$from, consensus$to)),
         reversed  = sum(paste(directed$to, directed$from) %in% paste(consensus$from, consensus$to)),
         undirected = nrow(undirected) / 2)
}

pc_obs <- pc.stable(as.data.frame(observational), alpha = 0.01)   # Gaussian tests
hc_obs <- hc(as.data.frame(observational))                          # hill climbing, BIC score
bind_rows(PC = score_graph(pc_obs), `hill climbing` = score_graph(hc_obs), .id = "method")
output

The results are sobering. From 2,603 cells, PC recovers only 6 of the 20 consensus edges, adds 5 that are not in the consensus, and orients just 2 correctly. Hill climbing finds a few more edges, 7 of 20, but also 8 extras. These are not bad algorithms. They are honest about how little observational data can say: signalling relationships are noisy and nonlinear, the Gaussian tests are approximations, and many of the true edges belong to equivalence classes whose directions no observational method could recover.

Sachs and colleagues had something better than more cells. They had experiments. In some batches a chemical blocked or stimulated a particular molecule, which in graph terms means its incoming arrows were cut (Section 2.6). A molecule whose level was set by the experimenter cannot be caused by its usual parents in those cells, and that breaks the symmetries that make equivalence classes. The bnlearn package can use this information with the modified Bayesian Dirichlet equivalent (mbde) score, which ignores, for each molecule, the cells in which it was manipulated. Following Sachs et al., this score works with the data discretised into three levels (low, medium, high).

Look also at what the methods get wrong, because one mistake is shared by all of them. Every method in the widget, with or without the experiments, adds an edge between Jnk and P38 that the consensus graph does not have. That is exactly the pair whose predicted independence failed in Section 2.2. A learning algorithm that assumes no hidden variables has only one way to explain a residual correlation, which is to draw an arrow. Two different lines of evidence point to the same gap in the textbook graph, and the most plausible repair is the one suggested earlier: an unmeasured upstream activator of both stress kinases.

Research Practice: Presenting a Discovered Graph

Causal discovery is publishable when it is presented as what it is: a disciplined way to generate and check hypotheses. Good practice in a paper:

  • State the algorithm, the independence test or score, and every tuning choice (such as alpha), and show how the graph changes when they change.
  • Report edge stability, for example bootstrap edge strengths or posterior edge probabilities, not a single graph.
  • Encode what you know before learning: bnlearn accepts a blacklist of forbidden arrows (for instance, anything pointing backwards in time) and a whitelist of required ones.
  • Never present a learned graph as established; present it as the graph the data favour under stated assumptions, and test its new predictions on new data or by experiment, as Sachs and colleagues did.

Project Connection: Cancer Gene-Expression Subtypes

Gene-expression studies measure thousands of genes in hundreds of tumours. Nobody can write the full graph by hand, so structure learning is not a check but a starting point. The lessons of this section transfer directly: discovery from observational expression data alone will orient few edges reliably; perturbation data (CRISPR knockouts, drug screens) are what make directions identifiable; and with far more variables than samples, the bootstrap stability of each edge matters more than the single best graph.

2.6 The limits of discovery, and how to push them

Three assumptions sit under every result in this lecture, and a careful reader of a discovery paper checks each one.

Faithfulness. PC assumes that every independence in the data comes from the graph's structure, never from effects that happen to cancel out. Suppose exercise lowers blood pressure directly but also raises appetite, which raises blood pressure by exactly the same amount. Exercise and blood pressure would then be independent even though exercise causes blood pressure along two paths. Exact cancellation is unlikely (for parameters drawn at random it has probability zero), but near-cancellation in finite samples is not, and systems that are regulated to stay stable, like body temperature or blood sugar, cancel by design.

No unmeasured common causes. PC and hill climbing assume every common cause of two measured variables is itself measured. Section 2.2 showed this failing for Jnk and P38. The FCI algorithm (Fast Causal Inference) relaxes the assumption: it returns a graph with marks meaning "this may be due to a hidden common cause", at the price of orienting even fewer edges.

Observational data identify only the equivalence class. Three escapes exist, each buying more structure with more assumptions:

  • Interventions set a variable from outside, deleting the arrows into it. Section 2.5 showed what they can do.
  • Time order forbids arrows from later variables to earlier ones; encode it as a blacklist.
  • Functional assumptions. If effects are linear and the noise is non-Gaussian, the direction of every edge is identifiable from observational data (the LiNGAM method of Shimizu and colleagues, 2006). Related "additive noise" methods use nonlinearity. Intuitively, if Y=f(X)+ε with ε independent of X, then usually no model X=g(Y)+η with η independent of Y exists, so the residuals reveal the direction.
Why do interventions orient edges? The do-operator in one paragraph

An intervention that sets X to a value x, written do(X=x), replaces the mechanism that normally produces X with a constant. In the graph, every arrow into X is deleted, while arrows out of X remain. Now consider two equivalent graphs, X→Y and X←Y. Under the first, setting X changes the distribution of Y; under the second, it does not, because the arrow from Y into X has been cut and nothing connects them. One experiment distinguishes what no quantity of observation could. This is why randomised trials (which are interventions on the treatment) are the gold standard of causal inference, and why Chapter 13 builds its whole theory around the do-operator.

Key Takeaways: Lecture 3

  • Graphs come from time order, design, mechanism and experts; data check them through implied conditional independencies.
  • Observational data identify a graph only up to its Markov equivalence class; colliders are the source of all directional information.
  • The PC algorithm learns the skeleton by independence tests, orients colliders, and propagates. Score-based and Bayesian methods search over graphs instead.
  • On real data, observational discovery is weak; interventions, time order and functional assumptions are what make directions identifiable.

Lecture 4 — Where priors come from

2.7 When the prior decides the answer

In the vaccine analysis of Chapter 1, 170 cases overwhelmed every reasonable prior. That is the usual situation for a headline result. It is not the usual situation for everything else in a paper: subgroups, rare outcomes, small pilot studies, parameters that the data barely inform. Here the choice of prior can decide the answer.

The FDA's briefing document on the same trial (FDA, 2020) reported efficacy within subgroups. Some subgroups had very few cases. Among participants aged 75 and over there were 0 cases in the vaccine arm and 5 in the placebo arm. Among Asian participants, 1 and 4. Among Black or African American participants, 0 and 7. The frequentist 95% confidence interval for VE in the oldest group ran from −12.1% to 100%: the data were consistent with a vaccine that was useless in the elderly.

We can analyse each subgroup exactly as in Section 1.2, under five priors on θ (the share of cases in the vaccine arm):

  • flat, Beta(1, 1): every value of θ equally plausible;
  • Jeffreys, Beta(0.5, 0.5): the "reference" prior of Section 2.9;
  • protocol, Beta(0.7, 1): the trial's own prior;
  • sceptical, Beta(5, 5): centred on no effect and worth 10 cases;
  • rest of trial: centred on the efficacy seen in everyone outside the subgroup, but deliberately down-weighted to be worth only 10 cases.
R
ve <- function(theta) (1 - 2 * theta) / (1 - theta)
subgroups <- tribble(
  ~group,                       ~vax, ~pbo, ~fda_lo, ~fda_hi,
  "Age 75 and over",               0,    5,  -0.121,   1.000,
  "Asian",                         1,    4,  -1.587,   0.995,
  "Black or African American",     0,    7,   0.304,   1.000,
  "Age 65 to 74",                  1,   14,   0.532,   0.998)
all_vax <- 9; all_pbo <- 169          # whole trial, same analysis population

priors_for <- function(v, p) {
  rest <- (all_vax - v) / (all_vax + all_pbo - v - p)   # theta outside the subgroup
  tribble(~prior,                       ~a,               ~b,
          "flat Beta(1, 1)",              1,                1,
          "Jeffreys Beta(0.5, 0.5)",      0.5,              0.5,
          "protocol Beta(0.7, 1)",        0.700102,         1,
          "sceptical Beta(5, 5)",         5,                5,
          "rest of trial (10 cases)",     10 * rest,        10 * (1 - rest))
}

post <- subgroups |>
  mutate(priors = map2(vax, pbo, priors_for)) |>
  unnest(priors) |>
  mutate(median = ve(qbeta(0.5,   a + vax, b + pbo)),
         lo     = ve(qbeta(0.975, a + vax, b + pbo)),     # VE falls as theta rises
         hi     = ve(qbeta(0.025, a + vax, b + pbo)),
         p_30   = pbeta(0.7 / 1.7, a + vax, b + pbo))     # Pr(VE > 30%)

post |> filter(group == "Age 75 and over") |>
  select(prior, median, lo, hi, p_30) |> mutate(across(where(is.numeric), \(x) round(x, 2)))

Research Practice: The Prior Sensitivity Analysis

A publishable Bayesian paper shows how its main conclusions depend on the priors. The standard pattern, recommended in the BARG (Kruschke, 2021) and the WAMBS checklist (Depaoli and van de Schoot, 2017), is:

  1. a primary analysis with priors justified in the methods section (cite the sources of any informative prior);
  2. re-analyses with a vaguer prior and with a sceptical prior centred on no effect;
  3. a table or figure, like the one above, showing the key posterior summaries under each;
  4. a sentence stating whether the substantive conclusion survives. "The conclusion that the effect exceeds the minimal important difference holds under all priors considered" is exactly what a referee wants to read.

The priorsense R package automates a version of this by "power-scaling" the prior and likelihood and flagging parameters whose posteriors are sensitive to either.

2.8 Maximum entropy: assuming as little as possible

Suppose you know something about a quantity, such as its possible values and its average, but nothing more. Which distribution expresses exactly that knowledge, and no more? The answer, developed by the physicist Edwin Jaynes from Claude Shannon's theory of information, is: the distribution with the largest entropy among all those that satisfy what you know.

Definition: Entropy

The entropy of a discrete distribution p=(p1,…,pK) is

H(p)=−∑k=1Kpklog⁡pk,

with 0log⁡0=0. For a density p(x), the sum becomes an integral, H(p)=−∫p(x)log⁡p(x)dx. Entropy measures how spread out, or how unpredictable, a distribution is: it is 0 when one outcome is certain and largest when all outcomes are equally likely.

Why maximise it? Here is the intuition, due to Graham Wallis and retold by Jaynes and McElreath. Imagine distributing N pebbles at random among K buckets, each pebble equally likely to land in any bucket. Some arrangements of counts can happen in many more ways than others. The number of ways to obtain counts (n1,…,nK) is the multinomial coefficient W=N!/(n1!⋯nK!), and for large N,

1Nlog⁡W≈−∑knkNlog⁡nkN=H(nN).

So the distribution with the highest entropy is the one that can happen in the most ways. Choosing it means choosing the outcome that is most likely if nothing beyond your constraints is pushing the pebbles around. Any other choice smuggles in extra structure that you have not justified.

Counting the ways: pebbles in buckets

Ten pebbles go into five buckets. Move pebbles between buckets with the buttons, or try the presets. The readout counts the number of ways W each arrangement can happen.

Jaynes's dice

Jaynes illustrated the principle with a puzzle. A six-sided die is rolled many times and you are told only that the average score was 4.5, not the fair 3.5. What probability should you give each face? Infinitely many distributions have mean 4.5. The maximum-entropy one solves

maxp−∑k=16pklog⁡pksubject to∑kpk=1,∑kkpk=4.5.
Solving it with Lagrange multipliers

Introduce a multiplier for each constraint and set the derivative of the Lagrangian with respect to each pk to zero:

∂∂pk[−∑jpjlog⁡pj+λ0(∑jpj−1)+λ(∑jjpj−4.5)]=−log⁡pk−1+λ0+λk=0.

So log⁡pk is linear in k, and pk∝eλk. The constant of proportionality makes the probabilities sum to 1, and λ is chosen so that the mean is 4.5. The same calculation with any constraints of the form E[fj(X)]=cj gives p(x)∝exp⁡(∑jλjfj(x)), which is the general theorem stated below.

R
faces <- 1:6
mean_given_lambda <- function(lambda) {
  p <- exp(lambda * faces); p <- p / sum(p)
  sum(faces * p)
}
lambda <- uniroot(\(l) mean_given_lambda(l) - 4.5, c(-5, 5))$root
p_maxent <- exp(lambda * faces) / sum(exp(lambda * faces))
round(p_maxent, 4)

entropy <- function(p) -sum(ifelse(p > 0, p * log(p), 0))
c(maxent = entropy(p_maxent),
  `only 4s and 5s` = entropy(c(0, 0, 0, 0.5, 0.5, 0)),
  `3s and 6s` = entropy(c(0, 0, 0.5, 0, 0, 0.5)),
  fair_die = entropy(rep(1/6, 6)))
output

The alternatives with the same mean, such as "only fours and fives", have far lower entropy, because they assert things (no sixes ever!) that the information given does not support. Result: Maximum Entropy and the Exponential Family

Among all distributions on a given set of values with fixed expectations E[fj(X)]=cj for j=1,…,m, the one with maximum entropy (when it exists) has the form

p(x)∝exp⁡(∑j=1mλjfj(x)),

with the λj chosen to satisfy the constraints. These are exactly the exponential families of distributions.

Almost every distribution you will use in this course is the maximum-entropy distribution for some simple state of knowledge.

what you knowmaximum-entropy distribution
one of K values, nothing elseuniform on the K values
a value in an interval [a,b]uniform on [a,b]
a positive value with known meanexponential
a real value with known mean and variancenormal
a positive value with known E[X] and E[log⁡X]gamma
a positive value with known mean and variance of log⁡Xlognormal
a proportion with known E[log⁡X] and E[log⁡(1−X)]beta
a count 0,1,2,… with known meangeometric
successes in n independent trials with constant expected countbinomial

This changes how to think about the normal distribution. It is not used because nature is normal (often it isn't). It is used because if all you are prepared to say about a quantity is its centre and spread, the normal is the distribution that says nothing more. A quick numerical check makes the point: among several familiar distributions with the same variance, the normal has the largest entropy.

# differential entropies of distributions with variance 1

  logistic = 2 + log(sqrt(3) / pi),          # scale s = sqrt(3)/pi
  Laplace  = 1 + log(2 / sqrt(2)),           # scale b = 1/sqrt(2)
  uniform  = log(sqrt(12)))                  # width sqrt(12)

The same logic applies to likelihoods, which is why Chapter 4 will choose binomial, Poisson and gamma likelihoods by asking what we know about the outcome rather than by habit. And it applies to priors: if all you will commit to is that a standard deviation is positive with some typical size, the exponential distribution is the honest prior, and that is why σ∼Exponential(1) appears throughout these notes.

History: From Steam Engines to Statistics

Entropy began in nineteenth-century thermodynamics. Ludwig Boltzmann's formula S=klog⁡W, engraved on his tombstone in Vienna, counts the ways W a physical system can be arranged: exactly the pebble-counting of this section. In 1948 Claude Shannon, an engineer at Bell Labs, needed a measure of the information in a message and arrived at −∑plog⁡p; John von Neumann reportedly advised him to call it entropy because "nobody knows what entropy really is, so in a debate you will always have the advantage". In 1957 E. T. Jaynes turned the idea around: statistical mechanics, he argued, is not physics at all but inference, choosing the distribution of maximum entropy given what is measured. His posthumous book Probability Theory: The Logic of Science (2003) remains the most forceful argument for Bayesian reasoning ever written.

2.9 Every prior lives on a scale

Maximum entropy has a catch, and it is the same catch that made the vaccine prior look strange in Section 1.2. A prior that is "flat" or "uninformative" on one scale is not flat on another. A flat prior on θ, the share of cases in the vaccine arm, is anything but flat on VE.

R
set.seed(5)
theta <- runif(1e5)
tibble(`theta (share of cases in vaccine arm)` = theta,
       `VE = (1 - 2 theta) / (1 - theta)`   = ve(theta)) |>
  pivot_longer(everything()) |>
  mutate(name = fct_inorder(name)) |>
  filter(value > -3) |>
  ggplot(aes(value)) +
  geom_histogram(bins = 60, fill = harbour[["sky"]], colour = NA) +
  facet_wrap(~ name, scales = "free") +
  labs(x = NULL, y = "draws")
mean(ve(theta) > 0.3)          # prior probability that VE exceeds 30%
output
[1] 0.4171

A "flat" prior on θ puts 42% of its weight on VE above 30%, a density that rises steadily towards perfect efficacy, and fully half its weight on a vaccine that does harm (VE below 0), spread along a tail that reaches minus infinity. Had we instead put a flat prior on VE between −1 and 1, the implied prior on θ would have been far from flat. There is no scale-free way to be ignorant.

Two responses to this problem are worth knowing. The first, due to Harold Jeffreys (1946), is to choose priors that give the same answer whichever parameterisation you use. For the binomial, the Jeffreys prior is Beta(0.5, 0.5), the prior used in Section 2.7. It has an attractive mathematical property (invariance under reparameterisation), but it is often improper or odd-looking in more complex models, and it is rarely used for regression coefficients.

The second response, which is the one this course takes, is practical: choose the scale on which you have knowledge, and put the prior there. You probably know more about efficacy than about θ, and more about a slope in kilograms per millimetre than about a slope in grams per micrometre. Then check, by prior predictive simulation, that the implied prior on everything else you care about is reasonable. Maximum entropy tells you what shape to use on a given scale; the scale itself is a modelling decision you must justify.

Warning: Non-Linear Transformations Move Prior Mass

Logistic regression (Chapter 4) puts priors on log-odds. A "vague" Normal(0, 10) prior on a log-odds intercept implies that about two-thirds of the time the probability is within 0.01 of 0 or 1 (Exercise 7): the opposite of vague. Always look at the implied prior on the scale of the outcome.

2.10 Weakly informative priors in practice

Between "flat" and "informative" lies the default of modern applied work: weakly informative priors. They rule out the absurd, gently pull estimates towards plausible values, and let the data decide everything else. Three habits make them easy to choose.

  1. Put variables on interpretable scales. Centre predictors at meaningful values (Section 1.7 centred flipper length at 200 mm). Or standardise outcome and predictors to mean 0 and standard deviation 1, after which a slope of 1 means a one-standard-deviation change in the outcome per standard deviation of the predictor, which is an enormous effect in most fields. Then Normal(0, 1), or Normal(0, 0.5), is a sensible weakly informative prior for every slope.
  2. Use maximum-entropy shapes on that scale: normal for locations and slopes, exponential or half-normal for standard deviations, and (Chapter 6) the LKJ distribution for correlation matrices.
  3. Check by prior predictive simulation, as in Section 1.7, but now let brms do it.

First, see what priors brms would use if you said nothing. It chooses its defaults from the data, which is convenient but means they are not really priors in the strict sense.

The defaults put flat, improper priors on the slopes, marked (flat), and Student-t priors centred and scaled using the data for the intercept and σ. So state your own priors and simulate from them alone, with sample_prior = "only", which runs the sampler while ignoring the likelihood.

R
m_prior <- brm(
  mass ~ 1 + flipper + species + sex, data = pen,
  prior = c(prior(normal(4, 1), class = Intercept),
            prior(normal(0, 1), class = b),
            prior(normal(0, 0.05), class = b, coef = flipper),
            prior(exponential(1), class = sigma)),
  sample_prior = "only",                       # ignore the data: simulate from the prior
  seed = 1, refresh = 0, file = "fits/ch2_prior_only"
)
# pp_check(m_prior) draws this plot in one line; here we build it by hand
set.seed(2)

Research Practice: Writing the Priors Paragraph

A methods section that reviewers will not query states, for every parameter, the prior and its justification, and points to the prior predictive check. For example:

"Body mass (kg) was modelled as normally distributed with mean linear in flipper length (centred at 200 mm), species and sex. The intercept had a Normal(4, 1) prior, reflecting published masses of 3–6 kg for these species; the flipper slope a Normal(0, 0.05) prior, allowing slopes of up to about ±0.1 kg per mm; other coefficients Normal(0, 1); and the residual standard deviation an Exponential(1) prior. Prior predictive simulations (Supplementary Figure S1) confirmed that these priors permit all biologically plausible masses while excluding impossible ones. Results under alternative priors are in Supplementary Table S2."

That paragraph names the scale, gives every prior, justifies each from outside the data, shows the check, and points to the sensitivity analysis. It is the whole of Lecture 4 in four sentences.

Normal(0, 1)

Flat priors are improper, so they cannot be simulated from and no prior predictive check is possible; they assert that absurd effect sizes are as plausible as sensible ones; and they are chosen by software rather than justified by knowledge, which a referee may reasonably question. Stating proper weakly informative priors costs nothing when data are plentiful and protects the analysis when they are not.

Key Takeaways: Lecture 4

  • With little data, the prior decides the answer. Report several priors and say which question each answers.
  • Maximum entropy chooses the distribution that assumes nothing beyond what you know; it explains the uniform, exponential, normal, binomial and gamma.
  • No prior is neutral on every scale. Put priors on the scale where you have knowledge, then check the implied priors by simulation.
  • In practice: interpretable scales, maximum-entropy shapes, weakly informative widths, prior predictive checks, sensitivity analysis.
