Chapter 14
Experiments, adaptive trials and decisions

Lectures 31–34 · A/B tests at scale, sequential and adaptive trials, decisions under uncertainty, and Bayesian answers with frequentist guarantees, including anytime-valid tests and confidence sequences

Chapter 13 ended with a lesson that every causal analysis eventually learns: when it can be done, randomise. A randomised experiment makes assignment ignorable by design, so the hardest assumption of observational work is simply true. This chapter is about what comes next, which is where most applied Bayesian work in industry, medicine and economics actually lives. Experiments are run in their thousands by technology companies, which call them A/B tests; they are monitored while they run, stopped early, and adapted as evidence arrives, as in modern platform trials of treatments for COVID-19; and they exist to inform decisions, which need more than a posterior: they need consequences.

The chapter also takes up an old argument. Regulators, journal editors and many statisticians judge procedures by their long-run error rates: how often a trial declares a useless drug effective. Bayesian posteriors make no such promise on their own. The last lecture shows that the two views can be reconciled, and that a Bayesian design can be tuned, by simulation or exact calculation, to have whatever frequentist guarantees are required, as the most famous vaccine trial of our time was.

Think About It: Before You Start

A news site tests two headlines for the same story on 3,000 readers each. Headline B gets 47 clicks and headline A gets 36. The analytics dashboard reports that B is better with probability 0.90. Before switching every reader to B, what would you want to know? Consider how many other tests the site has run, how large the differences in those tests turned out to be, how often the dashboard was checked during the test, and what it costs to choose wrongly. Each lecture in this chapter turns one of these considerations into a calculation.

Lecture 31 — A/B tests at scale

14.1 Twenty-seven thousand experiments

Between 2013 and 2015 the news site Upworthy ran an experiment for almost every story it published. Editors wrote several "packages" (a headline, sometimes with a different image) and the site showed each package to a random subset of visitors, counting impressions and clicks; the package with the most clicks became the story's headline. In 2021 J. Nathan Matias and colleagues released the complete record as the Upworthy Research Archive: 32,487 experiments, published in Scientific Data under an open licence (Matias et al., 2021). The archive was split by its creators into an exploratory sample, for developing ideas, and a larger confirmatory sample, held back so that ideas could be tested on data that did not suggest them. We follow the same discipline: we fit models on the exploratory experiments and check them on the confirmatory ones.

Each experiment compares several packages, usually four or five. To keep the analysis simple we take two packages at random from each experiment, call them A and B, and summarise the comparison by the difference in log-odds of a click, d^=logit(p^B)−logit(p^A), with its usual standard error s=1/cA+1/(nA−cA)+1/cB+1/(nB−cB), where n counts impressions and c clicks. A difference of 0.1 in log-odds is roughly a 10% relative difference in the click rate.

14.2 How large are the effects?

An observed difference d^k is the true difference δk plus noise of known size, d^k∼normal(δk,sk). With thousands of experiments, the true differences themselves have a distribution, δk∼G, and estimating G is a multilevel model of the kind built in Chapter 6, with experiments as groups. Its shape matters as much as its spread. If G is normal, big differences between headlines are vanishingly rare; if it has heavy tails, most headlines are about equally good but a few are far better or worse. We fit two versions: a normal G with standard deviation σ, and a Student-t G with scale σ and degrees of freedom ν, where small ν means heavy tails.

Because only the two parameters of G are of interest, we integrate out the 4,868 true differences instead of sampling them: the marginal density of each observed difference, p(d^k∣σ,ν)=∫normal(d^k∣δ,sk)G(δ∣σ,ν)dδ, is computed on a fine grid of δ values. (Sampling the latent differences directly works for the normal model, but for the heavy-tailed one HMC mixes badly, a reminder from Chapter 5 that how a model is written can matter as much as what it says.)

R
deconv_code <- "
data { int<lower=1> K; vector[K] d; vector<lower=0>[K] se; int<lower=1> J; vector[J] grid; int<lower=0, upper=1> heavy; }
transformed data {
  real h = grid[2] - grid[1]; matrix[K, J] kern;                    // kern[k, j] = normal(d_k | grid_j, se_k)
  for (k in 1:K) for (j in 1:J) kern[k, j] = exp(normal_lpdf(d[k] | grid[j], se[k]));
}
parameters { real<lower=0> sigma; real<lower=1, upper=100> nu; }
transformed parameters {
  vector[J] g;                                                        // G on the grid, times the grid spacing
  for (j in 1:J) g[j] = exp((heavy ? student_t_lpdf(grid[j] | nu, 0, sigma) : normal_lpdf(grid[j] | 0, sigma)) + log(h));
}
model {
  sigma ~ normal(0, 0.5); nu ~ gamma(2, 0.1);
  target += sum(log(kern * g));                                      // log of the marginal density of each d_k
}"
deconv_model <- stan_model(model_code = deconv_code)
grid <- seq(-2.5, 2.5, by = 0.02)

                chains = 4, iter = 1000, seed = 14, refresh = 0, pars = c("sigma", "nu"))

Both models fit cleanly (R^=1.00). The normal model puts the standard deviation of true differences at σ=0.30, so a typical pair of headlines differs by about 0.3 in log-odds, a click rate roughly a third higher for one than the other. The heavy-tailed model has a smaller scale, σ=0.23, and ν=4.7 degrees of freedom (95% interval 3.9 to 6.0): most pairs of headlines are closer together than the normal model says, and a few are much further apart. A Student-t with about five degrees of freedom has tails far heavier than a normal, with a variance that only just exists.

The confirmatory experiments are a fair test of the two shapes, because they played no part in fitting them. For each confirmatory comparison we compute the predictive density of the observed difference under each fitted G, averaging over the posterior of σ and ν, and compare the totals, exactly as LOO did in earlier chapters but with genuinely new data.

pred_logdens <- function(fit, heavy, data, draws = 100) {
  dr <- as_draws_df(fit)[round(seq(1, 2000, length.out = draws)), ]
  logk <- dnorm(outer(data$d, grid, "-"), 0, data$se, log = TRUE)
  dens <- sapply(seq_len(draws), function(i) {
    lg <- if (heavy) dt(grid / dr$sigma[i], dr$nu[i], log = TRUE) - log(dr$sigma[i]) else dnorm(grid, 0, dr$sigma[i], log = TRUE)
    m <- logk + matrix(lg + log(0.02), nrow(logk), length(grid), byrow = TRUE)
    mx <- apply(m, 1, max); mx + log(rowSums(exp(m - mx))) })
  mx <- apply(dens, 1, max); mx + log(rowMeans(exp(dens - mx)))       # log of the posterior-averaged density
}

14.3 Shrinkage and the winner's curse

Every experiment on the archive produced a winner, and the winner's observed advantage over the loser is a biased estimate of its true advantage. The reason is selection: among packages whose true click rates are similar, the one that wins is usually the one that was lucky, so the winning margin includes the luck. This is the winner's curse, and it has the same cure as the extreme cancer rates of Chapter 6: shrink each estimate toward what is typical, by an amount that depends on how noisy it is. With G estimated from thousands of experiments, the posterior mean of each δk is

𝔼[δk∣d^k]=∫δnormal(d^k∣δ,sk)G(δ)dδ∫normal(d^k∣δ,sk)G(δ)dδ,

and its shape depends on G. A normal G shrinks every estimate by the same proportion. A heavy-tailed G shrinks small estimates hard, since they are probably noise around a typical effect near zero, and leaves large estimates almost alone, since heavy tails make large true effects believable. The widget shows both.

post_mean <- function(d, se, heavy) {                          # E[delta | d] on the grid, at the posterior means of G
  lg <- if (heavy) dt(grid / sH, nH, log = TRUE) - log(sH) else dnorm(grid, 0, sN, log = TRUE)
  w <- exp(dnorm(outer(d, grid, "-"), 0, se, log = TRUE) + matrix(lg, length(d), length(grid), byrow = TRUE))
  as.vector(w %*% grid) / rowSums(w) }

14.4 How many headlines should we test?

The shape of G is not an academic detail; it changes how experiments should be run. Suppose an editor has a fixed number of visitors to spend on testing a story's headline, writes m candidate headlines, shows each to an equal share of the visitors, and then uses the one with the highest posterior mean for everyone else. More headlines give more chances of finding an exceptional one; fewer headlines give each a larger sample, so the winner is picked more reliably. The best m balances the two, and the balance depends on the tails of G.

R
set.seed(14); p <- median(ex$ctr)
best_of <- function(m, visitors, draw, R = 40000) {
  se <- sqrt(1 / (visitors / m * p * (1 - p)))                 # standard error of one headline's log-odds
  D <- matrix(draw(R * m), R, m); O <- D + matrix(rnorm(R * m, 0, se), R, m)
  mean(D[cbind(1:R, max.col(O))]) }                            # true value of the headline that looked best
draws <- list(normal = function(k) rnorm(k, 0, sN), `Student-t` = function(k) sH * rt(k, nH))

  mutate(gain = pmap_dbl(list(m, visitors, G), function(m, v, g) best_of(m, v, draws[[g]])))

Upworthy's editors tested four or five headlines per story; on these estimates, they could have gained by writing more.

Paper in Focus: A/B Testing with Fat Tails

The paper. Eduardo Azevedo, Alex Deng, José Luis Montiel Olea, Justin Rao and E. Glen Weyl, "A/B testing with fat tails", Journal of Political Economy 128(12), 2020. The authors had access to thousands of experiments run on Microsoft's Bing search engine and asked an economist's question: given a fixed amount of traffic, should a company run a few large experiments or many small ones?

The argument. The answer depends on the distribution of true effects across ideas. If it is thin-tailed, the gains come from detecting many small improvements, which needs large samples, so a company should run fewer, bigger experiments. If it is fat-tailed, most of the value lies in rare large effects, which are easy to detect even in small samples, so the company should run many small experiments, a "lean" strategy, and adopt only clear winners. They estimate the tail of the distribution from the experiments themselves (an empirical Bayes deconvolution, as in Section 14.2) and find fat tails at Bing, implying that more, smaller experiments would pay.

Reading it. Identify the estimand (the distribution of effects across ideas, not any single effect), the key assumption (that past experiments are exchangeable with future ones), and the decision model (what is gained by adopting an idea, and what is lost by testing it). We cannot use Bing's data, but Sections 14.2 to 14.4 reproduce the logic on Upworthy's: the tails are heavy, and heavy tails favour testing more headlines on fewer visitors each. Then ask what the paper does not show: whether the fitted tail is driven by a few unusual experiments, and whether exchangeability survives changes in a company's product over time. The Upworthy archive's confirmatory sample lets us check the first.

Check Yourself

A company reports that the 50 experiments it shipped last year had an average measured improvement of 4%, and that revenue therefore grew by about 200%. Which statement is right?

The measured improvements of winners are selected for being large.

Randomisation protects each estimate from confounding, not from selection.

Shipping is a selection on the measured effect, so the shipped experiments' measurements are biased upward, the more so the noisier they were. A hierarchical model of all experiments, shipped or not, estimates how much to shrink. Companies that compare a holdback group of users who never received any shipped change with everyone else typically find that the realised total is a fraction of the sum of measured effects.

The measured improvements of shipped experiments are inflated by the winner's curse, because experiments were shipped because they measured well; shrinking each toward the distribution of effects across all experiments (and checking with holdback groups) gives a more honest total, which is usually much smaller.

Key Takeaways: Lecture 31

  • Many experiments make a multilevel model: observed differences are true differences plus known noise, and the true differences have a distribution G that can be estimated from the experiments themselves.
  • The shape of G matters. In the Upworthy experiments it has heavy tails (ν≈4.7), and a held-out confirmatory sample preferred the heavy-tailed fit decisively.
  • Winning margins are inflated by selection (the winner's curse); shrinkage by the posterior under G corrects them, and a heavy-tailed G shrinks small effects hard and large ones little.
  • Decisions about how to experiment depend on G: with fat tails, test more ideas on less traffic each (Azevedo et al., 2020).

Lecture 32 — Sequential and adaptive trials

14.5 The peeking problem

An A/B test on a website accumulates data continuously, and the dashboard shows the current result at every moment. It is very tempting to stop as soon as the result looks decisive. A classical significance test is not designed for this. It controls the false-positive rate at 5% for one look at a pre-specified sample size; look twenty times and stop at the first |z|>1.96, and a useless change will be declared a winner far more often than 5% of the time. The same is true of a naive Bayesian rule that stops when the posterior probability that B is better exceeds 0.975. The simulation below runs 20,000 tests in which the two versions are identical, checking after each of L equal batches of visitors.

R
set.seed(14)
run_mean <- function(inc) {                                    # running mean of each row's batch estimates
  cs <- inc; if (ncol(inc) > 1) for (j in 2:ncol(inc)) cs[, j] <- cs[, j - 1] + inc[, j]
  cs / matrix(seq_len(ncol(inc)), nrow(inc), ncol(inc), byrow = TRUE) }
peek_once <- function(looks, delta, R = 20000, tau = sN, batch_se = 0.15) {  # one batch alone has standard error batch_se
  inc <- matrix(rnorm(R * looks, rep(delta, each = 1), batch_se), R, looks)       # per-batch estimates of the difference
  est <- run_mean(inc)                                                            # running mean
  v <- batch_se^2 / matrix(1:looks, R, looks, byrow = TRUE)                       # its variance
  z_hit <- rowSums(abs(est / sqrt(v)) > 1.96) > 0                                 # classical test at every look
  post_sd <- sqrt(1 / (1 / v + 1 / tau^2)); post_m <- post_sd^2 * est / v         # normal prior N(0, tau^2)
  pp <- pnorm(post_m / post_sd)
  b_hit <- rowSums(pp > 0.975 | pp < 0.025) > 0                                    # Bayesian rule at every look
  c(z_test = mean(z_hit), bayes_rule = mean(b_hit)) }
peeking <- map_dfr(c(1, 2, 5, 10, 20, 50), ~ c(looks = .x, peek_once(.x, delta = 0)))
peeking |> mutate(across(-looks, ~ round(.x, 3)))

With a single look, the classical test has its promised 5% false-positive rate. With 5 looks it is 14%, with 20 looks 25% and with 50 looks 32%. The Bayesian rule, which stops when the posterior probability (under a normal prior with the standard deviation estimated from the Upworthy experiments) leaves the interval from 0.025 to 0.975, does a little better because the prior shrinks early, noisy estimates, but it too reaches 22% with 20 looks and 29% with 50. Stopping at the first exciting moment makes exciting moments easy to find.

Does this mean that Bayesian inference is ruined by optional stopping? No, but the guarantee it offers is different. The posterior is computed from the likelihood, and the likelihood does not depend on why the experimenter stopped, so a posterior probability of 0.975 means what it says averaged over the prior: among all experiments whose effects really come from the prior and that stopped with a posterior probability of 0.975 that B is better, B is better in about 97.5% of them, however the stopping was decided. What it does not promise is a 2.5% error rate for every fixed effect, including an effect of exactly zero. The next simulation draws each experiment's true difference from the normal prior and checks the claims made by the Bayesian rule with twenty looks.

14.6 Monitoring a trial: posterior and predictive probabilities

Clinical trials are monitored too, but formally: a data monitoring committee looks at pre-specified interim analyses and stops the trial early if the evidence is overwhelming, if the treatment is futile, or if it is harmful. Two Bayesian quantities guide such decisions. The posterior probability of benefit, Pr(θT<θC∣data so far) for a mortality outcome, says what we believe now. The predictive probability of success says how likely the trial is to succeed if it runs to completion: average, over the posterior, the probability that the data still to come will push the final analysis over its success threshold. It is the natural quantity for futility decisions, because a trial whose predictive probability of success is 2% is not worth continuing, whatever the current estimate.

Consider an interim analysis of a two-arm trial in critically ill patients, planned for 400 patients per arm with a final success criterion of a posterior probability above 0.99 that mortality is lower on treatment. At the interim, 200 patients per arm have been followed: 60 deaths on treatment and 72 on control.

14.7 Adaptive allocation

If the evidence so far favours one arm, why keep giving half the patients, or half the visitors, the arm that looks worse? Response-adaptive randomisation shifts the allocation toward arms that are doing better. Its most elegant form is Thompson sampling (Thompson, 1933): for each new unit, draw one value of every arm's success rate from its current posterior, and assign the unit to the arm whose draw is largest. An arm is therefore chosen with exactly its posterior probability of being best. Early on every arm gets a fair share; as evidence accumulates, the allocation concentrates on the winner. Thompson sampling is a multi-armed bandit strategy, designed to maximise the total reward while learning, and it is how many companies now run headline and recommendation tests.

We compare it with equal allocation on simulated headline tests with four candidates, whose true differences in log-odds are drawn from the heavy-tailed distribution of Section 14.2, a base click rate of 1.4% and 20,000 visitors.

R
run_test <- function(true_lo, visitors = 20000, thompson = TRUE, batch = 100, drift = 0) {
  K <- length(true_lo); clicks <- shown <- numeric(K); share <- matrix(NA, visitors / batch, K)
  for (b in seq_len(visitors / batch)) {
    base <- drift * (b - 1) / (visitors / batch)                         # optional drift in the base rate over time
    if (thompson) { draws <- matrix(rbeta(K * 200, 1 + clicks, 1 + shown - clicks), 200, K, byrow = TRUE)
      w <- tabulate(max.col(draws), K) / 200 } else w <- rep(1 / K, K)
    n_b <- as.vector(rmultinom(1, batch, w)); share[b, ] <- n_b / batch
    clicks <- clicks + rbinom(K, n_b, plogis(qlogis(0.014) + true_lo + base)); shown <- shown + n_b }
  list(clicks = sum(clicks), best_found = which.max((clicks + 1) / (shown + 2)) == which.max(true_lo),
       est_gap = qlogis((clicks[which.max(true_lo)] + 1) / (shown[which.max(true_lo)] + 2)) - qlogis((sum(clicks[-which.max(true_lo)]) + 1) / (sum(shown[-which.max(true_lo)]) + 2)),
       true_gap = max(true_lo) - log(mean(exp(true_lo[-which.max(true_lo)]))), share = share)
}

Over 300 simulated tests, Thompson sampling earned 363 clicks per test against 290 for equal allocation, 25% more, because it stopped wasting traffic on poor headlines early. It also found the truly best headline slightly more often (79% against 76%). The price shows in the estimates: Thompson sampling underestimated the gap between the best headline and the others by 0.08 on average, while equal allocation was unbiased. Arms that fall behind early are starved of data, so their early bad luck is never corrected, and the comparison inherits it.

Adaptive randomisation is controversial in medicine, for reasons the simulation hints at (Robertson et al., 2023). Patients enrolled late are allocated differently from patients enrolled early, so if the patient population or standards of care drift during the trial, a time trend becomes confounded with treatment, and the comparison is biased unless the analysis models time. Imbalanced arms estimate the difference less precisely than equal ones, so a trial may need more patients for the same power. And the benefit to patients inside the trial is often small compared with the benefit to the far larger number treated after it, who need a precise answer. Platform trials such as REMAP-CAP use adaptive allocation with care: allocation is updated at scheduled analyses rather than patient by patient, a fixed share is kept for control, and time is modelled.

14.8 Platform trials

A platform trial studies several treatments for one disease under a single master protocol that runs indefinitely: arms are added as new candidates appear, dropped when a statistical trigger declares them effective, futile or harmful, and analysed together with a shared control group. The design was developed for exactly the conditions of a pandemic, and Bayesian models are its natural language, because they make posterior probabilities available at any moment and borrow strength across related arms.

Check Yourself

A trial's interim analysis reports a posterior probability of benefit of 0.80 and a predictive probability of success of 0.03. What should the monitoring committee conclude?

The two numbers answer different questions.

A low predictive probability is not evidence of harm.

A posterior probability of 0.80 is modest evidence of some benefit. The predictive probability of success integrates over the remaining patients and asks whether the final posterior will exceed a demanding threshold, such as 0.99; at 0.03, it almost certainly will not. That is futility with respect to the trial's stated goal, not evidence of no effect.

The treatment may well help, but even if the trial runs to completion it is very unlikely to reach its success criterion; continuing is worth it only if a smaller benefit, short of the criterion, would still change practice, so the criterion and the trial's purpose should be reconsidered rather than the trial continued by default.

Key Takeaways: Lecture 32

  • Checking a test repeatedly and stopping at the first "significant" result inflates false positives, for classical tests and for naive Bayesian thresholds alike (25% false positives with twenty looks at |z|>1.96).
  • Bayesian posterior probabilities stay calibrated under optional stopping when averaged over a prior that describes the effects being tested; they do not guarantee error rates for every fixed effect.
  • The predictive probability of success is the natural basis for futility decisions.
  • Thompson sampling allocates by the posterior probability of being best; it earns more during the test but distorts estimates and is vulnerable to time trends, which is why platform trials adapt cautiously and model time.

Lecture 33 — Decisions

14.9 From posteriors to actions

A posterior distribution summarises what we know; a decision also needs to know what we want. Bayesian decision theory joins the two. There is a set of possible actions a (approve the drug or not, ship headline B or keep A, fund the programme or not), an unknown state θ, and a loss L(a,θ), or equivalently a utility U(a,θ)=−L(a,θ), saying how bad each action is in each state.

Definition: Bayes Action

The Bayes action minimises the posterior expected loss,

a*=argmina𝔼[L(a,θ)∣data]=argmina∫L(a,θ)p(θ∣data)dθ.

Familiar summaries are Bayes actions for particular losses. If the action is a number and the loss is squared error, L(a,θ)=(a−θ)2, the Bayes action is the posterior mean; with absolute error it is the posterior median. The most useful case in practice is a choice between two actions with asymmetric consequences. Suppose treating a patient who would benefit gains b, and treating one who would not costs c (side effects, money, a forgone alternative). Then treating has posterior expected utility bPr(benefit∣data)−c(1−Pr(benefit∣data)), and it is the better action exactly when

Pr(benefit∣data)>cb+c.

The threshold is set by the costs, not by convention. A cheap, safe treatment for a deadly disease should be used at a posterior probability well below 0.5; an expensive one with serious side effects needs much more. The 0.95 and 0.975 of significance testing are not decision thresholds at all; they are conventions for controlling a particular error rate, which may or may not correspond to anybody's losses.

14.10 A trial that "failed"

The EOLIA trial randomised 249 patients with very severe acute respiratory distress syndrome to early extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (ECMO), in which a machine oxygenates the blood outside the body, or to conventional ventilation, with ECMO allowed as a rescue (Combes et al., 2018). At 60 days, 44 of 124 patients on early ECMO had died (35%), against 57 of 125 on conventional treatment (46%). 14.11 The value of information

Should a hospital adopt early ECMO after EOLIA, and should anyone run another trial? Decision theory answers both with the same posterior. To make the example concrete we need values that the trial does not supply, and we state them as illustrative assumptions, which a real analysis would take from health economics: each death averted gains 10 quality-adjusted life years (QALYs), a health system is willing to pay λ per QALY, and early ECMO costs an extra $40,000 per patient. The net monetary benefit per patient of adopting ECMO is then

NB(θ)=λ×10×(p0−p1)−40,000,

where p0−p1 is the absolute reduction in the risk of death. The Bayes decision adopts ECMO if the posterior mean of NB is positive. But the decision is made under uncertainty, and resolving that uncertainty has a value. The expected value of perfect information (EVPI) is how much better, on average, the decision would be if we knew θ:

EVPI=𝔼[max(NB(θ),0)]−max(𝔼[NB(θ)],0).

Multiplied by the number of patients the decision affects, EVPI is an upper bound on what any further research is worth. The expected value of sample information (EVSI) does the same calculation for a specific new trial of n patients per arm: simulate the trial's data from the current posterior, update, decide, and average. If EVSI exceeds the trial's cost, the trial is worth running.

Lecture 34 — Bayesian answers with frequentist guarantees

14.13 Calibrated Bayes

Statisticians have argued for a century about whether probability describes beliefs or long-run frequencies. In applied work the argument has largely been settled by a division of labour that Roderick Little (2006) called calibrated Bayes, building on George Box (1980) and Donald Rubin (1984): use Bayesian models to make inferences, because they handle complex models, prior information, nuisance parameters and missing data coherently; and use frequentist calculations to check them, because a procedure that is badly calibrated in repeated use should not be trusted, whatever its philosophy.

Two different guarantees are in play, and Lecture 32 has already separated them.

  • Calibration on average over the prior. If parameters really are drawn from the prior and data from the model, then 90% credible intervals contain the truth 90% of the time and posterior probabilities are accurate, by construction. This is what simulation-based calibration checks in Chapter 5, where it is used to test the computation.
  • Calibration for every parameter value. A frequentist guarantee, such as a type I error of at most 2.5%, must hold for each fixed θ, including values the prior thinks unlikely. Bayesian procedures do not have it automatically, but it can be checked, and a design can be tuned to have it, by choosing the decision thresholds, the prior or the sample size.

The rest of this lecture shows four ways of getting the second guarantee: tuning the thresholds of a Bayesian design (Section 14.14), using Bayes factors that are valid under optional stopping (Section 14.15), turning those Bayes factors into intervals that are valid at every moment (Section 14.16), and measuring the frequentist variability of a Bayesian estimate directly (Section 14.17). Two further ideas deserve a mention. Probability-matching priors are priors chosen so that one-sided credible intervals have exact or approximate frequentist coverage; for a normal mean the flat prior is one, and for many models Jeffreys' prior comes close. Conformal Bayesian computation (Fong and Holmes, 2021) uses the posterior predictive distribution to build prediction intervals that have finite-sample frequentist coverage whatever the model, a guarantee that is especially useful when a model is surely misspecified.

14.14 Tuning a Bayesian design: the Pfizer–BioNTech trial

Chapter 1 analysed the final result of the BNT162b2 vaccine trial. Its design deserves a closer look, because it is the most widely seen example of a Bayesian trial tuned to frequentist requirements (Polack et al., 2020). The analysis concerned θ, the probability that a COVID-19 case occurred in the vaccine group rather than the placebo group. With equal-sized groups, θ=(1−VE)/(2−VE), where VE is the vaccine efficacy, so that VE=(1−2θ)/(1−θ). The prior was beta(0.700102, 1), chosen so that its mean corresponds to an efficacy of 30% and it is only weakly informative. The trial would declare success if the posterior probability that VE exceeded 30% was above 0.995 at any of four interim analyses, after 32, 62, 92 and 120 cases, or above 0.986 at the final analysis after 164 cases.

Why these numbers? The regulators required that the probability of declaring success when the true efficacy is only 30%, the type I error, be at most 2.5% across all five looks. A posterior probability threshold of 0.95, applied at every look, would not achieve that. The thresholds 0.995 and 0.986 were chosen to make it so. Because the number of vaccine cases at each look is binomial, the operating characteristics can be computed exactly.

Tune the thresholds

The Pfizer–BioNTech design: success is declared at the first of five looks (32, 62, 92, 120 and 164 cases) at which the posterior probability that efficacy exceeds 30% passes its threshold. The prior and the posterior probabilities make the trial's statements interpretable ("given the data, the probability that efficacy exceeds 30% is above 99.9%"), while the thresholds give regulators the error rate they require. The same recipe, specify the Bayesian decision rule, compute or simulate its operating characteristics over a range of true effects, and tune until they are acceptable, is the backbone of the US Food and Drug Administration's draft guidance on Bayesian methods in drug trials (FDA, 2026). 14.15 Looking as often as you like: e-values

The thresholds of Section 14.14 work for a fixed schedule of five looks. A website wants to look continuously. For that, the right object is a statistic that keeps its meaning however often it is inspected, and a Bayes factor provides one. Let d^t be the estimated difference after t batches, with variance vt. Compare the null hypothesis δ=0 with an alternative that spreads δ as normal(0,τ2):

Et=normal(d^t∣0,vt+τ2)normal(d^t∣0,vt).

Why does this work? Recall from Chapter 1 that a martingale is a sequence whose expected next value, given everything so far, equals its current value. A Bayes factor monitored over time is one, under the null hypothesis, and a classical inequality bounds how often such a sequence can ever climb high.

Theorem: Ville's Inequality

Let E0=1,E1,E2,… be a nonnegative martingale under a hypothesis H0. Then for every α∈(0,1),

PrH0(supn≥0En≥1/α)≤α.

A nonnegative martingale with E0=1 is called a test martingale. Any Bayes factor against a simple null hypothesis, with a prior chosen before the data, is a test martingale under that null.

The Bayes factor is a martingale. Write mn(y1:n) for the marginal likelihood under the alternative and p0(y1:n) for the likelihood under the null, so En=mn/p0. Then, under the null,

𝔼H0[En+1∣y1:n]=∫mn+1(y1:n,y)p0(y1:n,y)p0(y∣y1:n)dy=1p0(y1:n)∫mn+1(y1:n,y)dy=mn(y1:n)p0(y1:n)=En,

since p0(y1:n,y)=p0(y1:n)p0(y∣y1:n) and integrating the alternative's marginal over the next observation gives its marginal for the first n.

Ville's inequality. Let τ be the first n at which En≥1/α (τ=∞ if this never happens), and fix a horizon N. The stopped process Emin(n,τ) is also a martingale, so 𝔼H0[Emin(N,τ)]=E0=1. Because E is nonnegative and is at least 1/α on the event {τ≤N},

1=𝔼H0[Emin(N,τ)]≥1αPrH0(τ≤N),

so PrH0(τ≤N)≤α for every N, and letting N→∞ gives the result. ∎

For the e-value above, Et is exactly such a Bayes factor, so rejecting the null hypothesis the first time Et reaches 1/α has a type I error of at most α, however many times, and at whatever moments, the data are inspected, and even if the experimenter decides to continue or stop by looking at the data.

Statistics with this property are called e-values (or, as a process over time, e-processes), and inference built on them is always valid (Johari et al., 2022; Grünwald, de Heide and Koolen, 2024). The mixture Bayes factor above is the "mixture sequential probability ratio test" used by several commercial experimentation platforms. The price is power: to be valid at every moment, the boundary must be higher than a single-look test's. The simulation compares a single test at the end with an e-value monitored after every batch, under no effect and under effects drawn from the Upworthy distribution.

The e-value above used a normal approximation to the difference in click rates. For counts there is an exact version, and it is worth seeing because it shows exactly where the guarantee comes from. Suppose two headlines are shown with fixed probabilities, a share q0 of visitors seeing B. If the headlines are equally good, each click, whenever it arrives, comes from B with probability q0, whatever the click rate is. So we can watch only the clicks: after n clicks, k of them on B. Under the null hypothesis the clicks are Bernoulli(q0); under the alternative the share of clicks on B is some other q, about which we put a beta(a, a) prior. The Bayes factor after n clicks is

En=∫qk(1−q)n−kbeta(q∣a,a)dqq0k(1−q0)n−k=B(a+k,a+n−k)B(a,a)q0k(1−q0)n−k,

with B the beta function. The relative lift of B over A is q1−q·1−q0q0, so a prior on q is a prior on the lift.

Two things about Ville's inequality deserve emphasis. The guarantee holds for every prior: the beta(a, a) prior can be chosen to reflect the effects seen in past experiments, and that changes how quickly real effects are detected, never the error rate. And the guarantee survives any stopping rule, including stopping because the result looks good, which is exactly what experimenters do. In the language of this lecture: the prior tunes power; the martingale guarantees validity.

The Upworthy deconvolution of Section 14.2 gives a prior. Its heavy-tailed effect distribution has scale 0.234 on the log-odds scale, and a beta(a, a) prior on the click share has a log-odds standard deviation of about 2/a, so a=37 matches it. We compare it with a uniform prior (a=1), first by simulation: 20,000 visitors per arm, a click rate of 1.5%, and either no effect or a lift of 20%. 14.16 Confidence sequences

A test can be turned into an interval by collecting the values it does not reject. Applying the Bayes factor to every possible click share q, not just q0, gives the set

Cn=\{q:B(a+k,a+n−k)B(a,a)qk(1−q)n−k<1α\},

and Ville's inequality, applied at the true q, says that Pr(q∈Cn for every n)≥1−α. This is a confidence sequence: a sequence of intervals that contain the truth simultaneously at all times with probability at least 1−α, so it can be reported after every click without any correction. Note the shape of the condition. The ratio is the prior predictive probability of the data divided by their likelihood at q, and that equals the prior density at q divided by the posterior density at q. So Cn is the set where the posterior density exceeds α times the prior density: a posterior region, with its level set by the prior rather than by the posterior mass. Robbins (1970) found this construction; Howard, Ramdas, McAuliffe and Sekhon (2021) and Waudby-Smith and Ramdas (2024) made it practical and sharp.

Confidence sequences now exist for far more than proportions: for means of bounded outcomes without any distributional assumption, by "betting" (Waudby-Smith and Ramdas, 2024), for quantiles, for regression coefficients, and, using asymptotic arguments, for average treatment effects estimated with propensity scores and outcome models (Waudby-Smith, Arbour, Sinha, Kennedy and Ramdas, 2024). Research Practice: Reporting an Anytime-Valid Analysis

  • State, before the data, the e-value or mixture used, including the prior, and the threshold 1/α. The prior may be informed by past experiments; say which.
  • Report the stopping time and the reason for stopping. Stopping early does not invalidate the error guarantee, but readers need it to interpret the estimate: an experiment stopped early on a large e-value tends to overestimate the effect (the winner's curse of Section 14.3 again), so report a shrunken estimate as well as the confidence sequence.
  • Report the confidence sequence at the stopping time, labelled as such, not a fixed-sample interval computed after stopping.
  • If the analysis also uses posterior probabilities, say which statements carry a frequentist guarantee (the e-value and the confidence sequence) and which do not.

History: From Ville to the Experimentation Platform

Jean Ville introduced the word "martingale" into probability in his 1939 thesis, which used it to criticise von Mises's definition of randomness, and proved the inequality that bears his name. Abraham Wald's sequential probability ratio test (1945), developed for testing munitions in the Second World War, is a likelihood-ratio martingale with two stopping boundaries. Herbert Robbins and his students, Donald Darling and David Siegmund, introduced mixtures of likelihood ratios and confidence sequences around 1970, and Tze Leung Lai developed their theory. The ideas were largely confined to clinical-trial monitoring until the 2010s, when online experimentation made continuous monitoring routine: Johari, Pekelis and Walsh's always-valid p-values were built into Optimizely's platform, and Netflix uses anytime-valid tests for count data (Lindon and Malek, 2022). Glenn Shafer and Vladimir Vovk's game-theoretic probability (2001, 2019) recast the whole subject as betting, and the "safe testing" of Grünwald, de Heide and Koolen (2024) and the e-value programme of Ramdas, Wang and others have made it one of the most active areas of statistics. Ramdas, Grünwald, Vovk and Shafer (2023) survey it.

14.17 The frequentist accuracy of a Bayesian estimate

A posterior standard deviation describes uncertainty given the data. How much would the posterior mean itself vary if the study were repeated? Bradley Efron (2015) gave a general answer that needs nothing beyond the posterior draws already in hand. Write the data as x and the likelihood as f(x∣θ), and let αx(θ)=∇xlog⁡f(x∣θ) be the gradient of the log likelihood with respect to the data. Then the gradient of the posterior expectation of any quantity t(θ) with respect to the data is a posterior covariance,

∇x𝔼[t(θ)∣x]=cov(t(θ),αx(θ)∣x),

which can be estimated from posterior draws, and the delta method turns it into a frequentist standard deviation, sdfreq≈g^⊤Vg^, with g^ the estimated gradient and V the sampling covariance of x. For the microcredit model of Section 14.12, the data are the five site estimates, xk∼normal(θk,sk), so αx(θ)k=(θk−xk)/sk2 and V=diag(sk2). We compare Efron's estimate with the posterior standard deviation, and check it with a parametric bootstrap that refits the model 200 times to data simulated from the fitted site effects.

The posterior standard deviation of μ is $5.94, but the frequentist standard deviation of its posterior mean is only about $3: Efron's formula gives $2.99 and the bootstrap $3.40. They answer different questions. The posterior standard deviation describes what we do not know about μ, including what the prior and the small number of sites leave open about the variation between sites. The frequentist standard deviation describes how much the estimate would move if the five studies were repeated with the same true site effects: not much, because shrinkage stabilises it. A Bayesian estimate can be more stable than its posterior uncertainty suggests, and Efron's formula measures the stability without refitting the model; the bootstrap, which needed 200 refits, agrees reasonably well.

14.18 Experiments and decisions in practice

The ideas of this chapter come together in a few practical rules. Design experiments with their decisions in mind: state the action that each result would lead to, and the costs of each kind of error. Use what is known about the population of effects, which for organisations that run many experiments is a hierarchical model of their own history. Decide in advance how often you will look, and use a method that is valid for that schedule. Report posterior probabilities of effects of meaningful size, not only of any effect. And, where a frequentist guarantee is required, compute the operating characteristics of the Bayesian procedure and tune it until they are met.

Research Practice: Reporting a Bayesian or Adaptive Trial

  • Pre-specify the model, the priors (with their effective sample sizes), the decision thresholds and the schedule of analyses, and publish them before the data are seen.
  • Report the operating characteristics: type I error and power over a range of true effects, from exact calculation or simulation, with the code.
  • Report the posterior probabilities of clinically meaningful effects (for example a 2% absolute risk reduction), and the results under alternative priors, as the EOLIA reanalysis did.
  • For adaptive designs, say how allocation changed, how time trends were handled, and follow the CONSORT extension for adaptive designs (Dimairo et al., 2020).
  • For A/B tests, report the number of looks, the stopping rule, and the shrunken (not raw) effect of the shipped change.

History: Thompson's Bandit and Wald's Sequential Test

William R. Thompson proposed allocating patients by the posterior probability that each treatment is better in 1933, in one of the first papers on what would become the multi-armed bandit problem; the idea was ignored for seventy years and rediscovered by the technology industry. During the Second World War Abraham Wald developed the sequential probability ratio test for the quality control of munitions, a test that looks at the data after every observation; it was classified until 1945. Wald's likelihood ratio is a martingale under the null hypothesis, and the modern theory of e-values grew from the same insight. Bayesian clinical trials became practical in the 1990s and 2000s, notably at the MD Anderson Cancer Center, and the COVID-19 pandemic brought adaptive platform trials such as RECOVERY and REMAP-CAP into the mainstream.

Check Yourself

A sponsor proposes to declare success if the posterior probability of benefit exceeds 0.95 at any of ten interim analyses, arguing that Bayesian analyses do not need multiplicity adjustment. How should a regulator respond?

Ten looks at a 0.95 threshold is exactly the setting of Section 14.5.

Bayesian designs can be calibrated; they need not be abandoned.

The likelihood principle makes the posterior the same whatever the stopping rule, but it says nothing about how often a procedure errs when the treatment is useless. With ten looks at 0.95 the false-positive rate is far above 5%. Tuning the thresholds restores the frequentist guarantee without giving up the Bayesian interpretation.

The posterior probabilities are valid statements under the prior, but the regulator's requirement is a bound on the error rate for every true effect, including none; the sponsor must compute the design's type I error by simulation or exact calculation and raise the thresholds (as Pfizer–BioNTech did) until it is acceptable.

Key Takeaways: Lecture 34

  • Calibrated Bayes: infer with Bayesian models; check, and if necessary tune, their frequentist behaviour.
  • Bayesian designs can meet frequentist requirements exactly: the Pfizer–BioNTech thresholds of 0.995 and 0.986 kept the type I error at 2.18%, where 0.95 at every look would not have.
  • Bayes factors against a mixture alternative are e-values: thresholded at 1/α, they control the type I error however often the data are inspected.
  • Any Bayes factor with a prior fixed in advance is a test martingale; by Ville's inequality the prior affects only how quickly real effects are found, never the error rate. Inverting it gives a confidence sequence, valid at every moment, at the cost of wider intervals (about twice as wide in the Upworthy example).
  • Efron's formula gives the frequentist standard deviation of any posterior expectation from the posterior draws alone.

  3. Five looks. An analyst checks an A/A test five times, at equal intervals, and declares a winner at the first |z|>1.96. Estimate the false-positive rate by simulation. What constant critical value would keep it at 5%?

    Use peek_once(5, 0); for the critical value, search over values between 1.96 and 3.

    With five looks the false-positive rate is about 14%. To bring it back to 5% the critical value must rise to about 2.41 at every look, the value Stuart Pocock derived for five equally spaced analyses in 1977. The price is power at the final analysis, where a single-look test would have used 1.96. Group-sequential designs used in trials spend the 5% more cleverly, with very high thresholds early and close to 1.96 at the end.

  4. Your own prior for ECMO. A clinician thinks before EOLIA that ECMO is as likely to harm as to help, but that a relative risk outside 0.5 to 2 is very unlikely: a normal prior on the log relative risk with mean 0 and standard deviation 0.35.     With a normal(0, 0.35) prior on the log relative risk, the posterior median relative risk is 0.81, the probability that ECMO reduces mortality is 0.94, and the probability of at least a 2-percentage-point absolute reduction is 0.88. Her prior is sceptical enough to pull the estimate toward 1, but not enough to overturn the trial's evidence, which is close to the paper's range of 88% to 99% across priors.

  5. One threshold for every look. Suppose the Pfizer–BioNTech design had used the same posterior-probability threshold at all five looks. What is the smallest such threshold that keeps the type I error at or below 2.5%, and what is the design's power at an efficacy of 60%, compared with the protocol's?

    Search over thresholds with boundary(rep(t, 5)) and p_success.

    The smallest constant threshold that keeps the type I error at or below 2.5% is about 0.991, giving 2.41%. Its power at an efficacy of 60% is 86.4%, against 88.4% for the protocol's 0.995 at the interims and 0.986 at the end. Spending less of the error budget at the interim looks, when the data are few and early stopping is less valuable, and more at the final analysis buys power: the same principle as the group-sequential designs of Exercise 3.

  6. Thompson sampling and a drifting audience. Repeat the comparison of Section 14.7 with a base click rate that rises by 0.5 on the log-odds scale over the course of each test (drift = 0.5). How does the error in the estimated gap between the best headline and the others change for equal allocation and for Thompson sampling?

    Pass drift = 0.5 to run_test.

    With a drifting base rate, the root mean squared error of the estimated gap is 0.12 for equal allocation and 0.31 for Thompson sampling, almost three times as large. Under Thompson sampling the best headline receives most of its traffic late in the test, when the base rate is higher, so its click rate is inflated relative to the others by the drift as well as by the effect. Equal allocation spreads every arm evenly over time, and the drift cancels. This is why adaptive clinical trials model calendar time explicitly.

  7. Choosing the mixing prior. Rerun the Upworthy application of Section 14.15 with beta(a, a) mixing priors for a=10 and a=150 as well as 37. How many experiments does each stop, and with what median share of the clicks? Check the type I error of each by replacing every experiment's clicks with clicks drawn under the null hypothesis.

    Change the third argument of log_bf. Under the null, draw each click from B with probability q0.

    The wider prior stops the most experiments: 11.9% for a=10, 7.6% for a=37 and 2.7% for a=150, with median shares of the clicks of 0.57, 0.68 and 0.73. With a median of 88 clicks, only large differences can be detected, and a prior that puts weight on large lifts pays off; the tighter prior, which matches the typical Upworthy effect, bets on effects too small to find in such short tests. Under the null hypothesis the error rates are 1.0% for a=10 and 0.04% for a=150, both below 5%: the prior changes the power, never the validity. Choosing the prior for the experiments you will actually run, short or long, is a design decision, and simulation under plausible effects is the way to make it.

  8. Stopping on a naive interval. Simulate 4,000 streams of up to 1,000 clicks with no difference between the headlines (q=0.5). An experimenter computes the standard 95% interval for q after every click from the 20th onwards and stops as soon as it excludes 0.5. How often does she stop, and so declare a difference that does not exist? At the moment she stops, how often does the 95% confidence sequence of Section 14.16 exclude 0.5?

    Record the first click at which |q^−0.5|>1.96se, then compute the confidence sequence at that click.

    She stops, wrongly, in 44% of the streams: the nominal 5% error rate is multiplied almost ninefold by looking after every click. At the moments she stops, the confidence sequence excludes 0.5 in none of the 4,000 streams. By Ville's inequality it can exclude the truth at any moment in at most 5% of streams, and the moments the naive interval picks out are exactly the chance fluctuations a confidence sequence is built to absorb.
