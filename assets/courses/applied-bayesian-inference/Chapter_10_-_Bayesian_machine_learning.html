Chapter 10
Bayesian machine learning

Lectures 19–20 · shrinkage priors from ridge to the horseshoe, prediction versus explanation, more features than observations, and Bayesian trees and networks

Machine learning, in its dominant tradition, is about prediction. Choose a flexible function, find the parameter values that fit the training data best, usually by minimising a loss plus a penalty whose strength is chosen by cross-validation, and use the result to predict new cases. This works very well, and for many problems it is the right approach. But it leaves something out. A prediction without a statement of how uncertain it is forces the user to guess which predictions to trust. When a wrong prediction is costly, as in a clinical decision, a loan approval or a fraud alert, that uncertainty is not a luxury; it is part of what has to be delivered.

This chapter is about prediction with the uncertainty put back. The models are familiar machine-learning shapes: regression with many features, ensembles of decision trees, neural networks. Each is fitted the Bayesian way, with a prior, a likelihood and a posterior, so that every prediction comes with a distribution rather than a single number. The central idea is that the classical regularisers of machine learning are priors in disguise, and that once we see them as priors we can design better ones.

  • Lecture 19 is about shrinkage priors. We will try to price houses from 39 features using only 100 sales, watch an unregularised model overfit, see that ridge regression and the lasso are posterior modes under normal and Laplace priors, and build the horseshoe prior, which shrinks noise hard and leaves real signals almost alone.
  • Lecture 20 is about practice. We will compare four priors on 2,830 houses the models never saw, choose a small set of predictors by projection, move to a breast-cancer data set with more features than patients, and look beyond linear models at Bayesian additive regression trees, Gaussian processes and Bayesian neural networks.

Think About It: Before You Start

You have 100 houses and 39 things you could measure about each one. You fit a regression with all 39. Its predictions for those 100 houses will be very good. Will its predictions for the next 100 houses be as good? Now suppose you had to guess, before seeing any data, how many of the 39 features really matter for price. Is your guess "all 39, each a little", or "a handful, and the rest hardly at all"? The answer changes which prior you should use, and Section 10.5 builds a prior for the second kind of belief.

Lecture 19 — Shrinkage priors

10.1 What is this house worth?

Dean De Cock (2011) assembled the records of every residential sale in Ames, Iowa, from 2006 to 2010: 2,930 houses, each with about 80 variables describing the lot, the building, its condition and the sale. He published them as a teaching alternative to the much older Boston housing data, and they have since become a standard benchmark in machine learning. We use the version in the modeldata package.

The question is a prediction question: given what is known about a house when it is put on the market, what will it sell for? To make the problem one where regularisation matters, we pretend that we have only a small sample to learn from: a random 100 sales. With 39 candidate predictors, that is fewer than three observations per coefficient. The other 2,830 sales are locked away as a test set, and we will use them only to judge the predictions. That is a luxury real studies rarely have, and it lets us check honestly what each method buys.

Now the split. One detail matters more than it looks: we standardise every predictor (and the outcome) using the means and standard deviations of the training houses only. Using the whole data set would let information from the test houses leak into the training step, a small version of the leakage of Chapter 9. Standardising also puts every coefficient on the same scale, "change in standardised log price per standard deviation of the predictor", which is what makes a single shrinkage prior for all of them sensible.

10.2 Prediction is not explanation

Every chapter of this course has started with a causal graph, and this one is no exception, but the graph plays a different role. For a prediction question, we do not need the arrows into the outcome to be causal effects, and we do not need to block back-door paths. A variable that is merely correlated with price, for whatever reason, helps predict it. What the graph still decides is which variables are allowed in: anything measured after the outcome (here, after the sale) is leakage, and anything that would not be available when the prediction is needed is useless. The sale condition and new-build indicators pass this test, because both are known when a house is listed; the final negotiated price of the previous sale of the same house, had it been in the file, would not.

The graph also warns us how not to read the fitted model. In a regression with many predictors, each coefficient is a conditional association: the difference in predicted price between two houses that differ in this predictor and are identical in all 38 others. For bedrooms, holding living area fixed, that is the effect of dividing the same floor space into more, smaller rooms, which buyers may well dislike; it is not the effect of adding a bedroom, which usually adds floor area too. For other predictors the coefficient may be confounded by unmeasured quality U. Westreich and Greenland (2013) called the habit of interpreting every coefficient in a multivariable model as a causal effect the Table 2 fallacy, after the table in which epidemiology papers traditionally print them. A good prediction model can be a poor guide to what would happen if we intervened, and that is not a flaw in the model; it is a different question, and Chapter 13 is about how to answer it.

Among houses with the same living area and the same values of the other predictors, those with more bedrooms tend to sell for less, perhaps because the same space is divided into smaller rooms. It is a conditional association, useful for prediction, not an effect of intervention.

10.3 Too many knobs

Start with the model that has no regularisation at all: a linear regression on all 39 standardised predictors with flat priors on the coefficients. Its posterior mean is essentially the least-squares estimate. We fit it, and then score it twice: on the 100 houses it was fitted to, and on the 2,830 it has never seen. The scores are the root mean squared error of the predicted log price (an error of 0.1 is roughly a 10% error in price) and the average log score, the log of the posterior predictive density at each test house's actual price, which rewards honest uncertainty as well as accurate centres (higher is better).

f <- bf(y ~ .)

It is spread across all 39: every coefficient is nudged by the noise in these particular 100 sales, and the nudges add up to an error in every prediction.

This is overfitting: with 39 knobs and 100 houses, the model can tune itself to the noise in these particular sales. No coefficient is under any pressure to stay small, and each small error in a coefficient becomes an error in every prediction. 10.4 Ridge and lasso are priors

The classical cures for overfitting add a penalty to the least-squares criterion. Ridge regression (Hoerl and Kennard, 1970) minimises

∑i=1n(yi−α−xi⊤β)2+λ∑j=1pβj2,

and the lasso (Tibshirani, 1996) replaces βj2 by |βj|. Both pull coefficients toward zero; the strength λ is usually chosen by cross-validation. Now take the Bayesian model yi∼Normal(α+xi⊤β, σ) with independent priors βj∼Normal(0,τ). Its log posterior is, up to a constant,

−12σ2∑i=1n(yi−α−xi⊤β)2−12τ2∑j=1pβj2.

Multiply by −2σ2 and this is exactly the ridge criterion with λ=σ2/τ2. So the ridge estimate is the posterior mode under a normal prior, and a large penalty is a narrow prior. In the same way, the lasso is the posterior mode under a Laplace (double-exponential) prior, p(βj)∝exp⁡(−|βj|/b). Regularisation has always been Bayesian inference with the posterior summarised by its mode. Doing the inference fully adds two things. The first is a whole posterior distribution for every coefficient and every prediction, not just a point. The second is that the penalty strength need not be chosen by cross-validation: give the prior scale τ its own prior and the data estimate it, exactly as the multilevel models of Chapter 6 estimated the spread of group effects. Ridge with a learned τ is a multilevel model, in which the "groups" are the coefficients.

The two priors shrink differently, and the clearest way to see it is with one coefficient. Suppose the data alone give an estimate β^ with standard error 1, and plot the posterior mean against β^: the shrinkage profile. A flat prior gives the 45° line (no shrinkage).

How three priors shrink

Left: three priors for a coefficient, the normal (ridge, blue), the Laplace (lasso, amber, dashed) and the horseshoe (red); the normal and Laplace have the same standard deviation. Right: the posterior mean as a function of the data's estimate β^ (standard error 1), computed by numerical integration. The grey diagonal is no shrinkage.

The normal prior shrinks every estimate by the same proportion: its profile is a straight line through the origin with slope τ2/(τ2+1). To tame small noisy estimates it must use a small τ, and then it also crushes large, real effects. The Laplace prior's profile bends: it shrinks small estimates harder than the normal does and large estimates less, and for large estimates it runs roughly parallel to the diagonal. But the gap never closes, because its tails are only exponential: large effects are always pulled in by about the same fixed amount. (The posterior mode under a Laplace prior is exactly zero for small estimates, which is how the lasso selects variables; the posterior mean never is.) The horseshoe, the subject of the next section, does what we actually want: small estimates are shrunk almost to zero, and large estimates are left almost untouched.

Check Yourself

A colleague fits ridge regression on standardised data with penalty λ=25, and the residual standard deviation is about σ=1. What standard deviation τ of a normal prior on each coefficient gives the same estimate as the posterior mode?

λ=σ2/τ2, so τ=σ/λ.

τ=1/25=0.2. A penalty that looks innocuous is a strong prior: it says each standardised coefficient is very probably between −0.4 and 0.4. Writing penalties as priors makes such assumptions visible, and lets you check them with a prior predictive simulation (Chapter 2).

10.5 The horseshoe

What prior would express the belief "most of these coefficients are essentially zero, but a few may be large"? It needs a tall spike at zero, to shrink noise hard, and very heavy tails, so that a genuinely large effect is not pulled in. Carvalho, Polson and Scott (2010) built such a prior as a scale mixture of normals:

βj∣λj,τ∼Normal(0, τλj), λj∼Half-Cauchy(0,1),τ∼Half-Cauchy(0,τ0).

Each coefficient has its own local scale λj, and all share one global scale τ. The global scale pulls everything toward zero; the local scales let individual coefficients escape. The half-Cauchy has so much probability near zero and so much in its far tail that most λj are small (the coefficient is shrunk) while a few can be enormous (the coefficient is left alone). The data decide which coefficient is which.

The name comes from a picture. With standardised predictors, n observations and residual standard deviation σ, the posterior mean of βj given its scale is approximately (1−κj)β^j, where

κj=11+nτ2λj2/σ2

is the shrinkage factor: κj=0 means no shrinkage, κj=1 means the coefficient is set to zero. When nτ2/σ2=1 and λj is half-Cauchy, κj has a Beta(12,12) prior: a U-shaped density piled up at both 0 and 1, shaped like a horseshoe. The prior says that each coefficient is very probably either kept or killed, and only rarely half-shrunk. A normal prior, by contrast, gives every coefficient the same κ.

Definition: Effective Number of Nonzero Coefficients

Under a shrinkage prior with shrinkage factors κ1,…,κp, the effective number of nonzero coefficients is meff=∑j=1p(1−κj). A coefficient that is not shrunk counts as one, a coefficient shrunk to zero counts as zero, and half-shrunk coefficients count as one half. Its prior distribution, implied by the priors on τ and the λj, shows how sparse a model the prior expects (Piironen and Vehtari, 2017).

The global scale matters a great deal, and the obvious default, τ∼Half-Cauchy(0,1), is a poor one. Piironen and Vehtari (2017) showed that it places most of its prior mass on models in which nearly all coefficients are unshrunk, the opposite of what we want. Their recommendation is to state a guess p0 of the number of relevant predictors and set

τ0=p0p−p0σn,

which centres the prior for meff near p0 while still letting the data move it a long way. The guess is soft: a p0 that is wrong by a factor of two or three changes the results little (Exercise 3). The widget simulates the prior distribution of meff.

With the default half-Cauchy(0, 1) and our Ames setting, the prior puts most of its mass near meff=p: it expects almost every coefficient to be large, and the horseshoe barely shrinks. With τ0 from the guess p0=5, the prior for meff is centred near five, with a long right tail that lets the data ask for more. Notice how the default's behaviour depends on n: the more data, the larger nτ2, and the less any coefficient is shrunk. Making the global scale depend on n and σ, as τ0 does, is what keeps the meaning of the prior stable.

One refinement completes the picture. The pure horseshoe lets a large coefficient be completely unregularised, which causes trouble when a predictor separates the outcome perfectly (logistic regression) or when the likelihood says little about a coefficient: the posterior then has a very long tail that the sampler struggles with. The regularised horseshoe of Piironen and Vehtari (2017) replaces λj by λ~j with λ~j2=c2λj2/(c2+τ2λj2), so that even unshrunk coefficients get a weak Normal(0,c) "slab" prior. In brms, horseshoe() is the regularised version, with the slab scale c given its own prior, and the argument par_ratio =p0/(p−p0) sets τ0 by the rule above.

Check Yourself

For one coefficient, the least-squares estimate is 3.0 and the posterior shrinkage factor is κ=0.9. Approximately what is its posterior mean? And if κ were 0.05?

The posterior mean is about (1−κ)β^.

(1−0.9)×3.0=0.3: the prior has decided this coefficient is mostly noise. With κ=0.05 it would be 0.95×3.0=2.85, almost the raw estimate. The horseshoe makes both kinds of decision in one model, coefficient by coefficient, depending on how strongly the data support each.

History: From Ridge to the Horseshoe

Arthur Hoerl and Robert Kennard (1970) introduced ridge regression to stabilise least squares with nearly collinear data, adding a constant to the diagonal of X⊤X; its reading as a Bayesian estimate under a normal prior was made explicit by Dennis Lindley and Adrian Smith (1972). Robert Tibshirani's lasso (1996) used an absolute-value penalty, which sets some coefficients exactly to zero and so selects variables; Tibshirani pointed out the link to the Laplace prior in the same paper, and Trevor Park and George Casella (2008) developed the full "Bayesian lasso". The Bayesian line of variable selection is older: Toby Mitchell and John Beauchamp (1988) proposed the spike-and-slab prior, in which each coefficient is either exactly zero (the spike) or drawn from a broad distribution (the slab), and Edward George and Robert McCulloch (1993) made it practical with Gibbs sampling. Spike-and-slab models must search over 2p subsets of predictors, which gets hard quickly, and their discrete indicators are invisible to Hamiltonian Monte Carlo. Carlos Carvalho, Nicholas Polson and James Scott (2010) designed the horseshoe as a continuous prior that behaves like a spike-and-slab, and Juho Piironen and Aki Vehtari (2017) made it usable in practice with the regularised version and the rule for τ0. All of these trace back to Stein's paradox of Chapter 6: when many quantities are estimated together, shrinking them toward a common value beats estimating each alone.

Key Takeaways: Lecture 19

  • For prediction, the causal graph decides what may enter the model (no leakage), but the fitted coefficients are conditional associations, not causal effects: beware the Table 2 fallacy.
  • With many predictors and few observations, unregularised regression overfits: excellent in sample, poor on new data.
  • Ridge and the lasso are posterior modes under normal and Laplace priors; λ=σ2/τ2. A full Bayesian fit gives uncertainty and can learn the penalty from the data.
  • The horseshoe combines a global scale with heavy-tailed local scales: noise is shrunk hard and real signals are left almost alone. Set its global scale from a guess p0 of the number of relevant predictors, and check the implied prior on meff.

10.6 Four priors, one hundred houses

We now fit three regularised versions of the 39-predictor model and compare them with the flat-prior fit. The Bayesian ridge and Bayesian lasso use a normal and a Laplace prior with a common scale sb that is learned from the data, with a half-normal(0, 1) prior; brms has no built-in option for a learned scale on regression coefficients, so we declare the parameter ourselves with stanvar(). The horseshoe uses the guess p0=5, so par_ratio =5/34. Horseshoe posteriors have narrow funnels near zero (Chapter 6), so we raise adapt_delta to make the sampler take smaller steps.

R
sb <- stanvar(scode = "real<lower=0> s_b;", block = "parameters") +          # a learned prior scale
      stanvar(scode = "target += normal_lpdf(s_b | 0, 1);", block = "model")  # half-normal(0, 1)
m_ridge <- brm(f, data = train, prior = c(base, prior(normal(0, s_b), class = b)),
               stanvars = sb, seed = 10, refresh = 0, file = "fits/ch10_ridge")
m_lasso <- brm(f, data = train, prior = c(base, prior(double_exponential(0, s_b), class = b)),
               stanvars = sb, seed = 10, refresh = 0, file = "fits/ch10_lasso")
m_hs <- brm(f, data = train, prior = c(base, prior(horseshoe(par_ratio = 5 / 34), class = b)),
            seed = 10, refresh = 0, control = list(adapt_delta = 0.999), file = "fits/ch10_hs")
rbind(ridge = posterior_summary(m_ridge, variable = "s_b"),

Check Yourself

Your model has 39 coefficients and 100 observations, and PSIS-LOO reports many observations with Pareto-k^ above 0.7. You have no test set. What should you do?

High k means the importance-sampling estimate itself is unreliable.

Removing influential points to make a diagnostic pass is not a fix.

High Pareto-k^ means that removing one observation changes the posterior too much for importance sampling to approximate it. That is expected when there are few observations per parameter: each house pulls on many coefficients. The cure is to compute the leave-out posteriors honestly by refitting, either for the flagged observations or with K folds. It costs more computation, which is cheap compared with reporting a wrong number.

Use a method that refits the model: exact leave-one-out for the flagged observations (reloo = TRUE), moment matching, or K-fold cross-validation (kfold()), and report which you used.

10.7 Which predictors matter?

The horseshoe does not select variables: every coefficient keeps a posterior, however concentrated near zero. Often, though, we want a small model, because measuring 39 things is expensive, or because a model with eight predictors can be explained and audited. A tempting shortcut is to keep the predictors whose 90% intervals exclude zero. It is a poor rule: with correlated predictors (three size measures, two garage measures), the posterior may be sure that one of a group matters while being unsure which, so each interval crosses zero even though dropping the whole group would hurt.

Projection predictive variable selection (Goutis and Robert, 1998; Piironen, Paasiniemi and Vehtari, 2020) handles this properly. Treat the full horseshoe model as the reference model, the best description of the data we have. For each candidate subset of predictors, find the submodel whose predictions are closest to the reference model's (in KL divergence), a projection that is computed draw by draw, so the submodel inherits the reference model's uncertainty. Add predictors one at a time, choosing at each step the one that brings the submodel closest, and stop when the submodel predicts about as well as the reference. The projpred package does this for brms fits.

Research Practice: Reporting a Prediction Model

The TRIPOD statement (Collins et al., 2015), updated as TRIPOD+AI for regression and machine-learning models (Collins et al., 2024), is the reporting standard for prediction models in health research, and its logic applies everywhere.

  • State the purpose (prediction, not explanation) and the moment at which the prediction will be made; list the candidate predictors and confirm none is measured after that moment.
  • Report the sample size relative to the number of candidate parameters, and how missing data were handled.
  • Describe every preprocessing step, and fit it (standardisation, imputation, feature filtering) inside the training data or inside each cross-validation fold.
  • Give the priors in full, including the global scale of a horseshoe and the guess p0 behind it, with a sensitivity analysis.
  • Report out-of-sample performance: accuracy (RMSE), discrimination (AUC), calibration (do 90% predictive intervals contain 90% of new outcomes?), and a proper scoring rule (log score). Say how it was estimated: external test set, K-fold, or LOO with its Pareto-k^ diagnostics.
  • Do not present the coefficients of a prediction model as causal effects; if variables were selected, say how (projection, not interval exclusion), and report the performance of the selected model on held-out data.

Warning: Where the Filter Looks

A filter that keeps the genes most correlated with the outcome, applied to all tumours before splitting, chooses features using the test set, and every accuracy computed afterwards is optimistic. This is the commonest way genomic prediction papers overstate their results (Ambroise and McLachlan, 2002). Our variance filter avoids the problem twice over: it ignores the labels, and it sees only the training tumours. It has a cost, visible in the output above: ERBB2 itself ranks 2,717th by variance and is not among the 1,000 genes kept. A filter that is honest about the outcome can still discard the most relevant feature. Every filtering step that looks at the outcome must be repeated inside each training fold, and every filter, honest or not, must be reported.

With 1,000 features and 250 tumours, least squares is not just unstable but undefined: there are infinitely many coefficient vectors that fit the training data perfectly. Even among pure noise features, some will be strongly correlated with the outcome by chance, and the more features there are, the stronger the best chance correlation. Check Yourself

A paper screens 20,000 genes in 80 patients, keeps the 50 most correlated with relapse, fits a model on those 50 using all 80 patients, and reports a cross-validated AUC of 0.95 computed by cross-validating only the final model. What is wrong?

Fifty predictors on 80 patients can be handled with a shrinkage prior.

The number of genes measured is not the problem in itself.

This is selection bias in cross-validation (Ambroise and McLachlan, 2002). Correlations of about 0.4 to 0.45 arise by chance among 20,000 noise genes with 80 patients, and a model built on the chance winners will "predict" its own training patients well. Only by repeating the whole pipeline, screening included, within each fold does cross-validation estimate performance on new patients.

The screening step used the outcomes of every patient, including those later held out, so the cross-validation never sees genes chosen without its test patients. With 20,000 genes, the top 50 by correlation will look predictive even if all are noise; the screening must be repeated inside each fold.

Project Connections: Many Features, Few Labels

The cancer gene-expression project lives in the regime of Section 10.8. Section 10.8 used 1,000 of the 20,530 genes; the horseshoe scales to all of them, though the sampler will need a few hours rather than minutes, and a common route is to keep all genes but use the regularised horseshoe with a deliberately small p0, then summarise with projection predictive selection. Subtype discovery itself is unsupervised, the subject of Chapter 11, but validating discovered subtypes against outcomes is this chapter's prediction problem. The AI-generated text detection project has the same shape: thousands of word, character and embedding features, a few thousand labelled documents, and a strong suspicion that only some features generalise across writing tasks. A horseshoe logistic model, evaluated on documents from tasks it was not trained on, says which features carry the signal and how sure the detector is about each document, which matters when a false accusation of using AI is costly. For EMS response equity, a horseshoe on many neighbourhood covariates is a good predictive baseline, but the equity question is causal: do not read its coefficients as effects of neighbourhood composition (Section 10.2).

10.9 Beyond linear models

Shrinkage priors work inside a linear model. Three families of Bayesian models go further, capturing nonlinearities and interactions without our specifying them, and still return a posterior over predictions.

Bayesian additive regression trees (BART; Chipman, George and McCulloch, 2010) write the regression function as a sum of m small trees, f(x)=∑k=1mTk(x), typically with m=200. The prior keeps each tree shallow (a split at depth d has probability 0.95(1+d)−2) and each leaf value small, so that every tree is a weak learner contributing a small adjustment. The posterior is explored by MCMC: each iteration updates one tree given all the others, proposing to grow, prune or change a split. In spirit BART is gradient boosting with a prior, and it returns predictive intervals and a count of how often each predictor is used in a split. We fit it to the same 100 houses with the dbarts package and compare its 90% predictive intervals with the horseshoe's.

Gaussian processes, met in Chapter 8, are the other main route to flexible Bayesian regression: a prior directly on the function, through a covariance kernel that says how similar the outcomes of two inputs should be. With an automatic relevance determination kernel (one length scale per input), a GP also learns which inputs matter. Its cost grows with the cube of the number of observations, so approximations are needed beyond a few thousand points; brms offers gp() terms with a low-rank approximation.

Bayesian neural networks put a prior on the weights of a neural network and report the posterior predictive distribution, averaging the predictions of many networks drawn from the posterior: p(ynew∣xnew,y)=∫p(ynew∣xnew,W)p(W∣y)dW. Radford Neal (1996) showed that HMC can sample these posteriors for small networks, and that as a one-hidden-layer network becomes infinitely wide, the prior over functions becomes a Gaussian process. For modern networks with millions of weights, exact sampling is out of reach: the posterior is high-dimensional and multimodal, with exact symmetries (swapping two hidden units leaves the function unchanged). Practice relies on approximations: variational inference on the weights (Chapter 9), Laplace approximations around a trained network, stochastic-gradient MCMC, and deep ensembles, a handful of networks trained from different random starts, which behave like a crude sample from the posterior and are often the best-calibrated option available (Lakshminarayanan, Pritzel and Blundell, 2017). The goal is the one this whole course has pursued: a flexible model that says, in calibrated terms, how much it does not know.

History: Neal's Networks and the Netflix Prize

In the early 1990s David MacKay (1992) applied Laplace approximations to neural networks and used the evidence to choose their regularisation. At the same time Radford Neal brought Hamiltonian Monte Carlo, invented by physicists for lattice field theory (Duane et al., 1987), into statistics in order to sample network weights (Neal, 1993), and developed the approach in his 1995 thesis, published as Bayesian Learning for Neural Networks (1996); the HMC that runs every model in this course descends from that work. Neal's networks won the NIPS 2003 feature-selection challenge, a competition built around data sets with thousands of features and few examples, using Bayesian neural networks with automatic relevance determination priors and Dirichlet diffusion trees. BART circulated as a working paper in the mid-2000s and was published in 2010; Jennifer Hill (2011) then showed that it makes an excellent engine for estimating causal effects, a use we return to in Chapter 13. Meanwhile the field moved toward point estimates from ever larger networks, and the Bayesian ideas returned in disguise: dropout, the most widely used regulariser of the 2010s, was later shown to be a form of approximate variational inference (Gal and Ghahramani, 2016).

Key Takeaways: Lecture 20

  • Judge prediction models out of sample, with a proper score and a calibration check. With few observations per parameter, PSIS-LOO is often unreliable (high Pareto-k^): use K-fold or a test set.
  • On Ames the horseshoe had the lowest error and the best log score on one split, but across ten splits it tied with the ridge. On TCGA it had a lower log loss than a normal prior but no better accuracy, and it put the signal on one gene instead of spreading it over a thousand; meff says how many coefficients a horseshoe effectively used.
  • Select variables by projection onto a reference model, not by which intervals exclude zero, and check the selected model on held-out data.
  • When features outnumber cases, chance correlations are large; every outcome-based filter must sit inside the cross-validation, and even a label-free filter can discard the most relevant feature.
  • BART, Gaussian processes and Bayesian neural networks extend Bayesian prediction to nonlinear functions; with small samples, a well-regularised linear model can still win.

  3. How much does the guess matter? Refit the horseshoe with guesses p0=1, 5 and 20 relevant predictors out of 39. Compare test RMSE and the posterior of meff. Is the horseshoe sensitive to p0?

  4. A prior on R². The R2D2 prior (Zhang et al., 2022) puts a prior directly on the model's R2 and splits the explained variance among coefficients with a Dirichlet distribution.     The posterior of the model's R2 is the parameter R2D2_R2.

    The R2D2 model's test RMSE is 0.178, between the lasso (0.181) and the horseshoe (0.176), and its posterior for the model's R2 has mean 0.80 (95% interval 0.67 to 0.90). A prior on R2 is often easier to choose than p0 because it is a statement about the outcome, not about the number of relevant predictors: most people can say whether a model of house prices will explain about half or about four-fifths of the variation, while few can say how many of 39 features matter. It also scales sensibly: adding more predictors spreads the same prior R2 more thinly, rather than adding prior signal with every new column. Here prior mean 0.5 was too low and the data moved it to 0.8 without difficulty.
