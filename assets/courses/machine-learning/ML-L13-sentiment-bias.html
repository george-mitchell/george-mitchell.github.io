The last three lectures built models that read text and images. This lecture closes the deep-learning part of the course by asking two questions about such models. First, a practical one: how do we get a machine to tell whether a piece of text is positive or negative, and how well do the different ways of doing it carry over from one kind of text to another? 1 — Sentiment as classification

"A lot of fun, if you can forgive the plot." "Not bad at all." "The worst film I have seen this year." People infer the writer's attitude from such sentences without effort; a machine needs a representation. The simplest is the bag of words: forget the order, and represent a document by the counts \(x_j\) of each word \(j\) of a vocabulary of size \(V\). It throws away a great deal ("not bad" and "bad, not" look the same), and yet it is a strong baseline. A refinement, TF-IDF, weights each count by the word's inverse document frequency \(\log(N/n_j)\), where \(n_j\) of the \(N\) documents contain word \(j\), so that words found in every document ("the", "film") count for little. Adding pairs of adjacent words ("bigrams") restores some order: "not bad" becomes a feature of its own.

data — the IMDb Large Movie Review dataset

50,000 movie reviews from the Internet Movie Database, half positive (rated 7–10 out of 10) and half negative (1–4), at most 30 reviews per film, collected by Maas et al. (2011) at Stanford. The original split has 25,000 reviews for training and 25,000 for testing; we use the CSV copy distributed on GitHub, which merges them, and split it 25,000/25,000 at random. The median review is 171 words long.

Two classifiers are natural for counts. The first is naive Bayes, a generative model: it models how each class produces words, assuming (naively) that the words of a document are drawn independently given its class, with class \(y\) using word \(j\) with probability \(\theta_{yj}\). The second is logistic regression (Lecture 4), which models \(P(y\mid x)\) directly. They turn out to be the same kind of classifier.

theorem 13.1 — naive Bayes is a linear classifier in log space

Under multinomial naive Bayes with class priors \(\pi_0,\pi_1\) and word probabilities \(\theta_{0j},\theta_{1j}\), the posterior log-odds of a document with word counts \(x\) is linear in \(x\):

$$\log\frac{P(y=1\mid x)}{P(y=0\mid x)}=\sum_{j=1}^V x_j\log\frac{\theta_{1j}}{\theta_{0j}}+\log\frac{\pi_1}{\pi_0}=w^\top x+b.$$

So naive Bayes and logistic regression choose from the same family of decision rules (hyperplanes in count space); they differ only in how they pick \(w\) and \(b\): naive Bayes from per-class word frequencies, logistic regression by maximising the conditional likelihood.

Under the model, a document of length \(n=\sum_jx_j\) has

$$P(x\mid y)=\frac{n!}{\prod_jx_j!}\prod_j\theta_{yj}^{x_j}.$$

By Bayes' rule, \(P(y\mid x)\propto\pi_yP(x\mid y)\), and in the ratio of the two classes the multinomial coefficient and the normalising constant \(P(x)\) cancel:

$$\frac{P(y=1\mid x)}{P(y=0\mid x)}=\frac{\pi_1\prod_j\theta_{1j}^{x_j}}{\pi_0\prod_j\theta_{0j}^{x_j}}.$$

Taking logarithms gives the formula, with \(w_j=\log(\theta_{1j}/\theta_{0j})\) and \(b=\log(\pi_1/\pi_0)\).

The same argument with binary features and Bernoulli naive Bayes gives a linear function of the presence indicators. \(\square\)

Bayes — smoothing is a prior, and the base rate is a prior too

The maximum-likelihood estimate \(\hat\theta_{yj}=c_{yj}/\sum_kc_{yk}\) (where \(c_{yj}\) counts word \(j\) in class \(y\)) is zero for any word never seen in class \(y\), and a single such word would then make the posterior of class \(y\) exactly zero. The standard fix, Laplace (add-\(\alpha\)) smoothing, \(\hat\theta_{yj}=(c_{yj}+\alpha)/(\sum_kc_{yk}+\alpha V)\), is the posterior mean of \(\theta_y\) under a symmetric Dirichlet(\(\alpha\)) prior (the beta–binomial calculation, in \(V\) dimensions). And the term \(\log(\pi_1/\pi_0)\) is the prior in the most literal sense: the same words give a different verdict when positive reviews are rare. Base rates will return in Section 7 as the source of the impossibility theorems.

Logistic regression on TF-IDF words and word pairs is right on 91.5% of the 25,000 test reviews; naive Bayes on raw counts, on 83.9%. The code checks Theorem 13.1 numerically: the log-odds from the library and from \(w^\top x+b\) agree to every printed digit. The two lists of top words show why naive Bayes loses. Its most decisive words are proper names: "Uwe" and "Boll" (a director of notoriously bad films), "Seagal", "MST3K" on the negative side, and "Ponyo", "DeVito", "Kolchak" on the positive. A name that appears in a few dozen reviews, nearly all of one class, gets a large weight \(\log(\theta_{1j}/\theta_{0j})\), because naive Bayes estimates each word's weight separately, as if the words were independent. Logistic regression fits all the weights jointly, so a word gets a large weight only if it adds information beyond the others, and its top features are the general words of praise and blame: "worst", "awful", "waste", "great", "excellent", "perfect", and the pairs "the worst" and "7 10" (reviewers rating a film 7/10). This is the classic contrast between generative and discriminative learning: naive Bayes approaches its (higher) asymptotic error with fewer examples, logistic regression reaches a lower error with enough data (Ng and Jordan, 2002).

spot check 13.1

A naive Bayes classifier has \(\log(\pi_1/\pi_0)=0\). A short review contains "great" once (\(w=+1.2\)), "boring" once (\(w=-1.5\)) and words whose weights sum to 0.6. What is its posterior log-odds of being positive?

Theorem 13.1: add the weights, each multiplied by its count, and the intercept.
\(1.2-1.5+0.6=0.3\), so \(P(\text{positive})=1/(1+e^{-0.3})=0.57\). Every word votes independently with a fixed weight; neither naive Bayes nor a bag-of-words logistic regression can learn that "boring" means something different after "never".
history — thumbs up?

Bo Pang, Lillian Lee and Shivakumar Vaithyanathan's "Thumbs up? Sentiment classification using machine learning techniques" (2002) took 1,400 movie reviews from the Internet Movie Database archive of the rec.arts.movies.reviews newsgroup, labelled them by the reviewers' star ratings, and showed that standard text classifiers (naive Bayes, maximum entropy, SVMs) beat lists of sentiment words chosen by people, while doing much worse on sentiment than on topic classification. The paper started the field of sentiment analysis in machine learning. Lexicons had a longer history: the General Inquirer, built at Harvard by Philip Stone and colleagues in the 1960s, tagged thousands of English words with categories such as "Positiv" and "Negativ", and is still in use. Maas et al.'s IMDb dataset (2011) and the Stanford Sentiment Treebank (Socher et al., 2013), with sentiment labels for every phrase of 11,855 sentences, became the benchmarks of the deep-learning era.

2 — Lexicons, learned models and domain shift

A sentiment lexicon is a list of words marked positive or negative, perhaps with strengths. Scoring a text by counting its positive and negative words needs no training data and is transparent, which is why lexicons remain popular in the social sciences and finance. Their weakness is that the meaning of a word depends on the domain. Loughran and McDonald (2011) found that almost three-quarters of the words that the Harvard General Inquirer classes as negative are not negative in companies' annual reports: "tax", "cost", "capital", "board", "liability", "vice" (as in vice-president). They built a lexicon for financial text, now standard in finance research. The same problem afflicts learned models: a classifier trained on movie reviews has learned the vocabulary of movie reviews. When the distribution of inputs at test time differs from that in training, we have domain shift.

data — the Financial PhraseBank

4,846 sentences from English-language financial news about companies listed on the Helsinki stock exchange, each labelled positive, negative or neutral "from the viewpoint of an investor" by 16 annotators with backgrounds in finance and business (Malo et al., 2014; CC BY-NC-SA 3.0). We use the 2,264 sentences on which all annotators agreed: 1,391 neutral, 570 positive and 303 negative. Lexicons: the Harvard General Inquirer (Harvard IV-4), VADER (Hutto and Gilbert, 2014; built for social media) and Loughran–McDonald's master dictionary, as distributed in the pysentiment2 and vaderSentiment Python packages.

On financial news, every general-purpose method struggles. Always answering "neutral" is right 61.4% of the time; the Harvard lexicon gets only 45.8% of the three-way labels right, because it finds sentiment where there is none: in neutral or good news it marks "order" (as in order intake), "capital", "board", "service", "deal" and "division" as negative. 3 — Pre-trained representations

Labelled data is expensive; unlabelled text is plentiful. The transfer-learning recipe of Lecture 12 is to learn a representation from unlabelled text and then fit a small model on top with few labels. The oldest version uses static word vectors: represent a review by the average of the GloVe vectors (Pennington, Socher and Manning, 2014; trained on six billion words of Wikipedia and newswire) of its words, and fit a logistic regression to the 100 averaged dimensions. A slightly richer version learns to weight the words: an attention-pooling classifier (Lecture 12, Section 3) computes a score for each word from its vector, turns the scores into weights with a softmax, and classifies the weighted average. The modern version fine-tunes a whole pre-trained transformer such as BERT, which we cannot download here; we report published numbers for it below.

# Transfer: pre-trained GloVe word vectors, from 100 labelled reviews to 25,000; and an attention-pooling classifier
import gzip, jax, jax.numpy as jnp
gv_words, gv_vecs = [], []
with gzip.open('data/glove100.gz', 'rt', encoding='utf8') as f:
    next(f)                                                    # header line: 400000 100
    for i, line in enumerate(f):
        if i == 100000: break                                  # the 100,000 most frequent words
        p = line.rstrip().split(' '); gv_words.append(p[0]); gv_vecs.append(np.array(p[1:], np.float32))
G = np.stack(gv_vecs); gidx = {w: i for i, w in enumerate(gv_words)}
print(f'GloVe: {G.shape[0]:,} words x {G.shape[1]} dimensions (trained on Wikipedia + Gigaword, 6 billion tokens)')
tokens = lambda s: re.findall(r"[a-z]+(?:'[a-z]+)?|[!?]", s.lower())
def glove_avg(texts):
    out = np.zeros((len(texts), 100), np.float32)
    for j, s in enumerate(texts):
        ix = [gidx[w] for w in tokens(s) if w in gidx]
        if ix: out[j] = G[ix].mean(0)
    return out

# Attention pooling over frozen GloVe vectors: weights a_i = softmax(v . tanh(W e_i)), document = sum_i a_i tanh(U e_i)
L_max = 300
def ids_of(texts):
    M = np.zeros((len(texts), L_max), np.int32); mask = np.zeros((len(texts), L_max), bool)
    for j, s in enumerate(texts):
        ix = [gidx[w] for w in tokens(s) if w in gidx][:L_max]; M[j, :len(ix)] = ix; mask[j, :len(ix)] = True
    return M, mask
I_tr, M_tr = ids_of(imdb.text.values[tr]); I_te, M_te = ids_of(imdb.text.values[te]); Gj = jnp.array(G)
k1, k2, k3 = jax.random.split(jax.random.PRNGKey(0), 3)
ap = {'W': jax.random.normal(k1, (100, 64)) * 0.1, 'v': jax.random.normal(k2, (64,)) * 0.1,
      'U': jax.random.normal(k3, (100, 128)) * 0.1, 'u': jnp.zeros(128), 'c': 0.0}
def attend(p, I, M):
    E = Gj[I]                                                    # (batch, L, 100), frozen
    s = jnp.where(M, jnp.tanh(E @ p['W']) @ p['v'], -1e9); a = jax.nn.softmax(s, axis=1)
    return a, jnp.einsum('bl,bld->bd', a, jnp.tanh(E @ p['U'])) @ p['u'] + p['c']
def ap_loss(p, I, M, yb):
    z = attend(p, I, M)[1]; return jnp.mean(jnp.logaddexp(0, z) - yb * z)
@jax.jit
def ap_step(p, m, v, t, I, M, yb):
    g = jax.grad(ap_loss)(p, I, M, yb)
    m = jax.tree_util.tree_map(lambda a, b: 0.9 * a + 0.1 * b, m, g); v = jax.tree_util.tree_map(lambda a, b: 0.999 * a + 0.001 * b * b, v, g)
    return jax.tree_util.tree_map(lambda q, a, b: q - 2e-3 * (a / (1 - 0.9 ** t)) / (jnp.sqrt(b / (1 - 0.999 ** t)) + 1e-8), p, m, v), m, v
m_, v_ = jax.tree_util.tree_map(jnp.zeros_like, ap), jax.tree_util.tree_map(jnp.zeros_like, ap); t = 0; rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
ap_pred = jax.jit(lambda p, I, M: attend(p, I, M)[1] > 0)
for ep in range(4):
    for b_ in np.array_split(rng.permutation(25000), 25000 // 64):
        t += 1; ap, m_, v_ = ap_step(ap, m_, v_, t, I_tr[b_], M_tr[b_], ytr[b_].astype(np.float32))
    acc = np.mean(np.concatenate([np.asarray(ap_pred(ap, I_te[i:i + 2500], M_te[i:i + 2500])) for i in range(0, 25000, 2500)]) == yte)
    print(f'attention pooling, epoch {ep + 1}: test accuracy {acc:.4f}')

4 — Bias in word embeddings

Word vectors learn meaning from co-occurrence (Lecture 12, Theorem 12.1), so they learn whatever associations the training text contains: that Paris is to France as Tokyo is to Japan, and also that some occupations are mentioned mostly alongside women and others alongside men. Bolukbasi et al. (2016) put it in their title: "Man is to computer programmer as woman is to homemaker?" Such associations matter because embeddings are inputs to systems that rank CVs, route customer complaints and flag content. Three tools measure them: analogies, projections onto a direction, and association tests.

But look at the second column. The standard analogy search excludes the three input words from the answers. When they are allowed, the nearest word to \(\text{doctor}-\text{man}+\text{woman}\) is "doctor" itself, and similarly for "programmer", "engineer" and "surgeon": the vectors say mainly that a woman doctor is a doctor. Projections onto a gender direction are a more direct measurement. Taking the first principal component of eight centred pairs (she/he, her/his, woman/man, …), which explains 74% of their variance, "nurse", "receptionist", "hairdresser" and "dancer" lie on the "she" side, and "manager", "architect", "engineer" and "mechanic" on the "he" side, mirroring occupational statistics (Caliskan et al. found a correlation of 0.90 between such projections and the percentage of women in each occupation in US labour statistics). Two cautions: "secretary" falls on the "he" side, because in news text "secretary" is mostly "Secretary of State"; and a single direction captures only part of how gender is encoded. Bolukbasi et al. proposed "debiasing" by removing the gender direction from words that should be neutral, but Gonen and Goldberg (2019) showed that the bias remained recoverable from the debiased vectors' neighbourhoods: projecting out one direction hides the symptom more than it removes the cause.

Analogies are a vivid but unreliable measuring instrument. A systematic test asks whether a whole set of target words is more strongly associated with one set of attribute words than another. Caliskan, Bryson and Narayanan (2017) adapted the Implicit Association Test of psychology (Greenwald, McGhee and Schwartz, 1998), which measures how much faster people pair, say, flowers with pleasant words than insects with pleasant words, into a test on vectors.

definition 13.1 — the Word-Embedding Association Test (WEAT)

Let \(X,Y\) be two sets of target words of equal size (flowers, insects) and \(A,B\) two sets of attribute words (pleasant, unpleasant). The association of a word \(w\) with the attributes is

$$s(w,A,B)=\frac1{|A|}\sum_{a\in A}\cos(w,a)-\frac1{|B|}\sum_{b\in B}\cos(w,b),$$

the test statistic is

$$S(X,Y,A,B)=\sum_{x\in X}s(x,A,B)-\sum_{y\in Y}s(y,A,B),$$

and the effect size is

$$d=\frac{\operatorname{mean}_{x\in X}s(x,A,B)-\operatorname{mean}_{y\in Y}s(y,A,B)}{\operatorname{sd}_{w\in X\cup Y}\,s(w,A,B)},$$

a standardised difference of means like Cohen's \(d\) (values around 0.8 and above are conventionally "large").

proposition 13.2 — the WEAT permutation test

Let \(\{X_i,Y_i\}\) range over all ways of splitting \(X\cup Y\) into two sets of equal size, and define the one-sided \(p\)-value

$$p=\Pr_i\big[S(X_i,Y_i,A,B)\ge S(X,Y,A,B)\big].$$

If the labels "\(X\)" and "\(Y\)" are exchangeable (the null hypothesis that the two target sets do not differ in their association with \(A\) and \(B\)), then \(\Pr(p\le\alpha)\le\alpha\) for every \(\alpha\in[0,1]\).

The same holds when \(p\) is estimated from \(M\) random splits as

$$(1+\#\{m:S_m\ge S\})/(M+1).$$

Condition on the multiset of association values \(\{s(w,A,B):w\in X\cup Y\}\). Under exchangeability, the observed split is equally likely to be any of the \(K=\binom{2n}{n}\) splits, so the observed statistic is a uniformly random draw from the list \(S_1,\dots,S_K\) of the statistics of all splits. Its rank from the top is then uniform, and \(p\) is its rank divided by \(K\). Hence \(\Pr(p\le k/K)\le k/K\) for each \(k\) (with equality when there are no ties), and so \(\Pr(p\le\alpha)\le\alpha\).

For the Monte Carlo version, under the null the observed statistic and the \(M\) random-split statistics are exchangeable, so the observed one is equally likely to occupy any of the \(M+1\) ranks, which gives the same bound for \((1+\#\{S_m\ge S\})/(M+1)\).

Note what the test does not say: the randomness is over which words were called \(X\), so it treats the word lists as given. A different, equally reasonable choice of words can change \(d\), and Ethayarajh, Duvenaud and Hirst (2019) showed how strongly WEAT results can depend on the choice of word lists. \(\square\)

5 — From representations to decisions

Associations in an embedding are not yet harm; harm happens when a system built on them treats people differently. The direct test is an audit with counterfactual inputs: feed the system inputs that differ only in a protected attribute, or a proxy for one such as a name, and compare the outputs. Kiritchenko and Mohammad (2018) built the Equity Evaluation Corpus of 8,640 template sentences for this purpose and found that many of 219 sentiment systems scored sentences with African-American names or female terms systematically differently. Speer (2017) showed that a sentiment classifier built in the obvious way on GloVe vectors rated "Let's go get Mexican food" well below "Let's go get Italian food". We can check both on our own models.

history — Gender Shades

In 2018 Joy Buolamwini and Timnit Gebru tested three commercial face-analysis systems (from IBM, Microsoft and Face++) on 1,270 photographs of parliamentarians from three African and three European countries, balanced by gender and skin type. Classifying gender, the systems erred on at most 0.8% of lighter-skinned men but on up to 34.7% of darker-skinned women. The benchmarks the companies had used were dominated by lighter-skinned faces, so their headline accuracies had hidden the gap. The companies released improved models within months, and a follow-up audit (Raji and Buolamwini, 2019) found the gaps had narrowed for the audited firms but not for others. The study made disaggregated evaluation (reporting performance separately for each group, and for intersections of groups) a standard part of responsible machine learning, and fed into the documentation practices of "datasheets for datasets" and "model cards".

6 — Fairness criteria

To say what a fair decision rule would be, fix notation. A model produces a score \(R\) (a risk, a probability) and perhaps a decision \(\hat Y\) (detain, approve); \(Y\) is the outcome (reoffends, repays); \(A\) is a protected attribute (race, sex). Most proposed definitions of group fairness are conditional independence statements among these three variables, and there are only three basic ones (Barocas, Hardt and Narayanan, 2023).

definition 13.2 — independence, separation, sufficiency
  • Independence (demographic or statistical parity): \(\hat Y\perp A\). Every group is selected at the same rate, \(P(\hat Y=1\mid A=a)\) the same for all \(a\).
  • Separation (equalised odds; Hardt, Price and Srebro, 2016): \(\hat Y\perp A\mid Y\). Equal true-positive rates and equal false-positive rates across groups. Requiring only equal true-positive rates is equal opportunity.
  • Sufficiency (calibration within groups): \(Y\perp A\mid R\). A given score means the same risk whatever the group: \(P(Y=1\mid R=r,A=a)\) does not depend on \(a\). For a binary decision this is predictive parity: equal positive predictive values \(P(Y=1\mid\hat Y=1,A=a)\) (and negative predictive values).

Each has an argument for it. Independence says outcomes should be shared out equally, whatever the base rates; it is the idea behind the "four-fifths rule" of US employment law, under which a selection rate for any group below 80% of the highest group's is evidence of adverse impact. Separation says people who will turn out the same way should be treated the same way: a person who would not have reoffended should not be more likely to be detained because of their group. Sufficiency says a score should mean what it says for everyone, so that a judge can read "70% risk" the same way for any defendant. In 2016 these three arguments collided in public.

7 — The impossibility theorems

The COMPAS numbers are not a coincidence of one tool. Two short theorems, found independently in 2016, show that when base rates differ between groups, no score or decision rule short of a perfect one can satisfy separation and sufficiency together.

theorem 13.3 — Chouldechova's identity (2017)

For a binary decision in a group with base rate \(p=P(Y=1)\), positive predictive value PPV, false-positive rate FPR and false-negative rate FNR,

$$\mathrm{FPR}=\frac{p}{1-p}\cdot\frac{1-\mathrm{PPV}}{\mathrm{PPV}}\cdot(1-\mathrm{FNR}).$$

Consequently, if two groups have different base rates and the same PPV, they cannot also have the same FPR and the same FNR (unless both rates are zero for one of them, or the PPV is 1).

Write the confusion matrix of a group of size \(N\) as counts TP, FP, FN, TN. Then \(\mathrm{TP}+\mathrm{FN}=pN\) and \(\mathrm{FP}+\mathrm{TN}=(1-p)N\), so \(\mathrm{TP}=(1-\mathrm{FNR})\,pN\) and \(\mathrm{FP}=\mathrm{FPR}\,(1-p)N\).

By definition \(\mathrm{PPV}=\mathrm{TP}/(\mathrm{TP}+\mathrm{FP})\), so

$$\mathrm{FP}=\mathrm{TP}\,(1-\mathrm{PPV})/\mathrm{PPV}.$$

Substituting,

$$\mathrm{FPR}\,(1-p)N=(1-\mathrm{FNR})\,pN\,\frac{1-\mathrm{PPV}}{\mathrm{PPV}},$$

and dividing by \((1-p)N\) gives the identity.

If the PPV and FNR are equal in two groups, the right-hand side differs between the groups through the factor \(p/(1-p)\), which is strictly increasing in \(p\); so the FPRs are equal only if the remaining factor \((1-\mathrm{PPV})(1-\mathrm{FNR})/\mathrm{PPV}\) is zero. \(\square\)

With the Broward numbers the identity holds to four decimal places (the code box checks it). Because more Black than white defendants were rearrested (52% against 39%), a tool with similar predictive values in both groups must have a higher false-positive rate for Black defendants. Kleinberg, Mullainathan and Raghavan proved the corresponding statement for scores rather than decisions.

theorem 13.4 — Kleinberg, Mullainathan and Raghavan (2017)

Consider a score \(S\in[0,1]\) and two groups with base rates \(p_1\ne p_2\). Suppose the score is

  • calibrated within groups: \(P(Y=1\mid S=s,\text{group }t)=s\);
  • balanced for the negative class: \(E[S\mid Y=0]\) is the same in both groups;
  • balanced for the positive class: \(E[S\mid Y=1]\) is the same in both groups.

Then the score is perfect: \(S=Y\) with probability 1. (Kleinberg et al. also show an approximate version: approximate satisfaction of all three forces approximately equal base rates or approximately perfect prediction.)

Step 1: calibration fixes the average score. In group \(t\), by the tower property,

$$E[S]=E\big[E[Y\mid S]\big]=E[Y]=p_t.$$

Writing \(x_t=E[S\mid Y=0]\) and \(y_t=E[S\mid Y=1]\) in group \(t\), the same mean is \((1-p_t)x_t+p_ty_t\), so

$$(1-p_t)\,x_t+p_t\,y_t=p_t,\qquad t=1,2.$$

Step 2: balance makes the unknowns common. With \(x_1=x_2=x\) and \(y_1=y_2=y\), both equations hold for the same \((x,y)\). Rearranged, each says \(x+p_t(y-x-1)=0\). Subtracting the two,

$$(p_1-p_2)(y-x-1)=0,$$

and since \(p_1\ne p_2\), \(y=x+1\). As \(x,y\in[0,1]\), this forces \(x=0\), \(y=1\).

Step 3: conclude. \(E[S\mid Y=0]=0\) with \(S\ge0\) means \(S=0\) whenever \(Y=0\), and \(E[S\mid Y=1]=1\) with \(S\le1\) means \(S=1\) whenever \(Y=1\), almost surely. \(\square\)

The theorems turn a political argument into arithmetic. ProPublica measured separation and found it violated; Northpointe replied that its scores satisfied sufficiency (Dieterich, Mendoza and Brennan, 2016), which our calibration table confirms to within sampling error. Both were right, and both could not be satisfied at once. Which criterion should give way depends on the decision and on whose interests are at stake, and that is not a question that mathematics can settle. Explore the trade-off yourself.

history — "Machine Bias" and the debate it started

On 23 May 2016 ProPublica published "Machine Bias", by Julia Angwin, Jeff Larson, Surya Mattu and Lauren Kirchner, under the subheading "There's software used across the country to predict future criminals. And it's biased against blacks." Northpointe's rebuttal argued that the tool was equally accurate for both groups. Within months, Alexandra Chouldechova (then at Carnegie Mellon), Jon Kleinberg, Sendhil Mullainathan and Manish Raghavan, and Sam Corbett-Davies and colleagues at Stanford independently showed that the disagreement was mathematically inevitable. In Wisconsin, the state Supreme Court ruled in State v. Loomis (2016) that judges may consider COMPAS scores at sentencing, provided they are warned of the tool's limitations. In 2018 Julia Dressel and Hany Farid showed that untrained people recruited online, given seven facts about each defendant, predicted reoffending about as accurately as COMPAS (67% against 65%), and that a logistic regression on two features, age and number of prior convictions, did as well as the tool's 137 inputs.

Separation fails: the model finds 75.1% of high-earning women but 81.2% of high-earning men, and its false-positive rate is higher for men (17.4% against 11.9%). Theorem 13.3 says this pattern is forced by the different base rates once the predictive values are (nearly) equal.

The first remedy, "fairness through unawareness", deletes the sex column. It changes little (women's true-positive rate moves from 75.1% to 77.3%), because the other features carry much of the same information: a model given only the other nine columns tells women from men with AUC 0.832. What it does change is calibration within groups: without the sex column the model can no longer adjust for it, and women's average score (36.7%) now exceeds their base rate (35.0%) while men's (45.0%) falls below theirs (46.4%). Removing a protected attribute from the inputs rarely removes it from the model, and it can make the scores mean different things for different groups. The second remedy, post-processing (Hardt, Price and Srebro, 2016), keeps the model and chooses group-specific thresholds to equalise a criterion. Lowering the threshold for women from 0.5 to 0.415 equalises the true-positive rates at 81.2% (equal opportunity) at a cost of only 0.09 points of accuracy; but women's false-positive rate rises from 11.9% to 15.5% and their positive predictive value falls from 77.3% to 73.8%, so the scores now mean different things for the two groups. Equal opportunity was bought with sufficiency, as the theorems said it must be. And using a person's sex or race explicitly to set their threshold is itself unlawful in some settings: the US Civil Rights Act of 1991, for example, forbids adjusting employment test scores or cut-offs by race, sex or national origin. Fairness interventions are made inside a legal framework, not outside it.

spot check 13.5

A bank removes race from its credit model's inputs and finds that the approval rates by race barely change. What is the most likely reason?

  • Other inputs (postcode, income, credit history, employer) are correlated with race, so the model reconstructs much of the same information from proxies.
  • Removing an input never changes a gradient-boosted model.
  • Race had no effect on the outcome in the training data.
  • The approval rates were measured on a different population.

big picture — sentiment, and audits in law

Sentiment analysis is a large industry. Brand-monitoring services score millions of social-media posts a day for companies; trading firms and data vendors turn news and earnings-call sentiment into market signals, often with Loughran–McDonald-style dictionaries alongside fine-tuned language models; platforms use related classifiers to flag toxic content, where the same name and dialect biases matter even more (Sap et al., 2019, found that tweets in African-American English were up to twice as likely to be flagged as offensive). Fairness audits have moved from research into law. New York City's Local Law 144, enforced since July 2023, requires employers who use automated employment decision tools to commission an independent bias audit every year, publish the selection rates and impact ratios by sex and race or ethnicity (and their intersections), and notify candidates. The EU's AI Act (Regulation 2024/1689) classes systems used in employment, credit, education and access to essential services as high-risk and requires their providers to examine training data for possible biases; under the 2026 "Digital Omnibus" amendment (Regulation 2026/1744), those obligations apply from 2 December 2027.

9 — Causal notions of fairness

The three criteria of Section 6 are statistical: they are properties of the joint distribution of \(R\), \(Y\) and \(A\). Hardt, Price and Srebro (2016) pointed out their limit: two scenarios can produce exactly the same joint distribution while differing in whether the decision is intuitively fair, because they differ in why \(A\) and \(Y\) are related. Whether a disparity is unfair depends on the causal path that produces it. The 1973 graduate admissions at Berkeley are the classic example (Bickel, Hammel and O'Connell, 1975): women were admitted at a lower rate overall, but within departments their rates were similar or higher, because women applied more often to the departments that admitted fewer applicants. The path from sex to admission through choice of department was widely judged acceptable; a direct path would not have been. Answering such questions needs the causal models of Lectures 3, 4 and 8.

definition 13.3 — counterfactual fairness (Kusner, Loftus, Russell and Silva, 2017)

Given a structural causal model with background variables \(U\), a predictor \(\hat Y\) is counterfactually fair if, for every individual (every \(x\) and \(a\)) and every \(y\) and \(a'\),

$$P\big(\hat Y_{A\leftarrow a}(U)=y\mid X=x,A=a\big)=P\big(\hat Y_{A\leftarrow a'}(U)=y\mid X=x,A=a\big):$$

the prediction would have been the same had the individual's protected attribute been different, with everything not caused by it held fixed.

Computing it follows Pearl's three steps: infer the background variables from what we observe (abduction), set \(A\) to \(a'\) (action), and recompute the features and the prediction (prediction).

A predictor that uses only variables that are not descendants of \(A\) is automatically counterfactually fair.

Path-specific fairness relaxes the requirement to some causal paths from \(A\), treating others, like the choice of department, as legitimate (Kilbertus et al., 2017; Nabi and Shpitser, 2018).

code — counterfactual fairness in a world whose causal model we know 
# Counterfactual fairness in a simulated world where we know the causal model (after Kusner et al., 2017)
from sklearn.linear_model import LinearRegression
r = np.random.default_rng(0); N = 20000
A = r.integers(0, 2, N)                           # group membership
K = r.normal(0, 1, N)                             # unobserved "knowledge", independent of A
e1, e2, e3 = r.normal(0, 0.6, (3, N))
def world(A):                                     # structural equations: the test and the grades are depressed for group 1
    X1 = K - 1.0 * A + e1; X2 = K - 0.5 * A + e2; return np.c_[X1, X2]
X = world(A); Yv = K + e3                         # the outcome depends only on K
Xcf = world(1 - A)                                # counterfactual: same people, same K and noise, group flipped
res = lambda X_, A_: X_ - np.c_[[X[A == a].mean(0) for a in (0, 1)]][A_]   # residuals of the features given the group
models = {'aware (features and group)': (np.c_[X, A], np.c_[Xcf, 1 - A]), 'unaware (features only)': (X, Xcf),
          'counterfactually fair (residuals)': (res(X, A), res(Xcf, 1 - A))}
print(f'{"predictor":36s} {"MSE":>6s} {"mean prediction, group 0 / 1":>30s} {"mean |change| if group flipped":>31s}')
for name, (Z, Zcf) in models.items():
    m = LinearRegression().fit(Z, Yv); yh, ycf = m.predict(Z), m.predict(Zcf)
    print(f'{name:36s} {np.mean((yh - Yv) ** 2):6.3f} {yh[A == 0].mean():+14.3f} / {yh[A == 1].mean():+.3f} {np.mean(np.abs(ycf - yh)):31.3f}')
predictor                               MSE   mean prediction, group 0 / 1  mean |change| if group flipped
aware (features and group)            0.511         +0.006 / -0.005                           0.000
unaware (features only)               0.594         +0.268 / -0.267                           0.525
counterfactually fair (residuals)     0.511         +0.000 / +0.000                           0.011

In the simulated world, the outcome depends only on an unobserved "knowledge" \(K\), which is the same in both groups; but group 1's test scores and grades are depressed (by 1.0 and 0.5) by barriers unrelated to knowledge. The unaware model, which uses the scores and grades but not the group, is the unfair one: it predicts 0.53 lower for group 1 than for group 0 on average, and flipping a person's group (holding their knowledge and luck fixed) would change their prediction by 0.53 on average. The aware linear model, given the group, learns to add back the penalty and is counterfactually fair, with lower error. The third predictor uses the residuals of the features after removing their group means, \(X-E[X\mid A]\), which in this additive model are functions of \(K\) and the noise alone: it is counterfactually fair by construction (the 0.011 is estimation error in the group means) and just as accurate. The example is idealised, since a real causal model is never known this precisely, but it makes the point that blindness to a protected attribute can produce exactly the unfairness it was meant to avoid.

causal — why fairness is ultimately a causal question

Every number in this lecture's audits depends on the label \(Y\), and labels are measurements. COMPAS's "reoffended" is a new arrest, and arrests depend on where police patrol; if one group is policed more heavily, the same behaviour produces more arrests, the base rates of Theorem 13.3 are partly artefacts of measurement, and a perfectly calibrated score inherits the distortion. Obermeyer, Powers, Vogeli and Mullainathan (2019) found the same mechanism in a widely used US health-care algorithm: it predicted future medical costs as a proxy for medical need, and because less money was spent on Black patients with the same illnesses, Black patients had to be considerably sicker than white patients to receive the same score. Changing the label to a direct measure of health would have raised the share of Black patients flagged for extra care from 17.7% to 46.5%. No statistical criterion computed from \((R,Y,A)\) can detect a biased \(Y\); asking what the label measures, and along which paths the protected attribute affects features, outcomes and measurements, is causal reasoning.

10 — Summary, exercises, projects, reading

summary
  • Naive Bayes is a linear classifier in log space (Theorem 13.1); on IMDb, discriminative logistic regression on TF-IDF features beats it (91.5% against 83.9%), because naive Bayes over-weights rare words such as proper names.
  • Lexicons and learned models carry their domain with them: on financial news, general lexicons misread "capital" and "board", a finance lexicon misses most positive news, and a movie-review model is barely better than chance; a small in-domain model does best.
  • Averaged static word vectors lose to TF-IDF even with 100 labels; attention pooling recovers part of the loss; contextual models make transfer work: DistilBERT, fine-tuned on other film reviews, gets 89.6% of IMDb reviews right with no IMDb labels, and FinBERT's 97.2% on the PhraseBank is inflated because it was trained on it.
  • Word embeddings encode social associations. Analogies overstate them; projections and the WEAT (Definition 13.1, with an exact permutation test, Proposition 13.2) measure them: all six of Caliskan et al.'s tests reproduce on GloVe.
  • Independence, separation and sufficiency (Definition 13.2) formalise three notions of group fairness. When base rates differ, separation and sufficiency are incompatible for any imperfect predictor (Theorems 13.3 and 13.4). COMPAS is calibrated but has twice the false-positive rate for Black defendants; the Lecture 8 income model is calibrated but finds fewer high-earning women.
  • Deleting a protected attribute ("unawareness") changes little because of proxies; post-processing can equalise a chosen criterion at the cost of others. Counterfactual fairness (Definition 13.3) and path-specific fairness ask the causal question that statistics alone cannot answer, including whether the labels themselves are biased.

exercises
  1. A naive Bayes classifier is trained on four documents: positive "great fun", "great acting"; negative "boring plot", "great bore". With vocabulary {great, fun, acting, boring, plot, bore} and add-one smoothing, compute \(\theta_{yj}\) for "great" in both classes and the log-odds of the new document "great plot".

    Each class has 4 word tokens and \(V=6\), so \(\theta_{yj}=(c_{yj}+1)/10\). Positive: great 3/10, plot 1/10. Negative: great 2/10, plot 2/10. Priors are equal. Log-odds \(=\log\frac{0.3}{0.2}+\log\frac{0.1}{0.2}=0.405-0.693=-0.288\): slightly negative, because "plot" appeared only in a negative review.

  2. Show that if base rates differ between groups (\(Y\not\perp A\)), no score can satisfy both independence (\(R\perp A\)) and sufficiency (\(Y\perp A\mid R\)).

    Sufficiency gives \(P(Y=1\mid A=a)=\int P(Y=1\mid R=r)\,dP(r\mid A=a)\), with the integrand not depending on \(a\). Independence makes the distribution of \(R\) the same for every \(a\). Then \(P(Y=1\mid A=a)\) is the same for every \(a\), contradicting \(Y\not\perp A\). (In the language of conditional independence, \(R\perp A\) and \(Y\perp A\mid R\) together imply \(Y\perp A\).)

  3. Show that a binary decision satisfying both independence and separation in two groups with different base rates must be independent of the outcome, \(\hat Y\perp Y\) (a useless predictor).

    With common TPR and FPR, the selection rate in group \(a\) is \(\mathrm{TPR}\,p_a+\mathrm{FPR}\,(1-p_a)=\mathrm{FPR}+p_a(\mathrm{TPR}-\mathrm{FPR})\). Independence requires this to be the same for \(p_1\ne p_2\), so \(\mathrm{TPR}=\mathrm{FPR}\): the decision is positive with the same probability whatever the outcome.

  4. A WEAT has \(X=\{x_1,x_2\}\), \(Y=\{y_1,y_2\}\) with associations \(s(x_1)=0.10\), \(s(x_2)=0.06\), \(s(y_1)=-0.02\), \(s(y_2)=0.02\). Compute the effect size (sample standard deviation over all four words) and the exact one-sided permutation \(p\)-value.

    Means 0.08 and 0.00; the four values have mean 0.04 and sample standard deviation \(\sqrt{(0.0036+0.0004+0.0036+0.0004)/3}=0.0516\), so \(d=0.08/0.0516=1.55\). There are \(\binom42=6\) splits; the statistic \(S=\sum_Xs-\sum_Ys\) is 0.16 for the observed split and smaller for every other (the next largest is 0.08), so \(p=1/6\). With two words per set no result can be significant at 5%: the smallest possible \(p\) is \(1/6\).

  5. Using the COMPAS calibration table, explain why a single threshold applied to both groups gives the same positive predictive value only approximately, and why moving the white defendants' threshold down by one decile raises their false-positive rate.

    The PPV at threshold \(t\) is the average rearrest rate over deciles \(\ge t\), weighted by the number of defendants in each decile. Even if the rate in each decile were identical for the two groups, the weights differ (white defendants are concentrated in low deciles), so the averages differ: 65.0% against 59.5% at \(t=5\). Lowering the threshold adds a decile of defendants, among whom those not rearrested become new false positives, so the FPR can only rise.

  6. In the additive model \(X=K+\beta A+\varepsilon\) with \(K,\varepsilon\) independent of \(A\), show that \(\tilde X=X-E[X\mid A]\) is unchanged by the intervention \(A\leftarrow a'\) (holding \(K\) and \(\varepsilon\) fixed), so any predictor built from \(\tilde X\) is counterfactually fair. Why does this fail if \(\beta\) varies from person to person?

    \(E[X\mid A]=E[K]+\beta A+E[\varepsilon]\), so \(\tilde X=(K-E[K])+(\varepsilon-E[\varepsilon])\), which does not involve \(A\). If the effect \(\beta_i\) differs by person, \(X-E[X\mid A]=K+\varepsilon+(\beta_i-\bar\beta)A-\text{const}\) still depends on \(A\) through \((\beta_i-\bar\beta)A\), so flipping \(A\) changes it. Counterfactual fairness then needs the individual-level model, which observational data rarely identify.
