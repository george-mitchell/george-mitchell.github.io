Lecture 14 listed four kinds of structure in unlabelled data; this lecture takes the second, groups. A clustering divides the data so that points in the same group are more alike than points in different groups. It is the most used unsupervised method and the least well defined: "alike" needs a similarity and "groups" a scale. We meet four families (centroids, trees, density, graphs) and end with a theorem: no clustering method has three properties that each seem indispensable.

By the end you should be able to run Lloyd's algorithm and k-means++ and prove that Lloyd's algorithm terminates; choose \(k\) three ways and explain why they disagree; read a dendrogram and the effect of the linkage; explain DBSCAN's core, border and noise points and what HDBSCAN adds; derive spectral clustering from the normalised cut, using the Laplacian of Lecture 9; and state Kleinberg's impossibility theorem.

1 — k-means

The simplest description of a group is its centre: summarise \(n\) points by \(k\) centres, minimising the squared distance from each point to its nearest centre.

definition 15.1 — the k-means objective

Let \(x_1,\dots,x_n\in\mathbb R^d\) and let \(k\) be an integer. The k-means problem is to choose

  • centres \(\mu_1,\dots,\mu_k\), and
  • an assignment \(c(i)\in\{1,\dots,k\}\) of each point to a cluster,

to minimise the within-cluster sum of squares

$$J(c,\mu)=\sum_{i=1}^n\lVert x_i-\mu_{c(i)}\rVert^2.$$

For fixed centres, the best assignment sends each point to its nearest centre. For a fixed assignment, the best centre of each cluster is the mean (centroid) of its points.

Lloyd's algorithm alternates these two steps from some starting centres until the assignment stops changing.

idea — shops and customers

Customers choose the nearest of \(k\) shops; each shop moves to the average position of its customers; repeat. The total walking only falls (Theorem 15.1), but two shops that start in one neighbourhood may stay there while a distant one shares a single shop: a local minimum. Squared distance also favours round clusters of similar spread, and lets one far-away point pull a centre a long way.

theorem 15.1 — Lloyd's algorithm decreases the objective and terminates

Each assignment step and each update step of Lloyd's algorithm leaves \(J\) unchanged or lowers it.

If ties in the assignment step are broken in favour of a point's current cluster, the algorithm reaches a partition that no step changes after finitely many iterations (at most the number of partitions of \(n\) points into \(k\) groups).

Assignment. With the centres fixed, \(J\) is a sum of separate terms \(\lVert x_i-\mu_{c(i)}\rVert^2\), each minimised by the nearest centre. The old assignment is one of the options, so \(J\) cannot rise.

Update. With the assignment fixed, \(J\) splits into one term per cluster \(S_j\). Writing \(\bar x_j\) for the mean of \(S_j\),

$$\sum_{i\in S_j}\lVert x_i-\mu\rVert^2=\sum_{i\in S_j}\lVert x_i-\bar x_j\rVert^2+|S_j|\,\lVert\bar x_j-\mu\rVert^2,$$

because the cross term vanishes:

$$2(\bar x_j-\mu)^\top\sum_{i\in S_j}(x_i-\bar x_j)=0.$$

So the mean is the unique minimiser, and \(J\) cannot rise.

Termination. After each update the centres are the means of the current partition, so \(J\) is a function of the partition alone. With the tie rule, an assignment step that changes the partition moves some point to a strictly nearer centre, so \(J\) falls strictly; the following update does not raise it.

Hence no partition occurs twice, and there are finitely many partitions. \(\square\)

The theorem promises a local minimum, a partition neither step can improve, not the global one. The global problem is hard.

theorem 15.2 — k-means is NP-hard

Finding the partition that minimises \(J\) is NP-hard:

  • even for \(k=2\) clusters in general dimension (Aloise et al., 2009; Dasgupta, 2008);
  • even for points in the plane when \(k\) is part of the input (Mahajan, Nimbhorkar and Varadarajan, 2012).

(Statement.)

So we run Lloyd's algorithm from several starts and keep the best. A point far from every centre chosen so far probably lies in a cluster with no centre yet, so k-means++ favours it, with enough randomness that a lone outlier is not always chosen.

theorem 15.3 — k-means++ seeding (Arthur and Vassilvitskii, 2007)

Choose the centres as follows.

  • The first centre is uniformly random from the data.
  • Each further centre is drawn from the data with probability proportional to \(D(x)^2\), the squared distance from \(x\) to the nearest centre already chosen.

Then, before any Lloyd iterations,

$$\mathbb E\,J_{\text{k-means++}}\;\le\;8(\ln k+2)\,J_{\text{opt}}.$$

(Statement. Lloyd's algorithm started from these centres can only lower \(J\) further, by Theorem 15.1.)

Our first data set is a city's bike-share system in its first winter. A station's character shows in when people ride from it (home stations empty in the morning, office stations in the evening, leisure stations at weekends and late at night), so we describe each station by the shape of its hourly departures.

data — Citi Bike trips, January 2014

All 300,400 Citi Bike trips that started in January 2014 (one row per trip: start and end times, stations with coordinates, rider type), from the operator's public system data, via the copy in the GitHub repository Tmills1/Citibike-Tripdata; the official Amazon S3 archive was not reachable from the build environment. The system then had about 330 stations, in Manhattan below 60th Street and western Brooklyn. We keep the 290 stations with at least 200 departures and describe each by 48 numbers: average departures per day in each hour on weekdays (23 days, including New Year's Day and Martin Luther King Jr Day) and weekends (8 days), divided by their total so that only the shape remains. January is cold, so weekend leisure trips are scarcer than in summer.

def lloyd(X, C, max_iter=100):                              # Lloyd's algorithm from given starting centres
    obj = []
    for _ in range(max_iter):
        d2 = ((X[:, None, :] - C[None]) ** 2).sum(-1); lab = d2.argmin(1)
        obj.append(d2[np.arange(len(X)), lab].sum())
        Cn = np.array([X[lab == j].mean(0) if np.any(lab == j) else C[j] for j in range(len(C))])
        if np.allclose(Cn, C): break
        C = Cn
    return lab, C, obj
def kmeanspp(X, k, rng):                                    # k-means++ seeding (Arthur and Vassilvitskii, 2007)
    C = [X[rng.integers(len(X))]]
    for _ in range(k - 1):
        d2 = ((X[:, None, :] - np.array(C)[None]) ** 2).sum(-1).min(1)
        C.append(X[rng.choice(len(X), p=d2 / d2.sum())])
    return np.array(C)

lab, C, obj = lloyd(Xc, kmeanspp(Xc, k, rng))

for s in range(50):
    r = np.random.default_rng(100 + s)

                           f"within 0.1% of the best found in {np.mean(v < best * 1.001):.0%}")

lab, C, obj = min(runs, key=lambda t: t[2][-1])             # keep the best of 20 runs
peak = lambda c: int(np.argmax(c[:24]))

spot check 15.1

The points 0, 2 and 10 on a line are clustered with \(k=2\), starting from centres at 0 and 10. After one assignment step and one update step, what is \(J\)?

Assign, average, then sum the squared distances.
Clusters {0, 2} and {10}; centres 1 and 10; \(J=1^2+1^2+0^2=2\). Nothing changes after this, and it is also the global minimum.
spot check 15.2

k-means++ has chosen its first centre. The three remaining points are at distances 1, 2 and 3 from it. What is the probability that the point at distance 3 becomes the second centre?

\(3^2/(1^2+2^2+3^2)=9/14\approx0.64\), against \(1/3\) for uniform seeding.

k-means also compresses. Replace each point by its nearest centre and the data become \(k\) centres plus an index of \(\log_2k\) bits per point. This is vector quantisation, the purpose of Lloyd's memo, and how GIF and PNG-8 reduce an image to a palette of \(k\) colours. Quality is the peak signal-to-noise ratio (PSNR): largest possible pixel value squared over mean squared error, in decibels (10 log₁₀ of the ratio).

2 — Choosing k

\(J\) always falls as \(k\) grows (it is zero at \(k=n\)), so it cannot choose \(k\). Three heuristics look for where more clusters stop paying.

  • The elbow: plot the minimum \(W_k\) of \(J\) against \(k\) and look for a bend after which the decrease flattens (diminishing returns).
  • The silhouette (Rousseeuw, 1987): is each point much closer to its own cluster than to the next nearest one (Definition 15.2)?
  • The gap statistic (Tibshirani, Walther and Hastie, 2001): compare \(\log W_k\) with its expected value for data with no clusters, simulated uniformly over a box aligned with the data's principal axes; choose the smallest \(k\) at which the gap stops growing by more than its simulation error.
definition 15.2 — silhouette

Let point \(i\) lie in cluster \(A\).

  • \(a(i)\) is its mean distance to the other points of \(A\).
  • \(b(i)\) is the smallest, over other clusters \(B\), of its mean distance to the points of \(B\).

The silhouette of \(i\) is

$$s(i)=\frac{b(i)-a(i)}{\max\{a(i),b(i)\}}\in[-1,1],$$

and the silhouette of a clustering is the mean of \(s(i)\) over all points.

Values near 1 mean tight, well-separated clusters; near 0, points on a boundary; negative, points nearer another cluster than their own.

code — three ways to choose k for the station profiles 
# Choosing k for the station profiles: elbow, silhouette and the gap statistic
from sklearn.metrics import silhouette_score
def km_fit(X, kk, seed=0): return KMeans(kk, n_init=10, random_state=seed).fit(X)
ks = range(2, 9); W = {}; sil = {}
for kk in ks:
    m = km_fit(Xc, kk); W[kk] = m.inertia_; sil[kk] = silhouette_score(Xc, m.labels_)
# gap statistic (Tibshirani, Walther and Hastie, 2001): compare log W_k with uniform data in the principal-axes box
rg = np.random.default_rng(1); mu = Xc.mean(0); U = np.linalg.svd(Xc - mu, full_matrices=False)[2]
Z = (Xc - mu) @ U.T; lo, hi = Z.min(0), Z.max(0); B = 10
refW = {kk: [np.log(km_fit(rg.uniform(lo, hi, Z.shape) @ U + mu, kk, b).inertia_) for b in range(B)] for kk in ks}
gap = {kk: np.mean(refW[kk]) - np.log(W[kk]) for kk in ks}
sk = {kk: np.std(refW[kk]) * np.sqrt(1 + 1 / B) for kk in ks}
print(" k   W_k (within SS)   drop     silhouette   gap     gap s.e.")
for kk in ks:

    gap[kk] >= gap[kk + 1] - sk[kk + 1] for kk in list(ks)[:-1]) else None

3 — Hierarchical clustering and dendrograms

Instead of one \(k\), we can build the whole nested family. Agglomerative clustering starts with every point alone and repeatedly merges the two closest clusters. The merges form a binary tree, the dendrogram, like a family tree drawn upside down: two branches join at the distance at which their groups merged. A horizontal cut gives a clustering; raising it gives fewer, larger clusters.

definition 15.3 — linkage

The linkage defines the distance between clusters \(A\) and \(B\) from point distances \(d\):

  • single: the closest pair, \(\min_{a\in A,b\in B}d(a,b)\);
  • complete: the farthest pair, \(\max_{a\in A,b\in B}d(a,b)\);
  • average: the mean of all \(|A||B|\) pairwise distances;
  • Ward (1963): the increase in the within-cluster sum of squares caused by merging,
$$\Delta(A,B)=\frac{|A||B|}{|A|+|B|}\lVert\bar x_A-\bar x_B\rVert^2.$$

Single linkage is "a friend of a friend is a friend": it follows curved clusters, but a few bridging points join two groups, and it often leaves one giant cluster plus outliers. Complete linkage wants everyone close to everyone (tight, round groups); average is in between. Ward's greedily minimises the k-means objective, giving compact clusters of similar size. The cophenetic correlation, the correlation between \(d(i,j)\) and the height at which \(i\) and \(j\) first join, measures how faithfully a dendrogram represents the distances.

The indicators below are first standardised (mean 0, standard deviation 1), so that GDP in dollars does not dominate the distances.

history — Lloyd's memo, Ward's tree, and a test of time

Stuart Lloyd devised the alternating algorithm at Bell Labs in 1957, in an internal memo on quantising signals for pulse-code modulation; it circulated for 25 years before appearing as "Least squares quantization in PCM" (IEEE Transactions on Information Theory, 1982). Hugo Steinhaus had proposed the objective in 1956, Edward Forgy published the same algorithm in 1965, and James MacQueen coined the name "k-means" in 1967. Joe Ward Jr., a statistician with the US Air Force, published his minimum-variance merging rule in 1963 (Journal of the American Statistical Association), and Robert Sokal and Peter Sneath's Principles of Numerical Taxonomy (1963) made dendrograms the standard picture in biology. DBSCAN, by Martin Ester, Hans-Peter Kriegel, Jörg Sander and Xiaowei Xu (KDD 1996), received the KDD test-of-time award in 2014, and is among the most cited papers in data mining.

4 — Density-based clustering: DBSCAN and HDBSCAN

k-means and Ward's linkage look for round groups and place every point somewhere. Alternatively (Lecture 14), a cluster is a high-density region separated from others by low density, and points in low-density regions belong to no cluster.

definition 15.4 — DBSCAN

Fix a radius \(\varepsilon\) and a count minPts.

  • A point is a core point if at least minPts points (itself included) lie within distance \(\varepsilon\) of it.
  • Two core points are connected if they are within \(\varepsilon\) of each other.
  • A cluster is a maximal set of core points linked by chains of such connections, together with every non-core point within \(\varepsilon\) of one of them (a border point).
  • Points that are neither core nor border are noise.

DBSCAN is single linkage restricted to dense points: clusters may take any shape, like a crowd that snakes as easily as it huddles, but chains must pass through core points, so a thin line of stray points no longer bridges two groups. The parameters set a density threshold (a box-kernel density estimate compared with a fixed level). To choose \(\varepsilon\), sort each point's distance to its \((\text{minPts}-1)\)-th neighbour and look for the knee. HDBSCAN (Campello, Moulavi and Sander, 2013) removes \(\varepsilon\): it builds the hierarchy of DBSCAN clusterings over all density levels, as a dendrogram, and keeps the clusters that persist over the widest range of levels, subject to a minimum cluster size.

The adjusted Rand index (ARI) scores agreement between two clusterings over all pairs of points, corrected for chance: 1 means identical, about 0 means chance.

5 — Spectral clustering

Lecture 9 built graphs whose nodes are data points and whose edges join similar points, with the graph Laplacian \(L=D-A\) (\(A\) the weight matrix, \(D\) the diagonal matrix of degrees). Clustering becomes a graph problem: cut few edges, without cheating by cutting off one lonely node.

definition 15.5 — normalised cut

Let a graph have weights \(w_{ij}\) and degrees \(d_i=\sum_jw_{ij}\). For a split of the nodes into \(A\) and its complement \(\bar A\), set

$$\operatorname{cut}(A,\bar A)=\sum_{i\in A,\,j\in\bar A}w_{ij},\qquad \operatorname{vol}(A)=\sum_{i\in A}d_i.$$

\(\operatorname{vol}(A)\) is the volume of \(A\). The normalised cut (Shi and Malik, 2000) is

$$\operatorname{Ncut}(A,\bar A)=\operatorname{cut}(A,\bar A)\left(\frac1{\operatorname{vol}(A)}+\frac1{\operatorname{vol}(\bar A)}\right).$$

The volume terms penalise unbalanced splits: cutting off one weak node has a small cut but a large \(1/\operatorname{vol}(A)\). Minimising Ncut is NP-hard; a relaxation makes it an eigenvalue problem.

theorem 15.4 — spectral clustering relaxes the normalised cut

For a split \((A,\bar A)\) define \(f\in\mathbb R^n\) by

$$f_i=\sqrt{\frac{\operatorname{vol}(\bar A)}{\operatorname{vol}(A)}}\ (i\in A),\qquad f_i=-\sqrt{\frac{\operatorname{vol}(A)}{\operatorname{vol}(\bar A)}}\ (i\in\bar A).$$

Then

$$f^\top D\mathbf 1=0\qquad\text{and}\qquad\operatorname{Ncut}(A,\bar A)=\frac{f^\top Lf}{f^\top Df}.$$

Minimising the right-hand side over all real \(f\) with \(f^\top D\mathbf 1=0\), instead of over two-valued \(f\), is solved by the eigenvector of \(Lf=\lambda Df\) with the second-smallest eigenvalue.

Splitting the nodes by the sign of that eigenvector (or running k-means on the first few eigenvectors, for more than two clusters) is spectral clustering.

Write \(V=\operatorname{vol}(A)+\operatorname{vol}(\bar A)\), and let \(\alpha\) and \(\beta\) be the two values of \(|f_i|\):

$$\alpha=\sqrt{\operatorname{vol}(\bar A)/\operatorname{vol}(A)},\qquad\beta=\sqrt{\operatorname{vol}(A)/\operatorname{vol}(\bar A)}.$$

Orthogonality. Both terms below equal \(\sqrt{\operatorname{vol}(A)\operatorname{vol}(\bar A)}\), so

$$f^\top D\mathbf 1=\alpha\operatorname{vol}(A)-\beta\operatorname{vol}(\bar A)=0.$$

Denominator.

$$f^\top Df=\alpha^2\operatorname{vol}(A)+\beta^2\operatorname{vol}(\bar A)=\operatorname{vol}(\bar A)+\operatorname{vol}(A)=V.$$

Numerator. The Laplacian's quadratic form (Lecture 9) is \(f^\top Lf=\tfrac12\sum_{i,j}w_{ij}(f_i-f_j)^2\). Only pairs across the cut contribute, each with \((f_i-f_j)^2=(\alpha+\beta)^2\). Since \(\alpha+\beta=V/\sqrt{\operatorname{vol}(A)\operatorname{vol}(\bar A)}\),

$$f^\top Lf=\operatorname{cut}(A,\bar A)\,(\alpha+\beta)^2=\operatorname{cut}(A,\bar A)\,\frac{V^2}{\operatorname{vol}(A)\operatorname{vol}(\bar A)}.$$

Dividing by \(f^\top Df=V\) gives

$$\frac{f^\top Lf}{f^\top Df}=\operatorname{cut}(A,\bar A)\,\frac{V}{\operatorname{vol}(A)\operatorname{vol}(\bar A)}=\operatorname{Ncut}(A,\bar A).$$

Relaxation. The smallest eigenvalue of \(Lf=\lambda Df\) is 0, with eigenvector \(\mathbf 1\). By the Rayleigh–Ritz (Courant–Fischer) theorem, the minimum of \(f^\top Lf/f^\top Df\) over \(f\) that are \(D\)-orthogonal to \(\mathbf 1\) is the second eigenvalue, attained at its eigenvector.

The relaxed minimum is a lower bound on the minimum Ncut; how close the rounded split comes to the optimum depends on the graph (von Luxburg, 2007, §5). \(\square\)

Two facts make the method work. If the graph has \(c\) components, the eigenvalue 0 has multiplicity \(c\) with eigenvectors constant on each component, so clusters can be read off exactly, and small perturbations move the eigenvectors only a little. And the eigenvectors depend on the graph, not on cluster shape, so a well-connected curved cluster is as easy as a round one. In practice one uses the normalised Laplacian \(D^{-1/2}LD^{-1/2}\), whose eigenvectors are those of \(Lf=\lambda Df\) rescaled by \(D^{1/2}\), and normalises the rows of the eigenvector matrix before k-means (Ng, Jordan and Weiss, 2002).

idea — the slowest-varying patterns on a graph

\(f^\top Lf\) is small when connected nodes have similar values, so the eigenvectors run from the smoothest pattern on the graph to the roughest, like a drum's modes from the deepest note up. The smoothest is constant. The next, the Fiedler vector after Miroslav Fiedler, who studied it in 1973, changes sign where there are few edges to cross: at the best cut.

Bayes — k-means is a Gaussian mixture with the variance turned down

Suppose the data come from \(k\) Gaussians with means \(\mu_j\), equal weights and common covariance \(\sigma^2I\). The posterior probability that \(x_i\) came from component \(j\) is

$$r_{ij}=\frac{\exp(-\lVert x_i-\mu_j\rVert^2/2\sigma^2)}{\sum_l\exp(-\lVert x_i-\mu_l\rVert^2/2\sigma^2)}.$$

As \(\sigma\to0\) this tends to 1 for the nearest mean and 0 for the others: a hard assignment. The expectation–maximisation algorithm for the mixture (Lecture 17a) alternates computing \(r_{ij}\) with re-estimating the means as \(r\)-weighted averages, so in the limit it is Lloyd's algorithm.

Hence k-means' preference for round clusters of equal spread and size; the full mixture relaxes each assumption and gives soft memberships. The same limit of a Dirichlet-process mixture gives DP-means (Kulis and Jordan, 2012): k-means plus a penalty \(\lambda\) per cluster, so a point farther than \(\sqrt\lambda\) from every centre starts a new cluster.

6 — Is clustering well defined?

Our methods disagree, and so do our rules for \(k\). Is the fault in the methods or the question? Kleinberg (2002) wrote down three properties any reasonable method seems to need, and proved that no method has all three. A clustering function \(f\) takes a distance function \(d\) on a set of \(n\ge2\) points and returns a partition of the points.

definition 15.6 — Kleinberg's axioms

A clustering function \(f\) may satisfy:

  • Scale invariance: \(f(\alpha d)=f(d)\) for every \(\alpha>0\). Changing units (kilometres for miles) does not change the clusters.
  • Richness: every partition of the points is \(f(d)\) for some \(d\). Nothing, including the number of clusters, is fixed in advance.
  • Consistency: if \(d'\) is obtained from \(d\) by shrinking distances within the clusters of \(f(d)\) and stretching distances between them, then \(f(d')=f(d)\). Making clusters more clearly separated does not change them.
theorem 15.5 — Kleinberg's impossibility theorem

For \(n\ge2\) points, no clustering function satisfies scale invariance, richness and consistency.

Each pair of the three can be satisfied (by single linkage with a suitable stopping rule).

A partition \(\Gamma'\) refines \(\Gamma\) if every cluster of \(\Gamma'\) lies inside a cluster of \(\Gamma\). The key lemma: under scale invariance and consistency, the range of \(f\) is an antichain, that is, no partition in it is a proper refinement of another.

Suppose \(\Gamma=f(d)\) and \(\Gamma'=f(d')\), with \(\Gamma'\) a proper refinement of \(\Gamma\). Build a distance \(d''\) that is

  • very small within the clusters of \(\Gamma'\);
  • moderate between clusters of \(\Gamma'\) that lie in the same cluster of \(\Gamma\);
  • very large between clusters of \(\Gamma\).

From \(d'\): rescale it so that its within-cluster distances exceed those of \(d''\) (scale invariance). If the moderate distances are large enough, \(d''\) shrinks distances inside the clusters of \(\Gamma'\) and stretches those between them, so consistency gives \(f(d'')=\Gamma'\).

From \(d\), with a different rescaling: if the moderate distances are small enough, \(d''\) shrinks distances inside the clusters of \(\Gamma\) and stretches those between them, so \(f(d'')=\Gamma\).

Kleinberg shows that one choice of the moderate distances meets both requirements, giving \(\Gamma=\Gamma'\), a contradiction.

Richness requires every partition to be in the range, including the partition into singletons, which refines every other partition. With at least two points this contradicts the antichain property. For the full argument see Kleinberg (2002), Theorem 3.1. \(\square\)

Each of our methods gives up one axiom. k-means with fixed \(k\) fails richness; single linkage stopped at a fixed distance threshold fails scale invariance; stopped at a threshold relative to the largest distance, it fails consistency. So every method makes a choice (of scale, number of clusters, or what counts as "more separated") that the data cannot make for it, and disagreement is to be expected. Kleinberg himself considered weakening consistency to "refinement consistency"; later work studied quality measures for clusterings rather than clustering functions (Ackerman and Ben-David, 2008).

The practical lesson: a clustering answers a question you chose, not a fact found in the data; report how it moves when the choices move.

big picture — where clustering runs in 2026

Retailers and banks segment customers with k-means or Gaussian mixtures. In single-cell RNA sequencing (thousands of genes in each of millions of cells), new cell types are found by clustering a nearest-neighbour graph with community detection (Louvain, Leiden), close to spectral clustering, by default in Seurat and Scanpy. Pipelines remove near-duplicates by clustering embeddings. 7 — Summary, exercises, projects, reading

summary
  • k-means minimises the within-cluster sum of squares (Definition 15.1). Lloyd's algorithm alternates nearest-centre assignment and averaging, never raises the objective and terminates (Theorem 15.1), but finds only a local minimum of an NP-hard problem (Theorem 15.2); k-means++ seeding is within \(O(\log k)\) of optimal in expectation (Theorem 15.3).
  • On Citi Bike's January 2014 stations, k-means finds morning-peak, evening-peak and evening-and-weekend stations, geographically coherent although location was not used. On a photograph it is a colour quantiser: 16 colours give 27.6 dB.
  • The elbow, silhouette (Definition 15.2) and gap statistic disagree on the stations (no elbow, \(k=2\), \(k=7\)): \(k\) is a choice of resolution.
  • A dendrogram depends on the linkage (Definition 15.3): on 160 countries single linkage chains into one giant cluster; Ward's gives four interpretable groups.
  • DBSCAN (Definition 15.4) and HDBSCAN find clusters of any shape and label noise; DBSCAN recovers the two moons only in a narrow range of \(\varepsilon\), HDBSCAN without one.
  • Spectral clustering relaxes the normalised cut (Definition 15.5, Theorem 15.4). It separates the moons perfectly when the neighbour graph splits them, less well as it densifies, and recovers 33 of 34 karate-club factions.
  • No clustering function is scale invariant, rich and consistent (Theorem 15.5). Methods agree only moderately (ARI 0.42–0.67 among four balanced methods): report what is robust across methods.

  2. Show that Ward's merge cost \(\Delta(A,B)\) of Definition 15.3 equals the increase in the within-cluster sum of squares when \(A\) and \(B\) are merged.

    Let \(m\) be the merged mean:

    $$m=\frac{|A|\bar x_A+|B|\bar x_B}{|A|+|B|}.$$

    By the identity in the proof of Theorem 15.1,

    $$\sum_{i\in A}\lVert x_i-m\rVert^2=\sum_{i\in A}\lVert x_i-\bar x_A\rVert^2+|A|\lVert\bar x_A-m\rVert^2,$$

    and similarly for \(B\). The increase is therefore \(|A|\lVert\bar x_A-m\rVert^2+|B|\lVert\bar x_B-m\rVert^2\). Since

    $$\bar x_A-m=\frac{|B|}{|A|+|B|}(\bar x_A-\bar x_B),\qquad\bar x_B-m=-\frac{|A|}{|A|+|B|}(\bar x_A-\bar x_B),$$

    the increase is

    $$\frac{|A||B|^2+|B||A|^2}{(|A|+|B|)^2}\lVert\bar x_A-\bar x_B\rVert^2=\frac{|A||B|}{|A|+|B|}\lVert\bar x_A-\bar x_B\rVert^2.$$
  3. A graph consists of two triangles joined by a single edge (6 nodes, 7 unit-weight edges). Compute the normalised cut of the split into the two triangles, and of the split that cuts off one node of degree 2.

    The degrees are 2, 2, 3 in each triangle (the bridge raises one node to 3), so each triangle has volume 7 and the total is 14.

    Splitting the triangles cuts 1 edge: \(\operatorname{Ncut}=1\cdot(1/7+1/7)=0.286\).

    Cutting off a degree-2 node cuts 2 edges: \(\operatorname{Ncut}=2(1/2+1/12)=1.167\).

    The balanced split wins, although a plain minimum cut (1 edge against 2) would also choose it here; with a pendant node of degree 1, the plain cut would be tied between cutting it off and splitting the triangles (both cost 1 edge), while Ncut clearly prefers the balanced split.

  4. Show that if a graph has two connected components, the generalised eigenproblem \(Lf=\lambda Df\) has eigenvalue 0 with two independent eigenvectors, the indicator vectors of the components.

    For the indicator \(\mathbf 1_C\) of a component \(C\),

    $$\mathbf 1_C^\top L\mathbf 1_C=\tfrac12\sum_{ij}w_{ij}(\mathbf 1_C(i)-\mathbf 1_C(j))^2=0,$$

    because every edge has both ends in \(C\) or neither. Since \(L\) is positive semi-definite, a zero quadratic form means \(L\mathbf 1_C=0=0\cdot D\mathbf 1_C\). The two indicators have disjoint supports, so they are independent.

    (Conversely every \(f\) with \(f^\top Lf=0\) is constant on each component, so the multiplicity is exactly the number of components; this is what happened with the 5-nearest-neighbour graph of the moons.)

  5. Show that single linkage stopped when \(k\) clusters remain satisfies scale invariance and consistency but not richness, and that single linkage stopped at a fixed distance threshold \(r\) satisfies richness and consistency but not scale invariance.

    Fixed \(k\). The merges depend only on the order of the distances, which scaling does not change. Shrinking within-cluster and stretching between-cluster distances keeps every within-cluster merge before every between-cluster merge, so the same \(k\) clusters result. But only partitions into exactly \(k\) clusters are produced: richness fails.

    Threshold \(r\). The clusters are the connected components of the graph joining points at distance at most \(r\). Any partition arises by making distances within the intended clusters small and those between them larger than \(r\) (richness), and shrinking within / stretching between keeps the components (consistency). Scaling by a large \(\alpha\) pushes all distances above \(r\), giving singletons: scale invariance fails.

  6. (Code.) Recompute the station profiles using only weekday hours (24 numbers), cluster them with k-means for \(k=3\), and compare with the 48-number clustering using the adjusted Rand index. Which stations change cluster, and why?

    Replace Xc by the first 24 columns of cnt, divided by their row sums and multiplied by 24, and use adjusted_rand_score against lab. The morning-peak and evening-peak groups mostly survive, because they are defined by weekday timing. The stations that move are those whose distinguishing feature was weekend use: without the weekend half, the evening-and-weekend cluster loses its defining feature and splits between the other two, or reforms around a different feature such as late-evening weekday trips. The choice of features defines what "similar" means, before any clustering method is chosen.

student projects

Each project uses this lecture's methods and public data, and has a clear result.

1. Station personalities before and after the pandemic

question
Did the pandemic and the shift to hybrid work change the "personalities" of Citi Bike stations (or subway stations), and which neighbourhoods changed most?
data
Citi Bike monthly trip files (citibikenyc.com/system-data) for 2019 and 2024, restricted to stations present in both years; alternatively, MTA subway hourly ridership by station complex (data.ny.gov) for 2019-equivalent turnstile data and 2024.
method
Hourly weekday and weekend profiles as in Section 1; k-means, Ward and spectral clustering for each year; stability across methods and bootstrap resamples (adjusted Rand index); transition matrices of stations between clusters from 2019 to 2024, mapped.
outcome
A measured answer to "how much did the morning commute disappear, and where", with a stability analysis showing which changes are robust to the method.

2. Do 311 complaints cluster the way the city files them?

question
When 311 complaint descriptions are clustered by meaning, do the clusters match the city's official complaint types, and where do they disagree?
data
NYC 311 Service Requests (NYC Open Data), the free-text descriptor and resolution fields, with the official complaint type held out for evaluation.
method
Sentence embeddings from an open pre-trained encoder; k-means, HDBSCAN and agglomerative clustering; agreement with official types (adjusted Rand index, normalised mutual information); inspection of clusters that split or merge official types.
outcome
A map of where the city's taxonomy and the language of complaints diverge, with candidate new categories, of practical interest to the city's 311 service.

3. Neighbourhoods from data

question
If New York's census tracts are clustered by demographic and housing indicators alone, how closely do the clusters follow the official Neighborhood Tabulation Areas, and which "neighbourhoods" are really several?
data
American Community Survey 5-year estimates by census tract (US Census Bureau); NYC Neighborhood Tabulation Areas (NYC Department of City Planning).
method
Standardised indicators; Ward clustering with and without a spatial-contiguity constraint; spectral clustering on a tract adjacency graph weighted by indicator similarity; agreement with the official areas.
outcome
Data-driven neighbourhood boundaries, with an analysis of how much they depend on the method (Section 6), useful to urban planners and social scientists.
