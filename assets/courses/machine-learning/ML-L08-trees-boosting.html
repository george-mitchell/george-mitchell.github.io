This is the first of three lectures on graph-based methods. Here the graph is a tree: a rooted graph in which every internal node asks a yes-or-no question about one feature, every edge is an answer, and every leaf makes a prediction. Following a path from the root to a leaf applies a chain of rules, and the leaves partition feature space into boxes. A single tree is easy to read and easy to overfit. Two ideas turn trees into the most accurate methods we have for tabular data. Averaging many trees grown on resampled data (bagging, random forests) reduces variance. Adding trees one at a time, each correcting the errors of the sum so far (boosting, XGBoost), reduces bias. We will also see how to explain a boosted model's individual predictions with Shapley values from game theory, and how forests estimate who benefits from a treatment.

By the end of this lecture you should be able to grow a classification or regression tree by greedy impurity reduction and prune it; explain why trees are unstable and prove the variance formula behind random forests; derive AdaBoost's training-error bound and its link to exponential loss; describe gradient boosting as gradient descent in function space for any loss; derive XGBoost's leaf weights and split gain; compare tree ensembles with linear models and neural networks on tabular data; define the Shapley value and read a SHAP explanation, with its caveats; and estimate heterogeneous treatment effects with causal forests and meta-learners.

1 — Trees as graphs

definition 8.1 — decision tree

A (binary) decision tree is a rooted tree in which each internal node \(v\) carries a split "\(x_{j(v)}\le t_v\)?" on one feature, with one child for "yes" and one for "no", and each leaf \(\ell\) carries a prediction \(c_\ell\). The leaves define regions \(R_\ell\) of feature space (boxes, for numeric features), and the tree is the function

$$f(x)=\sum_{\ell}c_\ell\,\mathbb 1[x\in R_\ell].$$

For classification \(c_\ell\) is the majority class in the leaf (or the class proportions); for regression it is the mean response.

Here is a depth-3 tree for the Palmer penguins of Lectures 1 and 3, using their four body measurements.

2 — Growing a tree: impurity, greedy splits, pruning

Finding the best tree of a given size is NP-hard (Hyafil and Rivest, 1976), so every practical method grows the tree greedily: at each node, try every feature and every threshold between consecutive observed values, keep the split that most reduces impurity, and recurse. This is CART (Breiman, Friedman, Olshen and Stone, 1984).

definition 8.2 — impurity and impurity decrease

For a node with class proportions \(p_1,\dots,p_K\) the Gini impurity and entropy are

$$G(p)=1-\sum_kp_k^2=\sum_kp_k(1-p_k),\qquad H(p)=-\sum_kp_k\log_2p_k.$$

For regression the impurity is the variance of the responses in the node. A split sending a fraction \(w_L\) of the node's points left (proportions \(p^L\)) and \(w_R=1-w_L\) right (\(p^R\)) has impurity decrease

$$\Delta=I(p)-w_LI(p^L)-w_RI(p^R),\qquad p=w_Lp^L+w_Rp^R.$$

Gini impurity is the probability that two penguins drawn at random from the node belong to different species; entropy is the information (in bits) needed to name the species. Both are zero for a pure node and largest when all classes are equally common. Why not just count mistakes? Because the misclassification rate often does not change when a split makes both children purer, as at depth 3 in the widget, and a greedy search guided by it stalls. Gini and entropy are strictly concave, which rewards any split that separates the classes at all.

proposition 8.1 — impurity never increases

For any concave impurity \(I\) (Gini, entropy, or variance), every split has \(\Delta\ge0\). For strictly concave \(I\), \(\Delta=0\) only if the two children have the same class proportions.

The parent's proportions are the weighted average \(p=w_Lp^L+w_Rp^R\). Concavity says

$$I(w_Lp^L+w_Rp^R)\ge w_LI(p^L)+w_RI(p^R),$$

which is \(\Delta\ge0\), with equality for strictly concave \(I\) only when \(p^L=p^R\) (or one weight is 0).

For regression, the variance of the parent equals the weighted within-child variance plus the weighted between-child variance of the child means (the law of total variance), so \(\Delta\) is the between-child part, which is \(\ge0\). \(\square\)

spot check 8.1

A node contains 40 Adélie and 10 Chinstrap penguins. What is its Gini impurity?

\(1-p_1^2-p_2^2\) with \(p_1=0.8\), \(p_2=0.2\).
\(1-0.64-0.04=0.32\). Equivalently \(2\times0.8\times0.2\): the chance that two penguins drawn with replacement differ in species.

A tree grown until every leaf is pure has zero training error and high variance. The classical fix is to grow a large tree and then prune it back.

definition 8.3 — cost-complexity pruning

For a tree \(T\) with \(|T|\) leaves and training loss \(R(T)\), and a penalty \(\alpha\ge0\), the cost-complexity is

$$R_\alpha(T)=R(T)+\alpha|T|.$$

For each \(\alpha\), the subtree of the full tree minimising \(R_\alpha\) is unique and can be found by repeatedly collapsing the "weakest link", the internal node whose removal increases \(R\) least per leaf removed. As \(\alpha\) grows these subtrees are nested, and \(\alpha\) is chosen by cross-validation.

This is the lasso idea of Lecture 3 applied to tree size: a penalty per leaf, a path of solutions, and cross-validation to pick a point on the path. In practice, simpler limits (maximum depth, minimum leaf size) chosen by cross-validation work about as well, and ensembles make pruning largely unnecessary.

spot check 8.2

What is the largest number of leaves a binary tree of depth 5 can have?

Each level at most doubles the number of nodes.
\(2^5=32\). A depth-\(d\) tree can represent interactions among up to \(d\) features, a point that matters for boosting, where shallow trees are the norm.

3 — Why one tree is not enough

Trees have low bias: deep enough, they can approximate any function. Their weakness is variance. Because each split depends on the ones above it, a small change in the data can change the root split, and with it everything below. Let us resample the training penguins with replacement (the bootstrap) and grow a tree on each resample.

4 — Bagging and random forests

Averaging reduces variance. If we had many independent training sets we could grow a tree on each and average the predictions; the bootstrap fakes those training sets from the one we have. This is bagging, for bootstrap aggregating (Breiman, 1996): grow \(B\) deep trees on bootstrap resamples and average their predictions (or take a vote). But trees grown on resamples of the same data are not independent. How much does that matter?

proposition 8.2 — variance of an average of correlated predictors

Let \(T_1,\dots,T_B\) be identically distributed with variance \(\sigma^2\) and pairwise correlation \(\rho\ge0\). Then

$$\operatorname{Var}\Big(\frac1B\sum_{b=1}^BT_b\Big)=\rho\sigma^2+\frac{1-\rho}B\sigma^2.$$
$$\operatorname{Var}\big(\sum_bT_b\big)=\sum_b\operatorname{Var}T_b+\sum_{b\ne b'}\operatorname{Cov}(T_b,T_{b'})=B\sigma^2+B(B-1)\rho\sigma^2.$$

Divide by \(B^2\):

$$\frac{\sigma^2}B+\frac{B-1}B\rho\sigma^2=\rho\sigma^2+\frac{1-\rho}B\sigma^2.\qquad\square$$

As \(B\to\infty\) the second term vanishes but the first does not: correlation sets a floor \(\rho\sigma^2\) that more trees cannot break. Breiman's random forest (2001) lowers \(\rho\) directly: at each split, only a random subset of the features (often a third of them for regression, \(\sqrt p\) for classification) is eligible. A dominant feature can then not appear at the top of every tree, the trees become more different, and their average is more stable, even though each individual tree gets slightly worse. We can check the formula on the California housing data of Lecture 3 by growing forests on 30 independent training sets.

Forests also rank features. The default "impurity importance" adds up each feature's impurity decreases across all splits; "permutation importance" shuffles one feature in held-out data and measures how much worse the predictions get. They can disagree, and the first has a known bias (Strobl et al., 2007): features with many distinct values offer many candidate thresholds, and some split looks good by chance.

theorem 8.3 — random forests are consistent (informal)

Suppose

$$Y=\sum_jm_j(X_j)+\varepsilon$$

with continuous component functions and \(X\) uniform on \([0,1]^p\).

Breiman's random forest, with trees grown on subsamples of size \(a_n\) to \(t_n\) leaves, is consistent (its mean squared error tends to the noise level) provided \(a_n\to\infty\), \(t_n\to\infty\) and

$$t_n(\log a_n)^9/a_n\to0$$

(Scornet, Biau and Vert, 2015).

The conditions are technical and the additive model is restrictive, but the result was the first to prove consistency for the algorithm people actually use, with data-dependent splits. Proving things about greedy, data-dependent partitions is hard, which is why forest theory lagged forest practice by fifteen years.

spot check 8.4

Why can restricting each split to a random subset of features make a forest more accurate, even though each tree gets worse?

  • It lowers the correlation between trees, and the variance floor \(\rho\sigma^2\) of the average falls by more than the individual trees' loss of accuracy.
  • It reduces the variance of each individual tree.
  • It gives each tree more data.
  • It reduces the bias of each tree.
Which term of Proposition 8.2 survives as \(B\to\infty\)?
With many trees the forest's variance is about \(\rho\sigma^2\). In the code box \(\sigma^2\) rose from 0.52 to 0.67 but \(\rho\) fell from 0.11 to 0.044, so \(\rho\sigma^2\) fell from 0.058 to 0.030.

5 — Boosting I: AdaBoost

Forests average strong, deep, noisy trees. Boosting does the opposite: it combines many weak learners, each only slightly better than guessing, built in sequence so that each concentrates on the examples its predecessors got wrong. Kearns and Valiant asked in 1988 whether weak learnability implies strong learnability; Schapire (1990) proved it does, and Freund and Schapire's AdaBoost (1997) made it practical.

definition 8.4 — AdaBoost

With labels \(y_i\in\{\pm1\}\), start with weights \(w_i=1/n\). For \(t=1,\dots,T\):

  1. fit a weak classifier \(h_t\in\{\pm1\}\) to minimise the weighted error $$\varepsilon_t=\sum_iw_i\mathbb 1[h_t(x_i)\ne y_i];$$
  2. set $$\alpha_t=\frac12\log\frac{1-\varepsilon_t}{\varepsilon_t};$$
  3. update \(w_i\leftarrow w_ie^{-\alpha_ty_ih_t(x_i)}\) and renormalise, so mistakes gain weight and correct answers lose it.

The final classifier is \(\operatorname{sign}F_T(x)\) with

$$F_T=\sum_t\alpha_th_t.$$
theorem 8.4 — AdaBoost drives the training error down exponentially

The training error of AdaBoost after \(T\) rounds satisfies

$$\frac1n\sum_i\mathbb 1[\operatorname{sign}F_T(x_i)\ne y_i]\ \le\ \prod_{t=1}^T2\sqrt{\varepsilon_t(1-\varepsilon_t)}\ \le\ \exp\Big(-2\sum_{t=1}^T\gamma_t^2\Big),\qquad\gamma_t=\tfrac12-\varepsilon_t.$$

So if every weak learner beats guessing by a margin \(\gamma_t\ge\gamma>0\), the training error falls below \(e^{-2\gamma^2T}\).

Let

$$Z_t=\sum_iw_ie^{-\alpha_ty_ih_t(x_i)}$$

be the normaliser in round \(t\). Unrolling the updates, the final weights are

$$w_i^{(T+1)}=\frac1n e^{-y_iF_T(x_i)}/\prod_tZ_t,$$

and they sum to 1, so

$$\frac1n\sum_ie^{-y_iF_T(x_i)}=\prod_tZ_t.$$

A mistake has \(y_iF_T(x_i)\le0\), so \(\mathbb 1[\text{mistake}]\le e^{-y_iF_T(x_i)}\), and the training error is at most \(\prod_tZ_t\).

Now

$$Z_t=(1-\varepsilon_t)e^{-\alpha_t}+\varepsilon_te^{\alpha_t},$$

which \(\alpha_t=\frac12\log\frac{1-\varepsilon_t}{\varepsilon_t}\) minimises, giving

$$Z_t=2\sqrt{\varepsilon_t(1-\varepsilon_t)}=\sqrt{1-4\gamma_t^2}\le e^{-2\gamma_t^2},$$

using \(1-u\le e^{-u}\). \(\square\)

The proof contains a second result. AdaBoost is minimising \(\frac1n\sum_ie^{-y_iF(x_i)}\), the exponential loss of Lecture 5, one weak learner at a time.

theorem 8.5 — AdaBoost is forward stagewise minimisation of exponential loss

Given \(F_{t-1}\), the pair \((\alpha,h)\) minimising

$$\sum_ie^{-y_i(F_{t-1}(x_i)+\alpha h(x_i))}$$

is AdaBoost's \(h_t\) (minimising weighted error with weights \(\propto e^{-y_iF_{t-1}(x_i)}\)) and \(\alpha_t\) (Friedman, Hastie and Tibshirani, 2000).

Write \(w_i\propto e^{-y_iF_{t-1}(x_i)}\), exactly AdaBoost's weights. The objective is proportional to

$$\sum_iw_ie^{-\alpha y_ih(x_i)}=e^{-\alpha}\sum_{\text{correct}}w_i+e^{\alpha}\sum_{\text{wrong}}w_i=e^{-\alpha}+(e^\alpha-e^{-\alpha})\varepsilon(h).$$

For any \(\alpha>0\) this is increasing in the weighted error \(\varepsilon(h)\), so the best \(h\) minimises weighted error.

Then minimising \((1-\varepsilon)e^{-\alpha}+\varepsilon e^\alpha\) over \(\alpha\) gives

$$\alpha=\frac12\log\frac{1-\varepsilon}\varepsilon.\qquad\square$$

Since the population minimiser of exponential loss is \(F^*(x)=\frac12\log\frac{P(Y=1\mid x)}{P(Y=-1\mid x)}\) (Lecture 5), AdaBoost's score estimates half the log-odds. Here it is from scratch, with decision stumps (depth-1 trees) on the breast-cancer data.

code — AdaBoost with stumps, and its bound

def best_stump(X, t, w):
    """Weighted-error-minimising stump: predict s if x_f > thr, else -s."""
    best = (np.inf, None)
    for f in range(X.shape[1]):
        order = np.argsort(X[:, f]); xs, ts, ws = X[order, f], t[order], w[order]
        # error of predicting +1 above the cut = weight of -1 above + weight of +1 at or below
        above_neg = np.cumsum((ws * (ts < 0))[::-1])[::-1]
        below_pos = np.cumsum(ws * (ts > 0))
        err = np.concatenate([[above_neg[0]], below_pos + np.append(above_neg[1:], 0)])
        for s, e in [(1, err), (-1, 1 - err)]:
            k = e.argmin()
            if e[k] < best[0]:
                thr = -np.inf if k == 0 else (xs[k - 1] + xs[k]) / 2 if k < len(xs) else np.inf
                best = (e[k], (f, thr, s))
    return best

history — from survey research to Kaggle

The first regression tree program, AID ("automatic interaction detection"), was written by James Morgan and John Sonquist at the University of Michigan's Survey Research Center in 1963 to find interactions in survey data. Leo Breiman, Jerome Friedman, Richard Olshen and Charles Stone's book Classification and Regression Trees (1984) gave the method its statistical foundations, while Ross Quinlan developed ID3 (1986) and C4.5 (1993) in machine learning. Robert Schapire's 1990 paper "The strength of weak learnability" answered a theoretical question of Kearns and Valiant and led to AdaBoost; Yoav Freund and Schapire won the 2003 Gödel Prize for it. Breiman introduced bagging in 1996 and random forests in 2001, building on Tin Kam Ho's random decision forests (1995). Friedman's gradient boosting machine (2001) recast boosting as optimisation. Tianqi Chen's XGBoost, released in 2014 and published with Carlos Guestrin in 2016, won so many data-science competitions that 17 of the 29 winning Kaggle solutions in 2015 used it.

6 — Boosting II: gradient boosting

Theorem 8.5 suggests a generalisation. AdaBoost adds the learner that most reduces exponential loss; why not any loss? Friedman (2001) saw that adding a small function \(h\) to the current model \(F\) is a step in function space, and the steepest step is in the direction of the negative gradient of the loss with respect to the predictions. We cannot add an arbitrary vector of values at the training points (that would not generalise), but we can fit a small tree to that vector and add the tree.

definition 8.5 — gradient boosting

Given a differentiable loss \(\ell(y,F)\), a learning rate \(\nu\in(0,1]\) and a tree size:

  1. start with the best constant, $$F_0=\arg\min_c\sum_i\ell(y_i,c);$$
  2. for \(m=1,\dots,M\): compute the pseudo-residuals $$r_i=-\partial\ell(y_i,F)/\partial F\big|_{F=F_{m-1}(x_i)};$$ fit a regression tree to \((x_i,r_i)\); in each leaf \(R_j\) choose the value $$\gamma_j=\arg\min_\gamma\sum_{x_i\in R_j}\ell\big(y_i,F_{m-1}(x_i)+\gamma\big);$$ set $$F_m=F_{m-1}+\nu\sum_j\gamma_j\mathbb 1[x\in R_j].$$

For squared loss \(\frac12(y-F)^2\), the pseudo-residuals are the ordinary residuals \(y-F\): each tree fits what the model so far gets wrong. For absolute loss they are the signs of the residuals and the leaf values are medians; for log loss with \(p=\sigma(F)\) they are \(y-p\), label minus prediction, the negative of the gradient of Lecture 4. Every loss of Lecture 5 now has a boosting algorithm, including quantile and Poisson losses. The learning rate \(\nu\) (shrinkage) takes only part of each step; smaller steps need more trees but generalise better, the same trade-off as gradient descent with a small step and early stopping (Lecture 2).

7 — XGBoost and its relatives

Gradient boosting fits each tree to first derivatives and then fixes the leaf values with a separate line search. XGBoost (Chen and Guestrin, 2016) uses second derivatives as well, as Newton's method does (Lecture 2), and adds a penalty on the tree itself. This gives closed-form leaf values and a closed-form score for every candidate split.

At round \(t\) we add a tree \(f\) to the current predictions \(\hat y_i\). The tree is a set of leaves with values \(w_1,\dots,w_T\), and \(q(x)\) is the leaf that \(x\) falls in, so \(f(x)=w_{q(x)}\). The objective is the training loss plus a complexity penalty

$$\sum_i\ell\big(y_i,\hat y_i+f(x_i)\big)+\gamma T+\tfrac12\lambda\sum_{j=1}^Tw_j^2,$$

a cost \(\gamma\) per leaf and a ridge penalty \(\lambda\) on the leaf values. Expand the loss to second order around \(\hat y_i\), with \(g_i=\partial\ell/\partial\hat y_i\) and \(h_i=\partial^2\ell/\partial\hat y_i^2\).

theorem 8.6 — XGBoost's leaf weights and split gain

Let \(G_j=\sum_{i:q(x_i)=j}g_i\) and \(H_j=\sum_{i:q(x_i)=j}h_i\).

For a fixed tree structure, the second-order objective is minimised by the leaf values

$$w_j^*=-\frac{G_j}{H_j+\lambda},\qquad\text{giving the objective}\qquad-\frac12\sum_{j=1}^T\frac{G_j^2}{H_j+\lambda}+\gamma T.$$

Splitting a leaf into left and right children therefore changes the objective by

$$\text{gain}=\frac12\Big[\frac{G_L^2}{H_L+\lambda}+\frac{G_R^2}{H_R+\lambda}-\frac{(G_L+G_R)^2}{H_L+H_R+\lambda}\Big]-\gamma,$$

and the split is worth making only if the gain is positive.

To second order,

$$\ell(y_i,\hat y_i+f(x_i))\approx\ell(y_i,\hat y_i)+g_if(x_i)+\frac12h_if(x_i)^2.$$

Dropping the constant and grouping the points by leaf (all points in leaf \(j\) get \(f=w_j\)), the objective is

$$\sum_{j=1}^T\Big[G_jw_j+\tfrac12(H_j+\lambda)w_j^2\Big]+\gamma T,$$

a sum of independent one-variable quadratics. Each is minimised at

$$w_j=-G_j/(H_j+\lambda),$$

with value

$$-\frac12G_j^2/(H_j+\lambda).$$

The gain compares the objective with one leaf (\(G=G_L+G_R\), \(H=H_L+H_R\)) against two leaves, minus the extra leaf's cost \(\gamma\). \(\square\)

Several things are visible in these formulas. For squared loss, \(g_i=\hat y_i-y_i\) and \(h_i=1\), so \(w_j^*\) is the mean residual in the leaf, shrunk toward zero by \(\lambda\): ridge regression of Lecture 3, one leaf at a time. For log loss, \(g_i=p_i-y_i\) and \(h_i=p_i(1-p_i)\), so a leaf full of confident predictions (small \(h_i\)) gets a larger step, exactly as Newton's method takes larger steps where the curvature is small. And \(H_j\) acts as an effective sample size: XGBoost's min_child_weight is a lower bound on it. Let us check the formulas against XGBoost itself.

A leaf has gradient sum \(G=-4\) and Hessian sum \(H=3\), with \(\lambda=1\). What value does XGBoost give the leaf?

The rest of XGBoost's design is engineering that made the method fast enough to dominate: approximate split finding on quantile sketches of each feature, so only a few hundred candidate thresholds are tried; sparsity-aware splits that learn a default direction for missing values; column subsampling borrowed from random forests; and cache-aware, parallel histogram construction. Two successors are worth knowing. LightGBM (Ke et al., 2017) bins features into histograms, grows trees leaf-wise (always splitting the leaf with the largest gain, rather than level by level), and samples data by gradient size. CatBoost (Prokhorenkova et al., 2018) targets categorical features: it encodes a category by the average target of earlier rows only, in a random order, and boosts with the same "ordered" trick, which prevents the target leakage that naive target encoding causes.

8 — Why trees still win on tables

Deep learning transformed images, sound and text, but on tabular data (rows of mixed numeric and categorical features, as in most of science, medicine and business) gradient-boosted trees remain hard to beat. Grinsztajn, Oyallon and Varoquaux (2022) compared them carefully with neural networks on 45 medium-sized datasets with equal tuning budgets, and found tree ensembles ahead on most. They traced the gap to three properties of tabular data:

  • Irregular targets. Tabular targets often change abruptly as a feature crosses a threshold. Neural networks are biased toward smooth functions; trees produce step functions naturally.
  • Uninformative features. Tables carry many weak or useless columns. Trees ignore a feature they never split on; networks must learn to ignore it, and degrade as useless columns are added.
  • Meaningful axes. A column means something (age, income) and rotating the feature space mixes meanings. Trees split along the axes; a fully connected network is rotation-invariant, which is the wrong symmetry here.

The challenger is a different kind of model. TabPFN (Hollmann et al., 2023; 2025) is a transformer trained once, on millions of synthetic datasets drawn from a prior over causal models. Given a new dataset's training rows and a test row as a single input, it outputs a predictive distribution in one forward pass, with no gradient steps on the new data. It is learning to do Bayesian inference: the network approximates the posterior predictive distribution under its synthetic prior. On datasets of up to about 10,000 rows it matches or beats tuned boosted trees in seconds (Hollmann et al., 2025). Whether such "tabular foundation models" will displace trees on larger data is an open question.

We can test the claim on the housing data of Section 7, giving each method only a small random sample of the training districts and scoring all of them on the same 2,000 test districts.

big picture — trees in industry

Gradient-boosted trees are the workhorse of tabular machine learning in industry: credit scoring, fraud detection, insurance pricing, demand forecasting and click prediction all run on XGBoost, LightGBM or CatBoost. Search engines rank results with LambdaMART, gradient-boosted trees trained on a pairwise ranking loss. Random forests remain standard in genomics and ecology, where species-distribution models predict where a species can live from climate layers. Regulated industries pair boosted models with SHAP explanations (Section 9) to meet requirements that individual decisions, such as a declined loan, be explainable. And one tree is still the method of choice when a human must apply the rule by hand: clinical decision rules and triage protocols are short decision trees.

9 — Opening the black box: Shapley values and SHAP

A boosted model with hundreds of trees is accurate and unreadable. For one prediction (this applicant, this patient), how much did each feature push the prediction up or down? Lloyd Shapley answered the corresponding question in cooperative game theory in 1953: how to divide a coalition's payoff fairly among its players. Here the "players" are features and the "payoff" is the prediction.

definition 8.6 — Shapley value

Let \(N=\{1,\dots,p\}\) and \(v\) assign a value \(v(S)\) to every coalition \(S\subseteq N\). The Shapley value of player \(j\) is its marginal contribution averaged over all orders in which players could join:

$$\phi_j=\sum_{S\subseteq N\setminus\{j\}}\frac{|S|!\,(p-|S|-1)!}{p!}\Big[v(S\cup\{j\})-v(S)\Big].$$

In SHAP (Lundberg and Lee, 2017), \(v(S)\) is the model's expected prediction when only the features in \(S\) are known:

$$v(S)=\mathbb E[f(X)\mid X_S=x_S],$$

or the "interventional" version that averages \(f(x_S,X_{\bar S})\) over the data distribution of the other features.

theorem 8.7 — Shapley's uniqueness theorem

The Shapley value is the only way to divide \(v(N)-v(\emptyset)\) among the players that satisfies:

  • efficiency, \(\sum_j\phi_j=v(N)-v(\emptyset)\);
  • symmetry, players who contribute equally to every coalition get equal shares;
  • dummy, a player who never changes \(v\) gets 0;
  • and additivity, the shares for the game \(v+w\) are the sums of the shares for \(v\) and \(w\)

(Shapley, 1953).

The Shapley formula satisfies all four (efficiency because, in each ordering, the marginal contributions telescope to \(v(N)-v(\emptyset)\)).

For uniqueness, every game is a linear combination of "unanimity games"

$$u_T(S)=\mathbb 1[T\subseteq S],$$

one for each non-empty \(T\). In \(u_T\), players outside \(T\) are dummies (share 0) and players in \(T\) are symmetric, so efficiency forces each to get \(1/|T|\). Additivity (and the scaling it implies) then fixes the shares for every game. \(\square\)

Efficiency is what makes a SHAP explanation readable: the contributions add up exactly to the difference between this prediction and the average prediction. Computing the Shapley value naively needs all \(2^p\) coalitions, but for tree ensembles Lundberg et al. (2020) found an exact algorithm, TreeSHAP, polynomial in the number of trees, leaves and depth, by pushing all coalitions through each tree at once. XGBoost has it built in.

10 — Bayesian trees and causal forests

bayes — BART and TabPFN

Bayesian additive regression trees (Chipman, George and McCulloch, 2010) write \(f\) as a sum of \(m\) small trees (typically 200), put a prior on each tree that strongly prefers shallow trees and small leaf values, and sample from the posterior by MCMC, updating one tree at a time against the residuals of the others ("Bayesian backfitting"). The result is a boosted-tree ensemble with posterior uncertainty for every prediction and little tuning. BART has become a standard tool for estimating treatment effects from observational data (Hill, 2011), and BART-based methods have done well in causal-inference competitions. TabPFN (Section 8) is the opposite trade: instead of sampling a posterior for each new dataset, it amortises Bayesian inference, paying once to train a network that maps datasets to posterior predictive distributions.

Lecture 3's and Lecture 4's causal estimates were averages: the average effect of a training programme. Often the question is who benefits. The conditional average treatment effect is

$$\tau(x)=\mathbb E\big[Y(1)-Y(0)\mid X=x\big],$$

the average effect among units with features \(x\). Two families of tree methods estimate it. Meta-learners (Künzel et al., 2019) reuse any regression method: the T-learner fits one model to the treated and one to the controls and subtracts their predictions; the X-learner refines this by regressing imputed individual effects. Causal forests (Wager and Athey, 2018; Athey, Tibshirani and Wager, 2019) change the trees themselves. Each split is chosen to make the treatment effect differ between the two children, rather than the outcome; and each tree is honest: it uses one half of its subsample to choose the splits and the other half to estimate the effect in each leaf, so the same data never both find a pattern and confirm it.

theorem 8.8 — causal forests are asymptotically normal (informal)

Under unconfoundedness, overlap, Lipschitz continuity of the outcome functions and suitable conditions on the subsample size and leaf sizes, the honest causal forest estimate \(\hat\tau(x)\) satisfies

$$(\hat\tau(x)-\tau(x))/\sqrt{\operatorname{Var}\hat\tau(x)}\to\mathcal N(0,1),$$

and the variance can be estimated consistently by the infinitesimal jackknife (Wager and Athey, 2018).

That gives confidence intervals for individual-level effects, which no off-the-shelf boosted model provides. Here are both approaches on a real randomised experiment.

spot check 8.9

Why is it legitimate to check predicted treatment effects by comparing treated and control visit rates within quintiles of predicted effect?

  • Treatment was randomised and the predictions come from the other half of the data, so within each quintile the treated and control customers are comparable and the difference in rates estimates that group's average effect without bias.
  • Because the individual treatment effects are observed in this experiment.
  • Because the models were fitted on the same customers, so they know their effects.
  • Because quintiles always contain equal numbers of treated and control customers.
What would go wrong in observational data, or if the groups were formed on the evaluation data?
Randomisation makes treatment independent of everything else, including a prediction computed from covariates on separate data, so the within-group difference in means is unbiased for the group's average effect (Exercise 10).
causal — uplift modelling in practice

Marketing calls this uplift modelling: send the offer to the customers it changes, not to those who would buy anyway or never will. The same tools serve personalised medicine (which patients benefit from a treatment with side effects?) and policy (which job-seekers gain from a training programme?). Two cautions apply everywhere. Heterogeneity estimates are noisy, so a targeting rule should be evaluated on held-out randomised data, as above, before it is deployed. And with observational data the trees only estimate causal effects under unconfoundedness (Lecture 4); causal forests make the estimation flexible, not the assumptions weaker.

quantum — decision trees as a model of computation

In complexity theory, a decision tree computing a Boolean function \(f:\{0,1\}^n\to\{0,1\}\) queries one input bit per node; its depth is the number of queries. The deterministic query complexity \(D(f)\) is the smallest depth of a tree computing \(f\). A quantum algorithm can query bits in superposition, and its query complexity \(Q(f)\) can be much smaller: Grover search computes OR of \(n\) bits with \(Q=O(\sqrt n)\) queries, against \(D=n\). How much smaller can it be? For total functions (defined on every input), Beals et al. (2001) proved \(D(f)=O(Q(f)^6)\), and after Huang's 2019 proof of the sensitivity conjecture, Aaronson et al. (2021) improved this to \(D(f)=O(Q(f)^4)\), which is tight up to logarithmic factors (Ambainis et al., 2017). So for total Boolean functions quantum computers give at most a polynomial speed-up in queries; exponential speed-ups, as in Shor's algorithm, need problems with structure, where the input is promised to have a special form.

11 — Summary, exercises, projects, reading

summary
  • A decision tree is a rooted graph of yes/no questions whose leaves partition feature space into boxes (Definition 8.1). CART grows it greedily by impurity decrease, which is never negative for concave impurities (Proposition 8.1), and prunes it with a per-leaf penalty (Definition 8.3).
  • Trees have low bias and high variance: bootstrap resamples change the root split. Averaging \(B\) trees with correlation \(\rho\) leaves variance \(\rho\sigma^2+(1-\rho)\sigma^2/B\) (Proposition 8.2); random forests lower \(\rho\) by subsampling features. Out-of-bag error is a free test estimate; prefer permutation importance to impurity importance.
  • AdaBoost reweights mistakes; its training error is at most \(\prod_t2\sqrt{\varepsilon_t(1-\varepsilon_t)}\) (Theorem 8.4), and it is stagewise minimisation of exponential loss (Theorem 8.5). Test error can keep falling after training error reaches zero, as margins grow.
  • Gradient boosting fits each tree to the negative gradient of any differentiable loss, with shrinkage and early stopping (Definition 8.5). XGBoost adds second derivatives and a penalty, giving \(w^*=-G/(H+\lambda)\) and a closed-form split gain (Theorem 8.6).
  • On tabular data, boosted trees usually beat neural networks; TabPFN, a transformer doing amortised Bayesian inference, is the challenger on small data: on 100 to 2,000 housing districts it beats a random forest and XGBoost.
  • Shapley values are the unique fair attribution (Theorem 8.7); TreeSHAP computes them exactly for tree ensembles. They explain the model, not the world.
  • BART puts a prior on sums of trees. Causal forests and meta-learners estimate heterogeneous treatment effects \(\tau(x)\); honest causal forests give confidence intervals (Theorem 8.8).

The new weighting makes \(h_t\) useless, so the next learner must capture something else.

  6. For log loss with \(y\in\{0,1\}\), $$\ell(y,F)=-y\log\sigma(F)-(1-y)\log\big(1-\sigma(F)\big),$$ show that \(\partial\ell/\partial F=\sigma(F)-y\) and \(\partial^2\ell/\partial F^2=\sigma(F)(1-\sigma(F))\).

    Using \(\sigma'=\sigma(1-\sigma)\): \(\frac{\partial}{\partial F}\big[-y\log\sigma\big]=-y(1-\sigma)\) and \(\frac{\partial}{\partial F}\big[-(1-y)\log(1-\sigma)\big]=(1-y)\sigma\). Adding gives \(\sigma-y\), and differentiating again gives \(\sigma(1-\sigma)\). These are XGBoost's \(g_i\) and \(h_i\) for classification.

  7. With \(\lambda=1\) and \(\gamma=1\), a leaf would split into children with \(G_L=-6\), \(H_L=4\) and \(G_R=2\), \(H_R=2\). Compute the gain. Is the split made?

    \(\tfrac12\big[36/5+4/3-16/7\big]-1=\tfrac12(7.2+1.3333-2.2857)-1=3.1238-1=2.1238>0\): yes. The leaf values would be \(6/5=1.2\) and \(-2/3\).

  8. Compute the Shapley values of the two features for \(f(x)=x_1+2x_2\) and for \(f(x)=x_1x_2\), at \(x=(1,1)\), using the "baseline" value function \(v(S)=f(x_S,0_{\bar S})\).

    For \(x_1+2x_2\): \(v(\emptyset)=0\), \(v(\{1\})=1\), \(v(\{2\})=2\), \(v(N)=3\); \(\phi_1=\tfrac12[(1-0)+(3-2)]=1\), \(\phi_2=2\). For an additive model the Shapley values are the terms. For \(x_1x_2\): \(v(\{1\})=v(\{2\})=0\), \(v(N)=1\), so \(\phi_1=\phi_2=\tfrac12\): an interaction is split equally between the features involved.

  9. Show that gradient boosting with stumps (depth-1 trees) always produces an additive model \(F(x)=\sum_jg_j(x_j)\), and that depth-\(d\) trees can produce interactions among at most \(d\) features.

    A stump depends on one feature, so a sum of stumps is a sum of one-feature functions; group the stumps by feature. A depth-\(d\) tree's leaf is reached by at most \(d\) tests, so each leaf's indicator depends on at most \(d\) features, and the tree is a sum of such indicators. Friedman recommends trees with 4 to 8 leaves in practice, allowing moderate interactions.

  10. In a randomised experiment, let \(G\) be a group of units defined by a function of the covariates computed from independent data. Show that the difference between the mean outcomes of treated and control units in \(G\) is unbiased for \(\mathbb E[Y(1)-Y(0)\mid X\in G]\).

    Randomisation makes \(T\) independent of \((Y(0),Y(1),X)\), and membership in \(G\) is a function of \(X\) (and of independent data), so \(T\) is independent of \((Y(0),Y(1))\) given \(X\in G\). Then \(\mathbb E[Y\mid T=1,X\in G]=\mathbb E[Y(1)\mid X\in G]\) and similarly for control; conditionally on the group sizes, the sample means are unbiased for these.

1. Who benefits? Heterogeneous effects in public experiments

question
Do causal forests, X-learners and BART agree about who benefits from an intervention, and do their targeting rules hold up on held-out randomised data?
data
The Criteo uplift dataset (this lecture uses 10% of its 14 million users), the Hillstrom e-mail experiment (64,000 customers), or an education randomised trial from openICPSR.
method
Fit each method on half the data; compare Qini and uplift curves, calibration by quintile and the stability of the top-ranked subgroups across random splits; report the gain from targeting against treating everyone.
outcome
A practical comparison of heterogeneous-effect methods on real experiments, with honest uncertainty, of interest to applied economists and marketers alike.

2. How stable are SHAP explanations?

question
If XGBoost is retrained with a different seed or on a bootstrap resample, how often do a person's top three SHAP features change?
data
Folktables ACS income for New York (this lecture uses California), or the 1994 Adult data it replaced.
method
Retrain 50 models across seeds and resamples with equal test accuracy; compute TreeSHAP (path-dependent and interventional) for fixed test people; measure rank agreement of attributions, overall and for people near the decision boundary.
outcome
A measurement of explanation instability, which matters wherever explanations are given to people about decisions affecting them; there is a small literature to build on and much left to measure.

3. Trees, networks and TabPFN on city data

question
On small tabular prediction problems from public-sector data, do boosted trees, tuned MLPs or a tabular foundation model predict best, and how does the answer change with sample size?
data
Several NYC Open Data tables (for example restaurant inspection grades, 311 response times, building energy benchmarking), subsampled to 500–10,000 rows.
method
Equal tuning budgets, repeated train/test splits, learning curves in the number of rows, and the Grinsztajn et al. (2022) diagnostics (adding uninformative features, rotating the features).
outcome
Evidence on whether the trees-versus-networks picture holds on civic data, and practical guidance for analysts in city agencies.
