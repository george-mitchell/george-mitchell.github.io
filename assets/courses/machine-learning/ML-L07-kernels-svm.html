Lecture 6 ended with a margin bound: a linear classifier generalises well when it separates the data with a wide margin relative to the size of its weights, whatever the dimension. The support vector machine turns that into an algorithm: find the separating line with the widest margin. Solving that problem through its dual reveals two surprises. The answer depends only on a few training points, the support vectors. And the data enter only through inner products \(x_i^\top x_j\), so we can replace them by a kernel \(k(x_i,x_j)\), an inner product in a much richer feature space that we never compute directly. That one substitution turns linear methods into flexible nonlinear ones, and leads to kernel ridge regression and to Gaussian processes, the Bayesian version of the same idea.

By the end of this lecture you should be able to set up the hard- and soft-margin SVM, derive its dual with Lagrange multipliers, and read off the support vectors from the KKT conditions; relate the soft-margin SVM to hinge loss with a ridge penalty; explain the kernel trick and check whether a function is a valid kernel; state Mercer's theorem and the representer theorem and prove the latter; fit kernel ridge regression and Gaussian process regression, and explain why they give the same predictions; choose kernel hyperparameters by cross-validation or marginal likelihood; and say what random features, the neural tangent kernel and quantum kernels have to do with all this.

1 — The widest street

Take two classes that a line can separate. Infinitely many lines do it. The perceptron of Lecture 4 stops at the first one it finds; logistic regression keeps pushing until the probabilities saturate. The SVM picks the line that stays as far as possible from both classes, the middle of the widest street that fits between them. Points near the boundary are the hard cases, and a boundary that leaves them room is less likely to be wrong on nearby new points; Lecture 6's margin bound makes that precise.

proposition 7.1 — distance to a hyperplane

The distance from a point \(x\) to the hyperplane \(\{z:w^\top z+b=0\}\) is

$$|w^\top x+b|/\|w\|.$$

The closest point of the hyperplane to \(x\) is \(x-tw\) for the \(t\) with \(w^\top(x-tw)+b=0\), that is

$$t=(w^\top x+b)/\|w\|^2.$$

The distance is

$$\|tw\|=|w^\top x+b|/\|w\|.\ \square$$

With labels \(y_i\in\{-1,+1\}\), the hyperplane classifies \(x_i\) correctly with room to spare when \(y_i(w^\top x_i+b)>0\), and its distance from the boundary is \(y_i(w^\top x_i+b)/\|w\|\). Rescaling \(w\) and \(b\) together does not change the hyperplane, so we may fix the scale by requiring the closest points to satisfy \(y_i(w^\top x_i+b)=1\). Then the margin on each side is \(1/\|w\|\), the street has width \(2/\|w\|\), and maximising the width means minimising \(\|w\|\).

definition 7.1 — the hard-margin SVM

Given linearly separable data \((x_i,y_i)\), \(y_i\in\{\pm1\}\), the hard-margin support vector machine solves

$$\min_{w,b}\ \tfrac12\|w\|^2\quad\text{subject to}\quad y_i(w^\top x_i+b)\ge1,\quad i=1,\dots,n.$$

The lines \(w^\top x+b=\pm1\) are the edges of the street, and the classifier is \(\operatorname{sign}(w^\top x+b)\).

2 — Constrained optimisation: Lagrange multipliers, duality and KKT

The SVM is a minimisation with inequality constraints. Gradient descent alone does not handle constraints, so we need the standard toolkit, which is worth learning once because it appears all over machine learning papers.

Start with the picture. Minimise a convex function \(f\) over a region cut out by constraints \(g_i(x)\le0\). If the minimum is inside the region, \(\nabla f=0\) there. If it is on the boundary, the downhill direction \(-\nabla f\) must point out of the region (otherwise we could keep going), and that means \(-\nabla f\) is a nonnegative combination of the outward normals \(\nabla g_i\) of the constraints that are active, those holding with equality. Think of a ball rolling downhill until it rests against walls: the walls push back with forces \(\mu_i\ge0\), and only the walls it touches can push.

theorem 7.2 — Karush–Kuhn–Tucker conditions

Consider \(\min_xf(x)\) subject to \(g_i(x)\le0\), \(i=1,\dots,m\), with \(f\) and the \(g_i\) convex and differentiable, and suppose some \(x\) satisfies every constraint strictly (Slater's condition).

Then \(x^*\) is optimal if and only if there are multipliers \(\mu_1,\dots,\mu_m\) with

$$\nabla f(x^*)+\sum_i\mu_i\nabla g_i(x^*)=0,\qquad g_i(x^*)\le0,\qquad \mu_i\ge0,\qquad \mu_ig_i(x^*)=0.$$

These are stationarity, primal feasibility, dual feasibility and complementary slackness: a multiplier can be nonzero only if its constraint is active.

Suppose the conditions hold and \(x\) is any feasible point. The Lagrangian

$$\mathcal L(x,\mu)=f(x)+\sum_i\mu_ig_i(x)$$

is convex in \(x\) (a sum of convex functions with nonnegative weights), and stationarity says \(\nabla_x\mathcal L(x^*,\mu)=0\), so \(x^*\) minimises \(\mathcal L(\cdot,\mu)\). Then

$$f(x)\ \ge\ f(x)+\sum_i\mu_ig_i(x)=\mathcal L(x,\mu)\ \ge\ \mathcal L(x^*,\mu)=f(x^*)+\sum_i\mu_ig_i(x^*)=f(x^*),$$

using \(\mu_i\ge0\) and \(g_i(x)\le0\) in the first step and complementary slackness in the last.

Necessity, which needs Slater's condition, is the harder direction; see Boyd and Vandenberghe (2004), Section 5.5. \(\square\)

The multipliers also define a second optimisation problem. The dual function \(q(\mu)=\min_x\mathcal L(x,\mu)\) is a lower bound on the optimum for every \(\mu\ge0\), and the dual problem is to find the best lower bound.

proposition 7.3 — weak and strong duality

For every \(\mu\ge0\), \(q(\mu)\le f(x)\) for every feasible \(x\) (weak duality), so

$$\max_{\mu\ge0}q(\mu)\le\min_{x\text{ feasible}}f(x).$$

Under the conditions of Theorem 7.2 the two are equal (strong duality), and the KKT multipliers solve the dual problem.

For feasible \(x\) and \(\mu\ge0\):

$$q(\mu)\le\mathcal L(x,\mu)=f(x)+\sum\mu_ig_i(x)\le f(x).$$

For strong duality, the chain in the proof of Theorem 7.2 with \(x=x^*\) shows \(q(\mu)=\mathcal L(x^*,\mu)=f(x^*)\) for the KKT multipliers. \(\square\)

3 — The dual SVM, support vectors and soft margins

theorem 7.4 — the dual of the hard-margin SVM

The hard-margin SVM (Definition 7.1) has the dual problem

$$\max_{\alpha\ge0}\ \sum_{i=1}^n\alpha_i-\frac12\sum_{i,j}\alpha_i\alpha_jy_iy_j\,x_i^\top x_j\qquad\text{subject to}\qquad\sum_i\alpha_iy_i=0.$$

At the optimum,

$$w=\sum_i\alpha_iy_ix_i,$$

and \(\alpha_i>0\) only for points on the edges of the street, \(y_i(w^\top x_i+b)=1\): the support vectors.

The offset is \(b=y_s-w^\top x_s\) for any support vector \(x_s\), and the classifier is

$$f(x)=\operatorname{sign}\Big(\sum_{i}\alpha_iy_i\,x_i^\top x+b\Big).$$

Write the constraints as \(g_i=1-y_i(w^\top x_i+b)\le0\). The Lagrangian is

$$\mathcal L(w,b,\alpha)=\tfrac12\|w\|^2-\sum_i\alpha_i\big[y_i(w^\top x_i+b)-1\big].$$

For fixed \(\alpha\ge0\), minimise over \(w\) and \(b\). Setting

$$\nabla_w\mathcal L=w-\sum_i\alpha_iy_ix_i=0$$

gives \(w=\sum_i\alpha_iy_ix_i\). The Lagrangian is linear in \(b\) with slope \(-\sum_i\alpha_iy_i\); unless that is zero the minimum over \(b\) is \(-\infty\), so the dual is only interesting when \(\sum\alpha_iy_i=0\). Substituting,

$$\mathcal L=\tfrac12\Big\|\sum_i\alpha_iy_ix_i\Big\|^2-\sum_{i,j}\alpha_i\alpha_jy_iy_jx_i^\top x_j+\sum_i\alpha_i=\sum_i\alpha_i-\tfrac12\sum_{i,j}\alpha_i\alpha_jy_iy_jx_i^\top x_j.$$

The problem is convex with affine constraints, so strong duality holds whenever the data are separable.

Complementary slackness,

$$\alpha_i\big[y_i(w^\top x_i+b)-1\big]=0,$$

says \(\alpha_i>0\) only when the constraint is tight, which gives the support vectors and the formula for \(b\). \(\square\)

The dual has two features that matter more than the derivation. The solution is a weighted combination of the support vectors alone, often a small fraction of the data. And the data appear only through the inner products \(x_i^\top x_j\) and \(x_i^\top x\). Hold on to the second point; it is the door to Section 4.

Real data are rarely separable. The soft-margin SVM (Cortes and Vapnik, 1995) lets point \(i\) violate its constraint by a slack \(\xi_i\ge0\), at a price \(C\) per unit:

$$\min_{w,b,\xi}\ \tfrac12\|w\|^2+C\sum_i\xi_i\qquad\text{subject to}\qquad y_i(w^\top x_i+b)\ge1-\xi_i,\quad\xi_i\ge0.$$
proposition 7.5 — the soft margin is hinge loss plus a ridge penalty

The soft-margin SVM is equivalent to the unconstrained problem

$$\min_{w,b}\ \sum_i\max\big(0,1-y_i(w^\top x_i+b)\big)+\frac1{2C}\|w\|^2,$$

hinge loss (Lecture 5) with an \(\ell_2\) penalty.

Its dual is the hard-margin dual with the extra box constraint \(0\le\alpha_i\le C\). At the optimum,

  • points outside the street have \(\alpha_i=0\);
  • points with \(0<\alpha_i<C\) lie exactly on its edges (a point on an edge may have any \(\alpha_i\in[0,C]\));
  • points inside it or misclassified have \(\alpha_i=C\).

For fixed \(w,b\), the best slack is the smallest allowed,

$$\xi_i=\max(0,1-y_i(w^\top x_i+b));$$

substituting and dividing by \(C\) gives the hinge form.

For the dual, the Lagrangian gains terms

$$C\sum\xi_i-\sum\alpha_i\xi_i-\sum\nu_i\xi_i$$

with \(\nu_i\ge0\) for the constraints \(\xi_i\ge0\). Setting its derivative in \(\xi_i\) to zero gives \(\alpha_i=C-\nu_i\le C\), and the \(\xi\) terms then vanish, leaving the same dual objective.

Complementary slackness on the two families of constraints gives the three cases: \(\alpha_i<C\) forces \(\nu_i>0\), so \(\xi_i=0\); \(\alpha_i>0\) forces the margin constraint to be tight. \(\square\)

So \(C\) is an inverse regularisation strength. Small \(C\) means a wide street and many points allowed inside it (high bias, low variance); large \(C\) approaches the hard margin. Here is the breast-cancer data of Lectures 4–6 with standardised features.

history — from the generalised portrait to the kernel era

Vapnik and Lerner's "generalised portrait" algorithm (1963) already found the maximum-margin hyperplane. The kernel idea appeared separately in 1964, when Aizerman, Braverman and Rozonoer, at the same Moscow Institute of Control Sciences, interpreted "potential functions" as inner products in a feature space. The two were combined only in 1992, when Bernhard Boser, Isabelle Guyon and Vapnik at AT&T Bell Labs applied the kernel trick to maximum-margin classifiers; Corinna Cortes and Vapnik added the soft margin in 1995. SVMs then matched hand-engineered systems on digit recognition with no image-specific design, and the 2000s became the "kernel era" of machine learning, until deep networks overtook them on images and speech around 2012. Platt's SMO algorithm (1998), which solves the dual two multipliers at a time, made them practical; LIBSVM, still behind scikit-learn's SVC, uses a refined version, as do this lecture's widgets.

4 — Feature maps and the kernel trick

A line cannot separate points that are positive far from the origin and negative near it. But give each point a second coordinate, its square, and the classes become separable by a horizontal line in the new plane. A linear classifier on the lifted features \(\varphi(x)=(x,x^2)\) is a quadratic classifier on \(x\).

Lifting a line into a parabola

Slide the lift from 0 (the points on the line) to 1 (each point raised to \((x,x^2)\)). Then move the amber line. A horizontal line in the lifted plane is a pair of thresholds back on the axis.

Any feature map \(\varphi:\mathcal X\to\mathbb R^D\) can be fed to the SVM by replacing \(x\) with \(\varphi(x)\). The catch is that rich feature maps are huge: all monomials of degree at most 3 in 30 variables already number 5,456. Theorem 7.4 shows the way out. The dual and the classifier use the data only through inner products, so all we ever need is the function

$$k(x,z)=\varphi(x)^\top\varphi(z),$$

and if \(k\) can be computed without forming \(\varphi\), the dimension never matters. This is the kernel trick. The kernel SVM solves the dual with \(x_i^\top x_j\) replaced by \(k(x_i,x_j)\) and predicts with \(\operatorname{sign}\big(\sum_i\alpha_iy_ik(x_i,x)+b\big)\).

proposition 7.6 — the polynomial kernel

For \(x,z\in\mathbb R^p\) and an integer \(d\ge1\),

$$k(x,z)=(x^\top z+1)^d$$

equals \(\varphi(x)^\top\varphi(z)\) for a feature map \(\varphi\) whose coordinates are all monomials of degree at most \(d\) in \(x\), each multiplied by the square root of a multinomial coefficient.

For \(p=2\), \(d=2\):

$$\varphi(x)=\big(1,\ \sqrt2x_1,\ \sqrt2x_2,\ x_1^2,\ x_2^2,\ \sqrt2x_1x_2\big).$$

Write \(x^\top z+1=\sum_{j=0}^px_jz_j\) with \(x_0=z_0=1\). By the multinomial theorem,

$$(\sum_jx_jz_j)^d=\sum_{|m|=d}\binom d{m}\prod_j(x_jz_j)^{m_j}=\sum_{|m|=d}\Big(\sqrt{\tbinom dm}\prod_jx_j^{m_j}\Big)\Big(\sqrt{\tbinom dm}\prod_jz_j^{m_j}\Big),$$

a sum of products of matching features. Dropping \(x_0=1\), each \(m\) gives a monomial of degree at most \(d\) in \(x_1,\dots,x_p\). \(\square\)

Computing \((x^\top z+1)^3\) on 30 features costs about 30 multiplications instead of 5,456. The most widely used kernel goes further. The Gaussian or RBF kernel

$$k(x,z)=\exp\big(-\gamma\|x-z\|^2\big)$$

corresponds to an infinite-dimensional feature map: in one dimension, \(e^{-\gamma(x-z)^2}=e^{-\gamma x^2}e^{-\gamma z^2}\sum_{m\ge0}\frac{(2\gamma)^m}{m!}x^mz^m\), an inner product of the features \(e^{-\gamma x^2}\sqrt{(2\gamma)^m/m!}\,x^m\), \(m=0,1,2,\dots\). The RBF classifier \(\sum\alpha_iy_ie^{-\gamma\|x_i-x\|^2}+b\) is a sum of bumps centred on the support vectors, and \(\gamma\) sets their width. Small \(\gamma\) gives wide bumps and a smooth, nearly linear boundary; large \(\gamma\) gives narrow bumps that can wrap around individual points. It is a bias–variance dial, like \(k\) in nearest neighbours.

5 — Which functions are kernels?

We can now design \(k\) directly, as a similarity between objects: strings, graphs, molecules, images. But not every similarity is an inner product in some feature space, and the SVM's optimisation is only convex if it is.

definition 7.2 — positive semi-definite kernel

A symmetric function \(k:\mathcal X\times\mathcal X\to\mathbb R\) is a (positive semi-definite) kernel if for every \(n\) and every \(x_1,\dots,x_n\in\mathcal X\), the Gram matrix \(K_{ij}=k(x_i,x_j)\) is positive semi-definite:

$$c^\top Kc=\sum_{i,j}c_ic_jk(x_i,x_j)\ge0$$

for all \(c\in\mathbb R^n\).

theorem 7.7 — kernels are exactly inner products of feature maps

\(k\) is a positive semi-definite kernel if and only if there is a Hilbert space \(\mathcal H\) and a map \(\varphi:\mathcal X\to\mathcal H\) with

$$k(x,z)=\langle\varphi(x),\varphi(z)\rangle_{\mathcal H}.$$

(\(\Leftarrow\))

$$\sum_{i,j}c_ic_j\langle\varphi(x_i),\varphi(x_j)\rangle=\big\|\sum_ic_i\varphi(x_i)\big\|^2\ge0.$$

(\(\Rightarrow\), sketch; Aronszajn, 1950.) Take \(\varphi(x)=k(x,\cdot)\), the function "similarity to \(x\)". On finite combinations \(f=\sum_ia_ik(x_i,\cdot)\) and \(g=\sum_jb_jk(z_j,\cdot)\) define

$$\langle f,g\rangle=\sum_{i,j}a_ib_jk(x_i,z_j).$$

Positive semi-definiteness makes this a valid inner product (the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality shows \(\langle f,f\rangle=0\) forces \(f=0\)), and by construction

$$\langle k(x,\cdot),k(z,\cdot)\rangle=k(x,z).$$

Completing the space gives \(\mathcal H\). \(\square\)

The space built in the proof deserves a name, because papers use it constantly.

definition 7.3 — reproducing kernel Hilbert space

The RKHS \(\mathcal H_k\) of a kernel \(k\) is the completion of the span of the functions \(k(x,\cdot)\), with the inner product above.

Its defining property is the reproducing property: for every \(f\in\mathcal H_k\) and \(x\in\mathcal X\),

$$f(x)=\langle f,k(x,\cdot)\rangle_{\mathcal H_k}.$$

The norm \(\|f\|_{\mathcal H_k}\) measures how "rough" \(f\) is relative to the kernel: for the RBF kernel, functions with small RKHS norm are smooth on the length scale \(1/\sqrt\gamma\).

Proving a function is a kernel from the definition is tedious. In practice one builds kernels from simpler ones.

proposition 7.8 — building kernels

If \(k_1,k_2\) are kernels, \(c\ge0\) and \(f:\mathcal X\to\mathbb R\), then these are kernels: \(ck_1\), \(k_1+k_2\), \(k_1k_2\), \(f(x)k_1(x,z)f(z)\), any limit of kernels, and \(\exp(k_1)\).

Consequently \(x^\top z\), \((x^\top z+1)^d\) and \(\exp(-\gamma\|x-z\|^2)\) are kernels.

Scaling and sums:

$$c^\top(aK_1+K_2)c=ac^\top K_1c+c^\top K_2c\ge0.$$

Products: the Gram matrix of \(k_1k_2\) is the elementwise (Schur) product \(K_1\circ K_2\). Writing \(K_2=\sum_\ell\lambda_\ell u_\ell u_\ell^\top\) with \(\lambda_\ell\ge0\),

$$c^\top(K_1\circ K_2)c=\sum_\ell\lambda_\ell(c\circ u_\ell)^\top K_1(c\circ u_\ell)\ge0.$$

The \(f\) rule: \(c^\top Kc\) with \(c_i\) replaced by \(c_if(x_i)\). Limits preserve \(\ge0\). \(\exp(k_1)=\sum_m k_1^m/m!\) is a limit of sums of products.

Finally,

$$e^{-\gamma\|x-z\|^2}=e^{-\gamma\|x\|^2}\,e^{2\gamma x^\top z}\,e^{-\gamma\|z\|^2},$$

the \(f\) rule applied to \(\exp(2\gamma x^\top z)\). \(\square\)

theorem 7.9 — Mercer's theorem

Let \(\mathcal X\) be a compact set with a finite measure \(\mu\) and \(k\) a continuous kernel. Then there are nonnegative eigenvalues \(\lambda_1\ge\lambda_2\ge\cdots\to0\) and orthonormal eigenfunctions \(e_j\) of the integral operator

$$(T_kf)(x)=\int k(x,z)f(z)\,d\mu(z)$$

such that

$$k(x,z)=\sum_{j\ge1}\lambda_j\,e_j(x)\,e_j(z),$$

with absolute and uniform convergence.

So \(\varphi(x)=(\sqrt{\lambda_j}\,e_j(x))_{j\ge1}\) is an explicit feature map (Mercer, 1909).

Mercer's theorem is the spectral theorem of Lecture 2 for an infinite matrix. It also explains what the RKHS norm penalises: writing \(f=\sum_ja_je_j\), one has \(\|f\|_{\mathcal H_k}^2=\sum_ja_j^2/\lambda_j\), so components along eigenfunctions with small eigenvalues (for the RBF kernel, the wiggly ones) are expensive.

6 — The representer theorem and kernel ridge regression

Why should the solution of a learning problem in an infinite-dimensional space be a combination of kernels centred at the training points? It was for the SVM by duality, and the same holds for a whole family of problems.

theorem 7.10 — the representer theorem

Let \(L\) be any loss of the predictions on the training points and \(\Omega\) a strictly increasing function. Every minimiser over \(f\in\mathcal H_k\) of

$$L\big(f(x_1),\dots,f(x_n)\big)+\Omega\big(\|f\|_{\mathcal H_k}\big)$$

has the form

$$f(x)=\sum_{i=1}^n\alpha_ik(x_i,x)$$

(Kimeldorf and Wahba, 1971; Schölkopf, Herbrich and Smola, 2001).

Split any \(f\in\mathcal H_k\) as \(f=f_\parallel+f_\perp\), where \(f_\parallel\) lies in the span of \(k(x_1,\cdot),\dots,k(x_n,\cdot)\) and \(f_\perp\) is orthogonal to it.

By the reproducing property,

$$f(x_i)=\langle f_\parallel+f_\perp,k(x_i,\cdot)\rangle=f_\parallel(x_i),$$

so the loss does not see \(f_\perp\). But

$$\|f\|^2=\|f_\parallel\|^2+\|f_\perp\|^2,$$

so \(\Omega\) strictly prefers \(f_\perp=0\). Hence every minimiser has \(f_\perp=0\). \(\square\)

An infinite-dimensional problem becomes an \(n\)-dimensional one. With squared loss it can be solved in closed form.

proposition 7.11 — kernel ridge regression

The minimiser of

$$\sum_i\big(y_i-f(x_i)\big)^2+\lambda\|f\|_{\mathcal H_k}^2$$

is

$$\hat f(x)=\sum_i\alpha_ik(x_i,x)=k(x)^\top\alpha,$$

with

$$\alpha=(K+\lambda I)^{-1}y,$$

where \(K\) is the Gram matrix and \(k(x)=(k(x_1,x),\dots,k(x_n,x))^\top\).

With the linear kernel \(k(x,z)=x^\top z\) this is exactly the ridge regression of Lecture 3.

By Theorem 7.10, \(f=\sum_j\alpha_jk(x_j,\cdot)\), so \(f(x_i)=(K\alpha)_i\) and \(\|f\|^2=\alpha^\top K\alpha\). The objective \(\|y-K\alpha\|^2+\lambda\alpha^\top K\alpha\) has gradient

$$-2K(y-K\alpha)+2\lambda K\alpha=-2K\big(y-(K+\lambda I)\alpha\big),$$

which vanishes at \(\alpha=(K+\lambda I)^{-1}y\).

For the linear kernel, \(K=XX^\top\) and

$$\hat f(x)=x^\top X^\top(XX^\top+\lambda I)^{-1}y=x^\top(X^\top X+\lambda I)^{-1}X^\top y$$

by the identity of Exercise 6. \(\square\)

proposition 7.12 — complexity of a kernel class

For \(\mathcal F_B=\{f\in\mathcal H_k:\|f\|_{\mathcal H_k}\le B\}\) and a sample \(x_1,\dots,x_n\),

$$\widehat{\mathfrak R}_S(\mathcal F_B)\le\frac Bn\sqrt{\operatorname{tr}K}=\frac Bn\sqrt{\sum_ik(x_i,x_i)}.$$

7 — Gaussian processes

Kernel ridge regression gives a single function. The Bayesian version gives a distribution over functions, and with it, error bars. The idea is to put a prior directly on the unknown function: a random function whose values at any finite set of points are jointly Gaussian, with the kernel as their covariance.

definition 7.4 — Gaussian process

A Gaussian process \(f\sim\mathcal{GP}(m,k)\) is a random function such that for every finite set of inputs \(x_1,\dots,x_n\), the vector

$$(f(x_1),\dots,f(x_n))$$

is multivariate normal with mean \((m(x_i))_i\) and covariance matrix \(K_{ij}=k(x_i,x_j)\).

The kernel must be positive semi-definite, since covariance matrices are.

The kernel encodes what we believe about the function before seeing data. An RBF kernel with length scale \(\ell\) says the function is smooth and varies on scales of about \(\ell\); a periodic kernel says it repeats; a sum of kernels says the function is a sum of independent components of those kinds. Observing \(y_i=f(x_i)+\varepsilon_i\) with Gaussian noise of variance \(\sigma^2\) and conditioning gives the posterior in closed form.

theorem 7.13 — the GP posterior, and its link to kernel ridge regression

Let \(f\sim\mathcal{GP}(0,k)\) and \(y_i=f(x_i)+\varepsilon_i\) with \(\varepsilon_i\sim\mathcal N(0,\sigma^2)\) independent. Given the data, \(f(x)\) is Gaussian with

$$\mathbb E[f(x)\mid y]=k(x)^\top(K+\sigma^2I)^{-1}y,\qquad\operatorname{Var}[f(x)\mid y]=k(x,x)-k(x)^\top(K+\sigma^2I)^{-1}k(x).$$

The posterior mean is exactly the kernel ridge regression prediction with \(\lambda=\sigma^2\).

The vector \((f(x),y)\) is jointly Gaussian with mean zero and covariance

$$\begin{pmatrix}k(x,x)&k(x)^\top\\k(x)&K+\sigma^2I\end{pmatrix},$$

since \(\operatorname{Cov}(f(x),y_i)=k(x,x_i)\) and \(\operatorname{Cov}(y)=K+\sigma^2I\).

The conditional distribution of one block of a Gaussian vector given another is Gaussian with mean \(\Sigma_{12}\Sigma_{22}^{-1}y\) and variance \(\Sigma_{11}-\Sigma_{12}\Sigma_{22}^{-1}\Sigma_{21}\) (the regression formulas of Lecture 3).

Comparing with Proposition 7.11, \(\alpha=(K+\sigma^2I)^{-1}y\). \(\square\)

The posterior variance does not depend on \(y\): it measures only how close \(x\) is, in the kernel's sense, to where data were collected. Kernel hyperparameters (length scales, signal and noise variances) can be chosen by maximising the marginal likelihood of the data, the probability of \(y\) with \(f\) integrated out:

$$\log p(y)=-\tfrac12y^\top(K+\sigma^2I)^{-1}y-\tfrac12\log\det(K+\sigma^2I)-\tfrac n2\log2\pi.$$

The first term rewards fitting the data; the second penalises flexible kernels, which spread their prior probability over many possible data sets. It is Occam's razor built into Bayes' rule, and it replaces cross-validation with a single optimisation.

8 — Kernels at scale, in deep learning and on quantum computers

Random features

Exact kernel methods cost \(O(n^3)\) time and \(O(n^2)\) memory, impractical beyond a few tens of thousands of points. Rahimi and Recht (2007) turned the kernel trick around: approximate \(k\) by an explicit, finite random feature map, then use a linear method.

theorem 7.14 — Bochner's theorem and random Fourier features

A continuous shift-invariant function \(k(x,z)=\kappa(x-z)\) on \(\mathbb R^p\) with \(\kappa(0)=1\) is a kernel if and only if \(\kappa\) is the Fourier transform of a probability distribution \(\rho\):

$$\kappa(\delta)=\mathbb E_{\omega\sim\rho}\cos(\omega^\top\delta)$$

(Bochner, 1933).

Consequently, with \(\omega_1,\dots,\omega_D\sim\rho\) and \(b_j\sim\mathrm{Unif}[0,2\pi]\) independent, the features

$$z(x)=\sqrt{2/D}\,\big(\cos(\omega_1^\top x+b_1),\dots,\cos(\omega_D^\top x+b_D)\big)$$

satisfy

$$\mathbb E\,z(x)^\top z(x')=k(x,x'),$$

with error of order \(1/\sqrt D\).

For the RBF kernel \(e^{-\|x-z\|^2/(2s^2)}\), \(\rho=\mathcal N(0,s^{-2}I)\).

$$2\cos(a+b)\cos(c+b)=\cos(a-c)+\cos(a+c+2b),$$

and averaging over \(b\sim\mathrm{Unif}[0,2\pi]\) kills the second term. So each coordinate pair contributes

$$\frac2D\cdot\frac12\mathbb E\cos(\omega^\top(x-x'))=\frac1D\kappa(x-x'),$$

and summing over \(D\) coordinates gives \(\kappa(x-x')\). \(\square\)

The neural tangent kernel

The link to networks goes further. Train a network \(f(x;\theta)\) by gradient descent on squared loss. If the network is very wide and the parameters barely move from their initial values \(\theta_0\), a first-order Taylor expansion \(f(x;\theta)\approx f(x;\theta_0)+\nabla_\theta f(x;\theta_0)^\top(\theta-\theta_0)\) is accurate, and the network is a linear model in the features \(\nabla_\theta f(x;\theta_0)\).

theorem 7.15 — the neural tangent kernel (informal)

For fully connected networks in the "NTK parameterisation", as the widths of all hidden layers tend to infinity, the kernel

$$\Theta(x,x')=\nabla_\theta f(x;\theta)^\top\nabla_\theta f(x';\theta)$$

converges to a deterministic limit and stays constant during training.

Gradient flow on squared loss then makes the network's function evolve exactly as kernel regression with kernel \(\Theta\), and the trained infinitely wide network equals the minimum-norm kernel interpolant (Jacot, Gabriel and Hongler, 2018; Lee et al., 2019).

The NTK made over-parameterised networks analysable with the tools of this lecture and Lecture 6. Its limitation is also its lesson: in this regime the features do not change, so the network does not learn representations, and finite networks that do (Chizat, Oyallon and Bach, 2019, call the NTK regime "lazy training") usually beat their NTK on real data. Why feature learning helps is one of the central open questions of deep learning theory.

Quantum kernels

A quantum computer can prepare a state \(|\varphi(x)\rangle\) in a space of dimension \(2^q\) by running a circuit whose gate angles depend on \(x\), and estimate overlaps \(|\langle\varphi(x)|\varphi(z)\rangle|^2\) by repeated measurement. That overlap is a kernel (it is \(\langle\rho(x),\rho(z)\rangle\) for the density matrices \(\rho=|\varphi\rangle\langle\varphi|\)), so any quantum feature map defines a kernel SVM. The code simulates the "ZZ feature map" of Havlíček et al. (2019) exactly on the breast-cancer data, compressed to \(q\) principal components.

code — a simulated quantum kernel 
# A quantum kernel, simulated exactly: the ZZ feature map of Havlicek et al. (2019) on q qubits
from functools import reduce
H = np.array([[1, 1], [1, -1]]) / np.sqrt(2)

def zz_state(x, reps=2):
    q = len(x)
    bits = (np.arange(2**q)[:, None] >> np.arange(q)[::-1]) & 1
    z = 1 - 2 * bits                                           # eigenvalues of Z on each qubit: +1 or -1
    phase = z @ x                                              # sum_i x_i Z_i
    for i in range(q):
        for j in range(i + 1, q):
            phase = phase + (np.pi - x[i]) * (np.pi - x[j]) * z[:, i] * z[:, j]   # the ZZ couplings
    Hq = reduce(np.kron, [H] * q)
    psi = np.zeros(2**q); psi[0] = 1
    for _ in range(reps):
        psi = np.exp(1j * phase) * (Hq @ psi)                  # layer: Hadamards, then diagonal phases
    return psi

def quantum_kernel(A, B):
    SA, SB = np.array([zz_state(a) for a in A]), np.array([zz_state(b) for b in B])
    return np.abs(SA.conj() @ SB.T) ** 2                       # k(x, z) = |<phi(x)|phi(z)>|^2

The standard deviation of the off-diagonal kernel values falls from 0.27 to 0.007 as \(q\) goes from 2 to 8: in a space of dimension \(2^q\), random-looking states are nearly orthogonal, so every pair of patients looks equally dissimilar and the Gram matrix approaches the identity. On real hardware each entry would be estimated from measurement shots, and resolving differences of 0.007 needs tens of thousands of shots per entry.

quantum — a quantum model is a kernel method

Schuld (2021) observed that most supervised quantum models, which encode data with a circuit and then measure an observable, are linear models in the feature space \(\rho(x)\), and hence kernel methods: by the representer theorem, the best such model is a kernel machine with the quantum kernel. The question becomes when a quantum kernel is both hard to compute classically and useful. Liu, Arunachalam and Temme (2021) proved a rigorous advantage for a kernel built around the discrete logarithm, a problem believed to be classically hard. Huang et al. (2021) showed that, given data, classical learners can often match quantum kernels, and proposed a test for when they cannot. Thanasilp et al. (2024) proved that many feature maps suffer exactly the exponential concentration seen above, making them untrainable at scale. Quantum kernels are an active research area precisely because the classical theory of this lecture says what would have to be true for them to help.

causal — kernels as independence tests

Causal discovery (Lecture 20) needs tests of whether two variables are independent, or independent given a third, without assuming linear relationships. Kernels supply them. The Hilbert–Schmidt independence criterion (Gretton et al., 2005) is the squared norm of the cross-covariance between the RKHS features of \(X\) and \(Y\); with a characteristic kernel such as the RBF, it is zero if and only if \(X\) and \(Y\) are independent, so it detects nonlinear dependence that correlation misses. The kernel conditional independence test of Zhang et al. (2011) extends this and is a standard choice inside the PC algorithm. In treatment-effect estimation, kernel balancing chooses weights that make the treated and control groups' kernel mean embeddings match, a nonlinear version of the covariate balance checks of Lecture 4.

big picture — where kernels live today

Deep networks displaced SVMs on images, speech and text, but kernels remain the method of choice when data are small and structured. Computational biology classifies protein sequences with string kernels (Leslie, Eskin and Noble, 2002) and molecules with graph kernels. Gaussian processes are the standard surrogate model in Bayesian optimisation, in geostatistics (where they are called kriging), and as fast emulators of expensive climate and engineering simulations. Kernel two-sample tests based on the maximum mean discrepancy check whether a generative model's samples match real data and detect dataset shift. And the theory of deep learning still speaks in kernels: NTK analyses, the random-feature models behind double descent, and the "kernel regime" against "feature learning regime" distinction appear throughout current papers.

9 — Summary, exercises, projects, reading

summary
  • The hard-margin SVM minimises \(\frac12\|w\|^2\) subject to \(y_i(w^\top x_i+b)\ge1\), maximising the street width \(2/\|w\|\). The soft margin adds slack at price \(C\) and is hinge loss with a ridge penalty (Proposition 7.5).
  • Lagrange duality and the KKT conditions (Theorem 7.2) give the dual SVM (Theorem 7.4): \(w=\sum\alpha_iy_ix_i\), with \(\alpha_i>0\) only for support vectors, and the data enter only through inner products.
  • The kernel trick replaces \(x^\top z\) by \(k(x,z)=\varphi(x)^\top\varphi(z)\). Polynomial kernels have \(\binom{p+d}d\) features; the RBF kernel has infinitely many, and \(\gamma\) is a bias–variance dial.
  • \(k\) is a kernel iff all Gram matrices are positive semi-definite (Theorem 7.7); kernels are closed under sums, products and limits (Proposition 7.8); Mercer's theorem is the spectral theorem for kernels.
  • The representer theorem (7.10) reduces regularised learning in an RKHS to \(n\) coefficients; kernel ridge regression has \(\alpha=(K+\lambda I)^{-1}y\).
  • A Gaussian process posterior mean equals kernel ridge regression with \(\lambda=\sigma^2\) (Theorem 7.13), plus a variance; hyperparameters come from the marginal likelihood.
  • Random Fourier features (Theorem 7.14) make kernels scale; wide networks behave like kernel machines with the NTK (Theorem 7.15); quantum models are kernel methods, and their kernels can concentrate.

Next: Lecture 8 turns to a very different way of building flexible models: decision trees, which split feature space into boxes, and the forests and boosted ensembles that make them the strongest methods for tabular data.

exercises 7
  1. Find the hard-margin SVM for the two points \(x_1=(1,1)\), \(y_1=+1\) and \(x_2=(-1,-1)\), \(y_2=-1\), and check that the street width is the distance between them.

    By symmetry \(b=0\) and \(w=c(1,1)\). Both constraints read \(2c\ge1\), so the smallest \(\|w\|\) has \(c=\tfrac12\), \(w=(\tfrac12,\tfrac12)\), \(\|w\|=1/\sqrt2\), width \(2\sqrt2\), which is \(\|x_1-x_2\|\). In the dual, \(\sum\alpha_iy_i=0\) forces \(\alpha_1=\alpha_2=\alpha\), and \(w=\alpha x_1-\alpha x_2=2\alpha(1,1)\), so \(\alpha=\tfrac14\).

  2. Show that at the hard-margin optimum \(\sum_i\alpha_i=\|w\|^2\), so the street width is \(2/\sqrt{\sum_i\alpha_i}\). Check with Exercise 1.

    Complementary slackness gives \(\alpha_iy_i(w^\top x_i+b)=\alpha_i\) for every \(i\). Summing, \(w^\top\sum_i\alpha_iy_ix_i+b\sum_i\alpha_iy_i=\sum_i\alpha_i\), and the left side is \(w^\top w+0\). In Exercise 1, \(\sum\alpha_i=\tfrac12=\|w\|^2\).

  3. Show that the number of monomials of degree at most \(d\) in \(p\) variables is \(\binom{p+d}d\).

    A monomial of degree at most \(d\) is a multiset of size exactly \(d\) drawn from the \(p\) variables plus a dummy symbol "1" (padding up to degree \(d\)). Multisets of size \(d\) from \(p+1\) symbols number \(\binom{p+1+d-1}d=\binom{p+d}d\) (stars and bars).

  4. Show directly from Proposition 7.8 that \(\exp(-\gamma\|x-z\|^2)\) is a kernel for \(\gamma>0\), and that \(\exp(-\gamma\|x-z\|^2)\,(x^\top z+1)^2\) is too.

    \(2\gamma x^\top z\) is a kernel (scaled linear kernel), so is its exponential, and multiplying by \(f(x)f(z)\) with \(f(x)=e^{-\gamma\|x\|^2}\) gives the RBF kernel. The second is a product of two kernels.

  5. Show that \(k(s,t)=\min(s,t)\) on \([0,\infty)\) is a kernel by finding a feature map into a space of functions. (It is the covariance of Brownian motion.)

    Take \(\varphi(s)=\mathbb 1_{[0,s]}\in L^2[0,\infty)\). Then \(\langle\varphi(s),\varphi(t)\rangle=\int_0^\infty\mathbb 1[u\le s]\mathbb 1[u\le t]\,du=\min(s,t)\).

  6. Prove the "push-through" identity, for \(\lambda>0\), $$(X^\top X+\lambda I)^{-1}X^\top=X^\top(XX^\top+\lambda I)^{-1},$$ and explain why it lets ridge regression with \(p\gg n\) features be solved with an \(n\times n\) system.

    \(X^\top(XX^\top+\lambda I)=(X^\top X+\lambda I)X^\top\); multiply on the left by \((X^\top X+\lambda I)^{-1}\) and on the right by \((XX^\top+\lambda I)^{-1}\). The left form inverts a \(p\times p\) matrix, the right an \(n\times n\) one; kernel ridge regression always uses the right form.

  7. A GP with \(k(x,x)=1\) observes a single value \(y\) at \(x\) with noise variance \(\sigma^2\). Find the posterior mean and variance of \(f(x)\).

    Mean \(\frac1{1+\sigma^2}y\), variance \(1-\frac1{1+\sigma^2}=\frac{\sigma^2}{1+\sigma^2}\). With \(\sigma^2=1\) the posterior splits the difference between prior mean 0 and observation \(y\), and the variance halves: two equally reliable sources of information.

  8. Show that a GP with the linear kernel \(k(x,z)=\tau^2x^\top z\) is Bayesian linear regression with prior \(w\sim\mathcal N(0,\tau^2I)\), and that its posterior mean is the ridge estimate of Lecture 3 with \(\lambda=\sigma^2/\tau^2\).

    If \(f(x)=w^\top x\) with \(w\sim\mathcal N(0,\tau^2I)\), then \(\operatorname{Cov}(f(x),f(z))=\tau^2x^\top z\), so \(f\) is this GP. Theorem 7.13 gives mean \(\tau^2x^\top X^\top(\tau^2XX^\top+\sigma^2I)^{-1}y=x^\top(X^\top X+\tfrac{\sigma^2}{\tau^2}I)^{-1}X^\top y\) by the push-through identity: ridge with \(\lambda=\sigma^2/\tau^2\), as in Theorem 3.7.

  9. In the RBF widget, set \(\gamma=50\), \(C=100\) and then \(\gamma=0.1\), \(C=0.1\). Describe each boundary and explain the training and test accuracies in terms of bias and variance.

    \(\gamma=50\): tiny bumps around individual points, 100% training accuracy, 93 support vectors, test accuracy 91.5%: low bias, high variance. \(\gamma=0.1\), \(C=0.1\): a nearly straight boundary that cannot follow the moons' curves, so both accuracies are mediocre: high bias, low variance. The best settings sit between.

  10. A quantum kernel's off-diagonal entries have standard deviation 0.007. Each entry is estimated by repeating the circuit \(N\) times and counting how often the all-zero outcome appears, a binomial proportion. Roughly how large must \(N\) be for the sampling error to be smaller than 0.007, if the entries are around 0.01?

    The standard error of a proportion near \(p=0.01\) is \(\sqrt{p(1-p)/N}\approx0.0995/\sqrt N\). Setting this to 0.007 gives \(N\approx(0.0995/0.007)^2\approx200\) for one standard error; to resolve the differences reliably (say, an error a tenth of the spread) needs about \(2\times10^4\) shots per entry, and a Gram matrix on 400 points has 80,000 distinct entries.
