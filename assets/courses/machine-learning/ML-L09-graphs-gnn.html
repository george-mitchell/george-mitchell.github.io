Lecture 8's graphs were models: a tree of questions. In this lecture the graph is part of the data. Sometimes it is given: friendships, citations, the bonds of a molecule, the stations of a subway. Sometimes we build it, joining each data point to its nearest neighbours. Either way, the graph encodes an assumption familiar from nearest neighbours in Lecture 1: points that are connected tend to share labels. We will make that assumption precise with the graph Laplacian, whose eigenvectors are the smooth "modes" of a graph and whose quadratic form measures how much a labelling disagrees across edges. Minimising that disagreement gives label propagation, which can classify thousands of points from a handful of labels. Making the propagation learnable gives graph neural networks, the deep-learning architecture for molecules, social networks and road maps, and also exposes what such networks fundamentally cannot see.

By the end of this lecture you should be able to encode a graph as matrices and explain why graph models must be permutation-invariant or equivariant; build similarity graphs from data; prove the basic facts about the Laplacian (its quadratic form, its zero eigenvalues) and read its eigenvectors; derive and solve the harmonic label-propagation problem, prove it is well posed, and recognise when it fails; explain random walks, diffusion and PageRank on graphs; implement a graph convolutional network and describe message passing; and classify whole graphs with a readout, knowing the Weisfeiler–Leman limit on what message passing can distinguish.

1 — Encoding graphs

definition 9.1 — graphs as matrices

A (simple, undirected) graph \(G=(V,E)\) has nodes \(V=\{1,\dots,n\}\) and edges

$$E\subseteq\{\{i,j\}:i\ne j\}.$$

It is encoded by:

  • the adjacency matrix \(A\in\{0,1\}^{n\times n}\), with \(A_{ij}=1\) if \(\{i,j\}\in E\); it is symmetric with zero diagonal (a weighted graph has \(W_{ij}\ge0\) instead);
  • the degree of node \(i\), \(d_i=\sum_jA_{ij}\), and the diagonal degree matrix \(D=\operatorname{diag}(d_1,\dots,d_n)\);
  • a node feature matrix \(X\in\mathbb R^{n\times p}\), whose row \(i\) describes node \(i\) (an atom type, a paper's words), and optionally edge features (bond types, distances).

The numbering of the nodes is arbitrary. Relabelling them by a permutation \(\pi\), with permutation matrix \(P\) (\(P_{i,\pi(i)}=1\)), replaces \(A\) by \(PAP^\top\) and \(X\) by \(PX\), and describes exactly the same graph. A model that gave different answers for the two descriptions would be answering a question about our bookkeeping, not about the graph.

definition 9.2 — invariance and equivariance

A function \(F(A,X)\) is permutation-invariant if

$$F(PAP^\top,PX)=F(A,X)$$

for every permutation matrix \(P\); this is what a graph-level prediction (is this molecule toxic?) must satisfy.

It is permutation-equivariant if

$$F(PAP^\top,PX)=P\,F(A,X);$$

this is what node-level outputs (one row per node) must satisfy: relabel the nodes and the outputs are relabelled the same way.

The simplest equivariant layer mixes each node's features with its neighbours' and then applies the same transformation to every node.

proposition 9.1 — message-passing layers are equivariant; sum readouts are invariant

Let \(\hat A=A+I\), \(W\) a weight matrix and \(\sigma\) any function applied entrywise. The layer

$$F(A,X)=\sigma(\hat AXW)$$

is permutation-equivariant, and so is any composition of such layers.

The sum readout

$$r(H)=\mathbf 1^\top H=\sum_iH_{i,:}$$

is permutation-invariant, so a stack of layers followed by a sum readout is a permutation-invariant graph-level model. Mean and max readouts are invariant too.

Permutation matrices satisfy \(P^\top P=I\), so

$$(PAP^\top+I)PXW=P(A+I)P^\top PXW=P\hat AXW.$$

Applying \(\sigma\) entrywise commutes with reordering rows: \(\sigma(PM)=P\sigma(M)\). Hence

$$F(PAP^\top,PX)=PF(A,X),$$

and composing equivariant maps gives an equivariant map.

For the readout, \(\mathbf 1^\top P=\mathbf 1^\top\) (each column of \(P\) contains a single 1), so \(\mathbf 1^\top PH=\mathbf 1^\top H\). The mean is the sum divided by \(n\), and the maximum over rows does not depend on their order. \(\square\)

2 — From data to graphs

When the data are points \(x_1,\dots,x_n\in\mathbb R^p\) rather than a network, we build the graph ourselves from a notion of similarity.

definition 9.3 — similarity graphs
  • \(\varepsilon\)-graph: join \(i\) and \(j\) when \(\|x_i-x_j\|\le\varepsilon\).
  • \(k\)-nearest-neighbour graph: join \(i\) and \(j\) when either is among the other's \(k\) nearest neighbours (symmetrised so that the graph is undirected).
  • Gaussian weights: give each edge the weight $$W_{ij}=\exp\big(-\|x_i-x_j\|^2/s^2\big),$$ with the bandwidth \(s^2\) often set to the median squared distance between neighbours.

The \(\varepsilon\)-graph is simple but fragile: in regions of low density it falls apart, in dense ones it becomes nearly complete. The kNN graph adapts to density and is the usual choice. Building it naively costs \(O(n^2)\) distance computations; libraries such as FAISS find approximate neighbours among billions of vectors, which is how kNN graphs over images and text embeddings are built in practice. The Gaussian weight is the RBF kernel of Lecture 7 restricted to near neighbours: a sparse similarity matrix.

3 — The graph Laplacian and smoothness

If connected nodes tend to share labels, a good labelling \(f:V\to\mathbb R\) should change little across edges. One matrix measures exactly that.

definition 9.4 — the graph Laplacian

For a weighted graph with weights \(W\) and degrees \(d_i=\sum_jW_{ij}\), the (combinatorial) Laplacian is

$$L=D-W.$$

The normalised Laplacians are

$$L_{\text{sym}}=I-D^{-1/2}WD^{-1/2}$$

and

$$L_{\text{rw}}=I-D^{-1}W.$$
proposition 9.2 — the Laplacian measures roughness

For every \(f\in\mathbb R^n\),

$$f^\top Lf=\frac12\sum_{i,j}W_{ij}(f_i-f_j)^2=\sum_{\{i,j\}\in E}W_{ij}(f_i-f_j)^2\ \ge0.$$

So \(L\) is symmetric positive semi-definite, and \(L\mathbf 1=0\): constant labellings cost nothing.

$$f^\top Lf=\sum_id_if_i^2-\sum_{i,j}W_{ij}f_if_j$$ $$=\frac12\sum_{i,j}W_{ij}f_i^2+\frac12\sum_{i,j}W_{ij}f_j^2-\sum_{i,j}W_{ij}f_if_j$$ $$=\frac12\sum_{i,j}W_{ij}(f_i-f_j)^2,$$

using \(d_i=\sum_jW_{ij}\) and the symmetry of \(W\). Each unordered edge appears twice in the double sum. \(\square\)

The name comes from calculus. On a grid, \((Lf)_i=\sum_j(f_i-f_j)\) is minus the discrete second derivative, and \(f^\top Lf\) is the discrete version of \(\int\|\nabla f\|^2\), the Dirichlet energy. A function with small \(f^\top Lf\) is smooth on the graph, whatever the dimension of the data the graph was built from.

proposition 9.3 — zero eigenvalues count connected components

The multiplicity of the eigenvalue 0 of \(L\) equals the number of connected components of the graph, and its eigenspace is spanned by the indicator vectors of the components.

\(Lf=0\) implies \(f^\top Lf=0\), so by Proposition 9.2 \(f_i=f_j\) for every edge with \(W_{ij}>0\), and hence \(f\) is constant on each connected component. Conversely, any function constant on each component satisfies \(Lf=0\). The component indicators form a basis of these functions, so the dimension of the kernel is the number of components. \(\square\)

4 — Spectra of graphs: what the eigenvectors look like

Since \(L\) is symmetric, it has an orthonormal basis of eigenvectors \(u_1,u_2,\dots,u_n\) with eigenvalues \(0=\lambda_1\le\lambda_2\le\dots\le\lambda_n\) (the spectral theorem of Lecture 2). For a unit eigenvector, \(u_k^\top Lu_k=\lambda_k\), so by Proposition 9.2 the eigenvalue is the roughness of its eigenvector. The eigenvectors play the role of Fourier modes: low eigenvalues give slowly varying patterns across the graph, high eigenvalues give patterns that flip sign across many edges. On a path or a cycle they are literally sines and cosines.

The second eigenvector \(u_2\), the Fiedler vector, is the smoothest labelling that is not constant and is orthogonal to the constants. Its sign therefore splits the graph into two parts with few edges between them, the idea behind spectral clustering (Lecture 15), and its eigenvalue \(\lambda_2\), the algebraic connectivity, measures how well connected the graph is: it is zero exactly when the graph is disconnected (Proposition 9.3).

history — from a karate club to Google

Miroslav Fiedler, a Czech mathematician, introduced the "algebraic connectivity" \(\lambda_2\) in 1973 and showed two years later that the signs of its eigenvector split a graph into connected pieces; Donath and Hoffman had proposed eigenvector partitioning the same year. Wayne Zachary's 1977 study of a karate club that split in two became the most famous small network in science, and network researchers award a mock "karate club club" prize to the first speaker at a conference who uses it. Label propagation entered machine learning with Zhu, Ghahramani and Lafferty's harmonic functions (2003) and Zhou et al.'s "local and global consistency" (2004). Sergey Brin and Larry Page's PageRank (1998), computed by the power iteration of Lecture 2, was the founding algorithm of Google, and an echo of Leo Katz's 1953 status index and Pinski and Narin's 1976 journal rankings.

5 — Semi-supervised learning: harmonic functions and label propagation

Labels are often expensive and unlabelled data cheap: a radiologist labels a few scans, millions are on file. Suppose a few nodes \(\ell\subset V\) carry known labels \(f_\ell\) and the rest \(u=V\setminus\ell\) do not. The smoothness assumption suggests choosing the unknown labels to make the whole labelling as smooth as possible.

definition 9.5 — the harmonic solution

The harmonic solution (Zhu, Ghahramani and Lafferty, 2003) minimises

$$f^\top Lf=\sum_{\{i,j\}}W_{ij}(f_i-f_j)^2$$

subject to \(f_\ell\) fixed. Splitting \(L\) into blocks for labelled and unlabelled nodes, setting the gradient with respect to \(f_u\) to zero gives

$$L_{uu}f_u=W_{u\ell}f_\ell,\qquad\text{equivalently}\qquad f_i=\frac1{d_i}\sum_jW_{ij}f_j\quad\text{for every }i\in u.$$

Each unlabelled node takes the weighted average of its neighbours, which is what "harmonic" means.

For \(K\) classes, solve once per class with \(f_\ell\) the one-hot labels and predict the class with the largest value.

theorem 9.4 — the harmonic solution exists, is unique, and obeys a maximum principle

If every connected component of the graph contains at least one labelled node, then \(L_{uu}\) is invertible, so the harmonic solution exists and is unique, and every value \(f_i\), \(i\in u\), lies between \(\min_{\ell}f_\ell\) and \(\max_\ell f_\ell\).

Moreover \(f_i\) is the probability that a random walk started at \(i\), moving to neighbour \(j\) with probability \(W_{ij}/d_i\), first reaches a labelled node of class 1 (for two classes with labels 0 and 1).

Maximum principle. Let \(f\) satisfy the averaging equations and let \(M=\max_if_i\). If \(M>\max_\ell f_\ell\), the maximum is attained at some unlabelled node \(i\). Being an average of its neighbours, \(f_i\) can equal \(M\) only if every neighbour also has value \(M\); repeating the argument, every node in \(i\)'s component has value \(M\), including the labelled node that the component contains, a contradiction. The same holds for the minimum.

Uniqueness and invertibility. If \(L_{uu}g=0\), then \(g\) on \(u\) together with \(0\) on \(\ell\) solves the averaging equations with all labels 0, so by the maximum principle \(g=0\). Hence \(L_{uu}\) has trivial kernel.

Random walks. Let \(h_i\) be the probability that the walk from \(i\) hits a class-1 node before a class-0 node. Conditioning on the first step,

$$h_i=\sum_j\frac{W_{ij}}{d_i}h_j$$

for unlabelled \(i\), with \(h=f_\ell\) on labelled nodes; by uniqueness \(h=f\). \(\square\)

The averaging form suggests an algorithm: start the unlabelled values anywhere, and repeatedly replace each by the weighted average of its neighbours while holding the labelled values fixed. This is label propagation.

proposition 9.5 — label propagation converges to the harmonic solution

The iteration

$$f_u\leftarrow D_{uu}^{-1}\big(W_{uu}f_u+W_{u\ell}f_\ell\big)$$

converges to the harmonic solution from any starting point, under the condition of Theorem 9.4.

The error \(e=f_u-f_u^*\) evolves as \(e\leftarrow Me\) with \(M=D_{uu}^{-1}W_{uu}\), a nonnegative matrix whose rows sum to at most 1, and to less than 1 for nodes with a labelled neighbour.

\((M^k)_{ij}\) is the probability that a random walk from \(i\) is at \(j\) after \(k\) steps without having touched a labelled node. Since every component contains a labelled node, from any start the walk hits one within \(m=|u|\) steps with probability at least some \(c>0\), so every row of \(M^m\) sums to at most \(1-c\). Hence

$$\|M^{km}\|_\infty\le(1-c)^k\to0,$$

the spectral radius of \(M\) is below 1, and \(e\to0\) geometrically. \(\square\)

import gzip, pickle
with gzip.open('data/mnist.pkl.gz', 'rb') as fh:
    (Xm, ym), _, _ = pickle.load(fh, encoding='latin1')
rng = np.random.default_rng(0)

labelled = np.array([np.where(yd == d)[0][0] for d in range(10)])      # ONE labelled image per digit
unl = np.setdiff1d(np.arange(2000), labelled)

# kNN similarity graph with Gaussian weights
sq = (Xd**2).sum(1); D2 = np.maximum(sq[:, None] + sq[None, :] - 2 * Xd @ Xd.T, 0)
def knn_graph(k):
    nn = np.argsort(D2, axis=1)[:, 1:k + 1]
    s2 = np.median(D2[np.arange(2000)[:, None], nn])
    W = np.zeros((2000, 2000)); rows = np.repeat(np.arange(2000), k)
    W[rows, nn.ravel()] = np.exp(-D2[rows, nn.ravel()] / s2)
    return np.maximum(W, W.T)                                          # symmetrise

def harmonic(W, lab, y_lab):
    """Zhu-Ghahramani-Lafferty: solve L_uu F_u = W_ul F_l, one column per class."""
    L = np.diag(W.sum(1)) - W
    u = np.setdiff1d(np.arange(len(W)), lab)
    return u, np.linalg.solve(L[np.ix_(u, u)], W[np.ix_(u, lab)] @ np.eye(10)[y_lab])

nearest = yd[labelled][np.argmin(D2[np.ix_(unl, labelled)], 1)]
print('1-nearest-neighbour from the 10 labelled images: accuracy', round(np.mean(yd[unl] == nearest), 3))

The plain harmonic solution does worse than simply giving each image the label of its nearest labelled image (37% against 44%), and gets worse as the graph gets denser. The last column shows why: it assigns more than half the images to the digit 1. The harmonic value of a node is a hitting probability, and the labelled "1" sits in a dense, well-connected part of the graph (ones are thin, so they are close to many other images), so random walks from everywhere tend to hit it first. Zhu, Ghahramani and Lafferty anticipated this and proposed class mass normalisation: rescale each class's column so that every class has the same total mass before taking the largest. That one line lifts accuracy to 67% from ten labels. The general lesson, studied by Nadler, Srebro and Zhou (2009), is that with very few labels and much unlabelled data, the harmonic solution becomes almost constant away from the labelled points, and its tiny variations decide everything.

bayes — the harmonic solution is a posterior mean

Put a Gaussian prior on labellings that penalises roughness, \(p(f)\propto\exp\big(-\tfrac12f^\top(L+\varepsilon I)f\big)\): a Gaussian Markov random field, whose precision matrix is the (regularised) Laplacian, so each node is conditionally independent of the rest given its neighbours. Conditioning on the observed labels \(f_\ell\) gives a Gaussian posterior for \(f_u\) with mean \(-(L_{uu}+\varepsilon I)^{-1}L_{u\ell}f_\ell\), which tends to the harmonic solution as \(\varepsilon\to0\) (Exercise 6), and with covariance \((L_{uu}+\varepsilon I)^{-1}\), which says how sure we should be about each propagated label. Zhu, Lafferty and Ghahramani (2003) made this a Gaussian process on the graph, with kernel \((L+\varepsilon I)^{-1}\), linking this lecture to Lecture 7.

6 — Random walks, diffusion and PageRank

Theorem 9.4 already used a random walk. The walk's transition matrix is \(P=D^{-1}W\), whose row \(i\) is the distribution of the next node from \(i\). A distribution \(\pi\) over nodes (a row vector) evolves as \(\pi\leftarrow\pi P\).

proposition 9.6 — the stationary distribution of a walk on an undirected graph

On a connected undirected graph,

$$\pi_i=d_i/\sum_jd_j$$

satisfies \(\pi P=\pi\): in the long run, a random walk spends time at each node in proportion to its degree.

$$(\pi P)_j=\sum_i\frac{d_i}{\sum d}\cdot\frac{W_{ij}}{d_i}=\frac{\sum_iW_{ij}}{\sum d}=\frac{d_j}{\sum d},$$

using the symmetry \(W_{ij}=W_{ji}\). \(\square\)

On the web, links are directed, some pages have no outgoing links, and the walk can get trapped. PageRank fixes this with teleportation: with probability \(\alpha\) (typically 0.85) follow a random outgoing link, otherwise jump to a page chosen uniformly at random.

theorem 9.7 — PageRank is well defined

Let \(P\) be the walk's transition matrix (with dangling nodes sent uniformly to all pages) and

$$G=\alpha P+\frac{1-\alpha}n\mathbf 1\mathbf 1^\top$$

with \(0<\alpha<1\). Then \(G\) has a unique stationary distribution \(\pi\) (\(\pi G=\pi\), \(\sum\pi_i=1\)), every \(\pi_i>0\), and the power iteration \(\pi\leftarrow\pi G\) converges to it from any starting distribution, with error shrinking by a factor \(\alpha\) per step.

Every entry of \(G\) is positive. The Perron–Frobenius theorem for positive matrices says that the eigenvalue 1 of a positive stochastic matrix is simple, with a positive left eigenvector, and every other eigenvalue has modulus less than 1, which gives existence, uniqueness and positivity.

For the rate: for two distributions \(\mu,\nu\), the teleportation part of \(G\) maps both to the same vector, so

$$\mu G-\nu G=\alpha(\mu-\nu)P,$$

and \(\|(\mu-\nu)P\|_1\le\|\mu-\nu\|_1\) because \(P\) is stochastic. \(\square\)

A walk takes discrete steps; heat diffusion is its continuous-time version. Heat \(h(t)\) on the nodes flows across each edge at a rate proportional to the temperature difference, \(\frac{dh}{dt}=-Lh\), so

Each eigenvector decays at the rate of its eigenvalue: rough patterns die quickly, smooth ones slowly, and as \(t\to\infty\) only the constant survives (Exercise 9). quantum — quantum walks: diffusion with a factor of \(i\)

A continuous-time quantum walk on a graph evolves a quantum state by \(|\psi(t)\rangle=e^{-iLt}|\psi(0)\rangle\): the heat equation with time made imaginary, the Schrödinger equation with the Laplacian as Hamiltonian (Farhi and Gutmann, 1998). Where heat spreads diffusively and settles to the uniform distribution, the quantum walk spreads ballistically and keeps interfering with itself. Childs et al. (2003) used this to find an exponential quantum speed-up: on two binary trees glued together at their leaves, a quantum walk crosses from one root to the other in polynomial time, while any classical algorithm needs exponentially many queries. Quantum walks also underlie Grover-type search on graphs and are natural to simulate on quantum hardware, because \(e^{-iLt}\) is exactly the kind of evolution such hardware performs.

7 — Graph neural networks: learnable propagation

Label propagation uses only the graph; a classifier like logistic regression uses only each node's features. Graph neural networks use both, by alternating "mix with your neighbours" with "transform the features", and learning the transformation.

$$H^{(1)}=\mathrm{ReLU}\big(\hat AXW^{(1)}\big),\qquad Z=\operatorname{softmax}\big(\hat AH^{(1)}W^{(2)}\big),\qquad\hat A=\tilde D^{-1/2}(A+I)\tilde D^{-1/2},$$

def gcn_train(seed, hidden=16, lr=0.01, wd=5e-4, p_drop=0.5, epochs=200):
    r = np.random.default_rng(seed)
    W1 = r.uniform(-1, 1, (Xn.shape[1], hidden)) * np.sqrt(6 / (Xn.shape[1] + hidden))   # Glorot initialisation
    W2 = r.uniform(-1, 1, (hidden, 7)) * np.sqrt(6 / (hidden + 7))

    Y1 = np.eye(7)[yc[tr_i]]; best = (-1, None)
    for t in range(1, epochs + 1):
        Z1 = AX @ W1; H = np.maximum(Z1, 0)
        mask = (r.random(H.shape) > p_drop) / (1 - p_drop); Hd = H * mask      # dropout
        Z2 = A_hat @ (Hd @ W2)
        P = np.exp(Z2 - Z2.max(1, keepdims=True)); P /= P.sum(1, keepdims=True)
        dZ2 = np.zeros_like(Z2); dZ2[tr_i] = (P[tr_i] - Y1) / len(tr_i)       # softmax cross-entropy on labelled nodes
        G2 = A_hat @ dZ2                                                    # A_hat is symmetric
        gW2 = Hd.T @ G2 + wd * W2
        dH = (G2 @ W2.T) * mask * (Z1 > 0)
        gW1 = AX.T @ dH + wd * W1
        for i, (W, g) in enumerate(zip(params, [gW1, gW2])):              # Adam (Lecture 2)
            m[i] = 0.9 * m[i] + 0.1 * g; v[i] = 0.999 * v[i] + 0.001 * g * g
            W -= lr * (m[i] / (1 - 0.9**t)) / (np.sqrt(v[i] / (1 - 0.999**t)) + 1e-8)
        out = A_hat @ (np.maximum(AX @ W1, 0) @ W2)                          # evaluation: no dropout
        va = np.mean(out[va_i].argmax(1) == yc[va_i])
        if va > best[0]:
            best = (va, out.argmax(1))
    return best

definition 9.6 — message passing

A message-passing neural network (Gilmer et al., 2017) updates each node's hidden state \(h_v\) in rounds:

$$m_v^{(t)}=\bigoplus_{u\in N(v)}\psi^{(t)}\big(h_v^{(t)},h_u^{(t)},e_{uv}\big),\qquad h_v^{(t+1)}=\phi^{(t)}\big(h_v^{(t)},m_v^{(t)}\big),$$

where \(\bigoplus\) is a permutation-invariant aggregation (sum, mean or max), \(e_{uv}\) are edge features, and \(\psi,\phi\) are learned networks.

GCNs, GraphSAGE, graph attention networks (whose aggregation weights are learned attention scores, Lecture 12) and GIN are all special cases, and all are equivariant by the argument of Proposition 9.1.

Stacking many message-passing layers is not straightforward. Repeated averaging is diffusion, and like heat it smooths node states toward a constant (Exercise 9): the problem of oversmoothing, which is why most GNNs use two to four layers, plus residual connections (Lecture 11) when they go deeper.

8 — Graph-level tasks and the limits of message passing

To predict a property of a whole graph (is this molecule mutagenic?), run message passing and then pool the node states into one vector with an invariant readout (sum, mean or max over nodes, Proposition 9.1), and feed it to a classifier. Sum readouts can count (how many nitro groups?), which mean and max cannot (Exercise 7). What can message passing distinguish at all? The classic answer comes from graph isomorphism testing.

definition 9.7 — the Weisfeiler–Leman test

The (one-dimensional) Weisfeiler–Leman (WL) test starts by colouring every node by its label (or all the same colour) and repeatedly recolours each node by the pair (its colour, the multiset of its neighbours' colours), giving each distinct pair a new colour.

Two graphs whose colour histograms differ at some round are certainly not isomorphic; if the histograms never differ, the test cannot tell them apart.

theorem 9.8 — message passing is at most as powerful as Weisfeiler–Leman

If two graphs receive the same WL colour histogram after \(t\) rounds, then every \(t\)-layer message-passing GNN (Definition 9.6, any aggregation, any readout of the node multiset) maps them to the same embedding.

Conversely, a GNN whose aggregation and update are injective on multisets, such as the graph isomorphism network (GIN), with sum aggregation followed by a multilayer perceptron, distinguishes every pair of graphs that WL distinguishes (Xu et al., 2019; Morris et al., 2019).

By induction on rounds: if two nodes have the same WL colour after round \(t\), they have the same GNN state after layer \(t\). Initially both are functions of the node label. If two nodes share a WL colour at round \(t+1\), they had the same colour and the same multiset of neighbour colours at round \(t\), hence (induction) the same state and the same multiset of neighbour states, hence the same message and the same updated state. Equal colour histograms then give equal multisets of node states and equal readouts.

For the converse, sums of a suitable feature map are injective on bounded multisets, and an MLP can approximate such a map. \(\square\)

9 — Summary, exercises, projects, reading

summary
  • A graph is encoded by its adjacency matrix and node features; relabelling nodes conjugates \(A\) by a permutation, so graph models must be invariant (graph-level) or equivariant (node-level). Layers \(\sigma(\hat AXW)\) are equivariant and sum readouts invariant (Proposition 9.1).
  • Data become graphs via \(\varepsilon\)-, kNN- or Gaussian-weighted similarity.
  • The Laplacian \(L=D-W\) has \(f^\top Lf=\sum_{\text{edges}}W_{ij}(f_i-f_j)^2\) (Proposition 9.2); its zero eigenvalues count components (9.3); its eigenvectors are a graph's Fourier modes, and the Fiedler vector splits it in two.
  • The harmonic solution minimises \(f^\top Lf\) with labels fixed; it exists, is unique and satisfies a maximum principle (Theorem 9.4), and label propagation converges to it (9.5). With very few labels it can collapse onto one class; class mass normalisation helps.
  • Random walks spend time in proportion to degree (9.6); PageRank adds teleportation and is unique by Perron–Frobenius (9.7). Heat diffusion \(e^{-tL}\) damps rough modes and gives the diffusion kernel.
  • GCNs and message-passing networks learn what to propagate; on Cora a two-layer GCN beats links alone and words alone. Message passing is bounded by the Weisfeiler–Leman test (Theorem 9.8) and cannot count triangles.

  2. Show that every eigenvalue of \(L\) is at most \(2d_{\max}\), where \(d_{\max}\) is the largest (weighted) degree.

  4. On a path of \(n\) nodes with node 1 labelled 0 and node \(n\) labelled 1, show that the harmonic solution is \(f_i=(i-1)/(n-1)\).

    The averaging condition \(f_i=\frac12(f_{i-1}+f_{i+1})\) says the second difference is zero, so \(f\) is linear in \(i\); the boundary values fix it. By Theorem 9.4 this is the only solution.

  5. Explain why class mass normalisation does not change predictions when the classes' harmonic columns already have equal total mass, and why it helps when one labelled point sits in a dense hub.

    Dividing every column by the same constant does not change which column is largest. When the class-1 label sits in a hub, its column has large values everywhere (walks from everywhere hit it early), so its mass is inflated; dividing by the mass removes that global advantage and leaves the local comparison of each node's relative closeness to each label.

  6. Show that the posterior mean of \(f_u\) under the GMRF prior \(p(f)\propto\exp(-\frac12f^\top(L+\varepsilon I)f)\), given \(f_\ell\), is \(-(L_{uu}+\varepsilon I)^{-1}L_{u\ell}f_\ell\), and that it tends to the harmonic solution as \(\varepsilon\to0\).

    The log density in \(f_u\) is \(-\frac12f_u^\top(L_{uu}+\varepsilon I)f_u-f_u^\top L_{u\ell}f_\ell+\text{const}\), a Gaussian with precision \(L_{uu}+\varepsilon I\) and mean \(-(L_{uu}+\varepsilon I)^{-1}L_{u\ell}f_\ell\). Since \(L_{u\ell}=-W_{u\ell}\) and \(L_{uu}\) is invertible (Theorem 9.4), the limit is \(L_{uu}^{-1}W_{u\ell}f_\ell\), the harmonic solution.

  7. Show that a mean readout cannot distinguish a triangle from a hexagon when all nodes have the same features (whatever the GNN), but a sum readout can. Can a sum readout distinguish the hexagon from two triangles?

    Every node in both graphs has degree 2 and the same features, so every node ends with the same state \(h\) in both. The mean readout gives \(h\) for both; the sum gives \(3h\) against \(6h\), which differ whenever \(h\ne0\). The hexagon and two triangles both have six nodes in state \(h\), so even the sum gives \(6h\) for both (Theorem 9.8).

  8. Show that 1-WL assigns the same colour to every node of any \(d\)-regular graph with uniform initial colours, and give two non-isomorphic 3-regular graphs on 8 nodes.

    Initially all colours are equal; a node's new colour depends on its colour and the multiset of \(d\) equal neighbour colours, the same for all nodes. By induction this persists. Examples: the cube graph \(Q_3\) (connected, no triangles) and two disjoint copies of \(K_4\) (disconnected, eight triangles).

  9. Show that \(e^{-tL}\mathbf 1=\mathbf 1\), so total heat \(\mathbf 1^\top h(t)\) is conserved, and that on a connected graph \(e^{-tL}\to\frac1n\mathbf 1\mathbf 1^\top\) as \(t\to\infty\). What does this imply for very deep GCNs?

    \(L\mathbf 1=0\) gives \(e^{-tL}\mathbf 1=\mathbf 1\), and since \(L\) is symmetric, \(\mathbf 1^\top e^{-tL}=\mathbf 1^\top\). In the eigen-expansion every term with \(\lambda_k>0\) decays, leaving the projection onto \(u_1=\mathbf 1/\sqrt n\). Repeated neighbourhood averaging behaves the same way: node states converge to a common value and lose the information that distinguishes nodes, which is oversmoothing.

  10. Show that the number of triangles in a simple graph is \(\operatorname{tr}(A^3)/6\), and that it is a permutation-invariant function.

    \((A^3)_{ii}\) counts closed walks of length 3 from \(i\), which are triangles through \(i\) traversed in either direction; each triangle is counted from each of its 3 vertices in 2 directions. Invariance: \(\operatorname{tr}((PAP^\top)^3)=\operatorname{tr}(PA^3P^\top)=\operatorname{tr}(A^3)\).

1. Street trees from a few labels

question
How well can tree health or species be predicted across a borough from a small fraction of surveyed trees, using where trees are and what is around them?
data
The 2015 NYC Street Tree Census (683,788 trees, NYC Open Data), with species, health, trunk diameter, sidewalk damage and location.
method
Spatial kNN graphs; label propagation with and without class mass normalisation; a GCN using tree attributes as features; accuracy as the labelled fraction falls from 10% to 0.1%, with spatially blocked test sets to avoid leakage between neighbours.
outcome
A practical estimate of how many trees a city must survey to map health citywide, and a clean comparison of graph methods on real spatial data.

2. The subway as a graph

question
Which station characteristics (ridership recovery after 2020, accessibility, crowding) can be predicted from a station's position in the network, and which need local features?
data
MTA station and line data and hourly ridership (data.ny.gov), with census tract demographics around each station.
method
Build the station graph (and a transfer-weighted version); compare label propagation, PageRank and Laplacian-eigenvector features, logistic or linear models on local features, and a GCN that combines them.
outcome
A measure of how much "network position" explains about stations, with maps that transit planners can read.

3. Do graph neural networks beat fingerprints on small molecules?

question
Under a fair protocol, do trained GNNs outperform atom-count, WL and random-feature baselines on small molecular benchmarks?
data
TUDataset collections (MUTAG, PTC, NCI1, PROTEINS) and MoleculeNet tasks.
method
Repeat the protocol of Errica et al. (2020): nested cross-validation, equal tuning budgets, several seeds; include random-weight GNNs, WL kernels and GIN; report confidence intervals and paired tests.
outcome
An updated, honest benchmark; results that change published rankings are publishable and useful to the field.
