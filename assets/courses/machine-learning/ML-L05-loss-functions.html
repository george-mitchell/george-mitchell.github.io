Every method so far minimised some loss: squared error for regression, cross-entropy for logistic regression, 0–1 loss when counting mistakes. This lecture asks what those choices mean. A loss is not a neutral measure of error; it decides which feature of the data the model learns to predict. Squared loss learns the mean, absolute loss the median, the quantile loss a percentile. For probabilities, some losses reward honest forecasts and others reward exaggeration. Then we return to the U-shaped test error of Lecture 1 and take it apart exactly: noise, bias and variance, computed in closed form for ridge regression and nearest neighbours.

By the end of this lecture you should be able to derive the optimal decision from a predictive distribution and a loss, including cost-sensitive thresholds; say which statistical functional each regression loss estimates and prove it; read common losses as negative log-likelihoods and relate cross-entropy to KL divergence; explain why classifiers are trained on surrogate losses and which surrogates recover the Bayes classifier; define proper scoring rules and prove that the Brier and log scores are strictly proper; derive the bias–variance decomposition and compute bias and variance exactly for ridge and kNN; and separate approximation, estimation and optimisation error.

1 — What a loss encodes

A prediction is usually an input to a decision: order stock, send a patient for a biopsy, hold a flight. The loss should be the cost of that decision when the truth is revealed. Suppose we have a predictive distribution for \(Y\) given the features \(x\), from a model or a posterior. Which single action should we take?

theorem 5.1 — the Bayes decision rule

Given a loss \(\ell(a,y)\) for taking action \(a\) when the outcome is \(y\), the action minimising expected loss at \(x\) is

$$a^*(x)=\operatorname*{arg\,min}_a\ \mathbb E\big[\ell(a,Y)\mid X=x\big],$$

and using \(a^*(x)\) at every \(x\) minimises the overall risk \(\mathbb E[\ell(a(X),Y)]\).

In particular, for a binary outcome with \(\eta(x)=P(Y=1\mid x)\), a false negative costing \(c_{\text{FN}}\) and a false positive costing \(c_{\text{FP}}\), the optimal rule predicts 1 exactly when

$$\eta(x)\ \ge\ \frac{c_{\text{FP}}}{c_{\text{FP}}+c_{\text{FN}}}.$$

The risk of any rule \(a(\cdot)\) is

$$\mathbb E\big[\mathbb E[\ell(a(X),Y)\mid X]\big],$$

an average over \(x\) of the conditional expected loss. Minimising the inner expectation separately at each \(x\) minimises the average (the same argument as Theorem 1.3, which is the case of 0–1 loss).

For the binary case, predicting 1 costs \(c_{\text{FP}}(1-\eta)\) in expectation and predicting 0 costs \(c_{\text{FN}}\,\eta\). Predict 1 when

$$c_{\text{FN}}\,\eta\ \ge\ c_{\text{FP}}(1-\eta),$$

which rearranges to the threshold. With equal costs it is \(\tfrac12\). \(\square\)

If missing a cancer is ten times as costly as a false alarm, the threshold drops from ½ to \(1/11\approx0.09\). The theorem needs calibrated probabilities: the threshold is only right if \(\eta(x)\) means what it says. Here is the test set of Lecture 4's breast-cancer model.

Choosing a threshold from costs

171 test patients from Lecture 4, scored by penalised logistic regression. Set how much worse a missed cancer is than a false alarm. The curve is the total cost of each threshold on these patients; the moss line is the Bayes threshold \(1/(1+\text{ratio})\), the amber line your choice.

At a ratio of 20, the Bayes threshold 0.048 misses one cancer and raises 20 false alarms, a cost of 40 units; hindsight on these same patients finds a threshold costing only 24. That gap is the familiar optimism of choosing with the data you report on (Lecture 1, Section 4): the hindsight threshold is tuned to 64 particular malignant patients. The Bayes threshold was fixed before looking, and would be optimal if the probabilities were perfectly calibrated. They are not quite: only 17 of the 171 patients have probabilities between 0.2 and 0.8, so the middle of the probability scale is barely tested, and at a cost ratio of 5 the default threshold of ½ happens to beat the Bayes threshold on these particular patients. With a test set this small, differences of a few patients are noise (Lecture 1, Exercise 9).

bayes — decision theory turns a posterior into an action

Bayesian inference ends with a posterior distribution; Bayesian decision theory says what to do with it. Theorem 5.1 applies with the posterior (predictive) distribution in place of the true conditional law. Under squared loss the optimal point estimate is the posterior mean; under absolute loss, the posterior median; under 0–1 loss for a discrete parameter, the posterior mode, the MAP estimate of Lecture 3. "Which estimate should I report?" is therefore not a question about the posterior at all, but about the loss. The classic reference is Berger (1985).

2 — Regression losses and what they estimate

definition 5.1 — four regression losses

With residual \(u=y-\hat y\):

  • squared: \(u^2\);
  • absolute: \(|u|\);
  • pinball (quantile) at level \(\tau\in(0,1)\): $$\rho_\tau(u)=\max(\tau u,(\tau-1)u),$$ which charges \(\tau\) per unit of under-prediction and \(1-\tau\) per unit of over-prediction;
  • Huber with threshold \(\delta\): \(\tfrac12u^2\) for \(|u|\le\delta\), and \(\delta(|u|-\tfrac\delta2)\) beyond: squared for small residuals, absolute for large ones.
theorem 5.2 — each loss estimates a different summary

For a random variable \(Y\) with finite mean, the constant \(c\) minimising \(\mathbb E[\ell(Y-c)]\) is:

  • the mean for squared loss;
  • any median for absolute loss;
  • any \(\tau\)-quantile (a \(c\) with \(P(Y<c)\le\tau\le P(Y\le c)\)) for the pinball loss \(\rho_\tau\).

Applied conditionally on \(X=x\), the best predictor under each loss is the conditional mean, median or quantile.

Squared.

$$\mathbb E[(Y-c)^2]=\mathrm{Var}(Y)+(\mathbb E Y-c)^2,$$

minimised at \(c=\mathbb EY\).

Pinball (absolute loss is twice \(\rho_{1/2}\)). The function \(g(c)=\mathbb E[\rho_\tau(Y-c)]\) is convex. Moving \(c\) up by a small \(h>0\) changes the loss of an outcome \(y>c\) by \(-\tau h\) (we under-predict it by less) and of an outcome \(y\le c\) by \(+(1-\tau)h\). So the right derivative is

$$g'_+(c)=-\tau P(Y>c)+(1-\tau)P(Y\le c)=P(Y\le c)-\tau,$$

and similarly the left derivative is \(P(Y<c)-\tau\).

A convex function is minimised where the left derivative is \(\le0\) and the right derivative is \(\ge0\):

$$P(Y<c)\le\tau\le P(Y\le c),$$

the definition of a \(\tau\)-quantile. \(\square\)

When the distribution is skewed, these summaries differ, and the difference is not a technicality.

data — every flight out of New York in 2013

All 336,776 flights that departed from JFK, LaGuardia and Newark in 2013, with scheduled and actual times, carrier, origin, destination and distance, from the US Bureau of Transportation Statistics, packaged by Hadley Wickham as the R package nycflights13 (CC0); we use the copy in the Rdatasets collection. 327,346 flights have an arrival delay (the rest were cancelled or diverted). Arrival delay is in minutes; negative means early.

code — the best single prediction under four losses 
import numpy as np
import pandas as pd
from scipy.optimize import minimize_scalar

fl = pd.read_csv('data/flights.csv', usecols=['month', 'hour', 'origin', 'carrier', 'distance', 'arr_delay'])
fl = fl.dropna(subset=['arr_delay'])                # cancelled and diverted flights have no arrival delay
d = fl['arr_delay'].to_numpy()                      # minutes late on arrival (negative = early)
print(f'{len(d)} flights: mean {d.mean():.2f}, median {np.median(d):.0f}, 90th percentile {np.quantile(d, 0.9):.0f} minutes')

def pinball(u, tau):                                # the quantile loss
    return np.maximum(tau * u, (tau - 1) * u)

def huber(u, delta):
    return np.where(np.abs(u) <= delta, 0.5 * u ** 2, delta * (np.abs(u) - delta / 2))

losses = {'squared': lambda c: np.mean((d - c) ** 2),
          'absolute': lambda c: np.mean(np.abs(d - c)),
          'pinball, tau = 0.9': lambda c: np.mean(pinball(d - c, 0.9)),
          'Huber, delta = 15': lambda c: np.mean(huber(d - c, 15))}
for name, risk in losses.items():
    c = minimize_scalar(risk, bounds=(-60, 120), method='bounded', options={'xatol': 1e-4}).x
    print(f'{name:19s}: best constant prediction {c:6.2f} minutes')

The mean delay is about 7 minutes late, the median 5 minutes early. Both are correct answers to different questions. Most flights arrive a little early; a long tail of badly delayed flights (the worst more than 21 hours) pulls the mean up. An airline promising "typical" arrival times should quote the median; one budgeting total passenger delay needs the mean; a traveller with a tight connection cares about the 90th percentile, 52 minutes. Huber loss lands near the median: it treats the tail as outliers to be resisted.

history — absolute or squared?

The absolute-error criterion is older than least squares. Roger Boscovich fitted a line to measurements of the Earth's shape in 1757 by minimising the sum of absolute deviations, and Pierre-Simon Laplace argued for absolute error in 1774. Squared error won in the nineteenth century largely for convenience: it has a closed-form minimiser, and Gauss tied it to the normal distribution (Lecture 3). The other losses returned when computers made optimisation cheap. Peter Huber's 1964 paper founded robust statistics, and Roger Koenker and Gilbert Bassett's 1978 quantile regression became a standard tool in economics for studying inequality, where what happens in the tails matters as much as the average.

3 — Losses from likelihoods

Lecture 3 showed that squared loss is the negative log-likelihood of Gaussian noise, and Lecture 4 that cross-entropy is the negative log-likelihood of a Bernoulli label. This is a general recipe: choose a noise distribution suited to the data, and its negative log-likelihood is the loss.

proposition 5.3 — a dictionary of likelihoods and losses

Up to terms that do not depend on the prediction \(\mu\):

model for \(Y\)negative log-likelihoodname
\(\mathcal N(\mu,\sigma^2)\)\((y-\mu)^2/2\sigma^2\)squared loss
Laplace\((\mu,b)\)\(|y-\mu|/b\)absolute loss
Bernoulli\((\mu)\)\(-y\log\mu-(1-y)\log(1-\mu)\)cross-entropy (log loss)
Poisson\((\mu)\)\(\mu-y\log\mu\)Poisson deviance

Take minus the log of each density and drop terms free of \(\mu\).

Gaussian: \(\tfrac12\log(2\pi\sigma^2)+(y-\mu)^2/2\sigma^2\).

Laplace, density \(\frac1{2b}e^{-|y-\mu|/b}\): \(\log2b+|y-\mu|/b\).

Bernoulli, mass \(\mu^y(1-\mu)^{1-y}\): immediate.

Poisson, mass \(e^{-\mu}\mu^y/y!\): \(\mu-y\log\mu+\log y!\).

The deviance rescales by 2 and subtracts the same expression at \(\mu=y\), so that a perfect prediction scores 0. \(\square\)

The cross-entropy has an information-theoretic reading that runs through the rest of the course.

definition 5.2 — entropy, cross-entropy, KL divergence

For distributions \(p\) and \(q\) on a finite set, the entropy is

$$H(p)=-\sum_kp_k\log p_k,$$

the cross-entropy is

$$H(p,q)=-\sum_kp_k\log q_k,$$

and the Kullback–Leibler divergence is

$$\mathrm{KL}(p\,\|\,q)=\sum_kp_k\log\frac{p_k}{q_k}=H(p,q)-H(p).$$
proposition 5.4 — Gibbs' inequality

\(\mathrm{KL}(p\,\|\,q)\ge0\), with equality if and only if \(q=p\).

Hence the cross-entropy \(H(p,q)\) is minimised over \(q\) exactly at \(q=p\).

Sum over \(k\) with \(p_k>0\). Since \(\log\) is concave, Jensen's inequality gives

$$-\mathrm{KL}(p\|q)=\sum_kp_k\log\frac{q_k}{p_k}\le\log\sum_kp_k\frac{q_k}{p_k}=\log\sum_{k:p_k>0}q_k\le\log1=0.$$

Equality in Jensen requires \(q_k/p_k\) to be constant on the support of \(p\), and equality in the last step requires \(q\) to put no mass elsewhere, together forcing \(q=p\). \(\square\)

So minimising average cross-entropy over training data is minimising the KL divergence from the empirical label distribution to the model's: a model trained with log loss is pushed to match the true probabilities, not just the most likely class. Not all data are Gaussian, and counts are the commonest exception.

data — hourly bike rentals in Washington, D.C.

Number of Capital Bikeshare rentals in each hour of 2011 (8,645 hours), with month, hour of day, working day, weather situation, temperature, humidity and wind speed. From Fanaee-T & Gama (2014), as distributed with the ISLP Python package (James et al., 2023). Counts range from 1 to 651 per hour.

code — counts: least squares versus Poisson regression, and quantile bands 
from sklearn.linear_model import PoissonRegressor

bk = pd.read_csv('data/Bikeshare.csv')              # hourly rentals, Capital Bikeshare, 2011
Xb = pd.get_dummies(bk[['mnth', 'hr', 'weathersit']].astype(str), drop_first=True).astype(float)
Xb[['temp', 'hum', 'windspeed', 'workingday']] = bk[['temp', 'hum', 'windspeed', 'workingday']]
yb = bk['bikers'].to_numpy()
j = np.random.default_rng(1).permutation(len(bk)); a, b = j[:6000], j[6000:]
Xa, Xz = Xb.to_numpy()[a], Xb.to_numpy()[b]

ols = LinearRegression().fit(Xa, yb[a]).predict(Xz)
poi = PoissonRegressor(alpha=1e-6, max_iter=5000).fit(Xa, yb[a]).predict(Xz)
def deviance(y, mu):                                # mean Poisson deviance: 2 * (log-lik of perfect fit - log-lik of model)
    mu = np.maximum(mu, 1e-9)
    return 2 * np.mean(np.where(y > 0, y * np.log(y / mu), 0) - (y - mu))
for name, p in [('least squares', ols), ('Poisson', poi)]:

          f'   Poisson deviance {deviance(yb[b], p):6.2f}')

      f' below the band {np.mean(yb[b] < q10):.3f}, above {np.mean(yb[b] > q90):.3f}')

Least squares predicts a negative number of riders for 259 of the 2,645 test hours. Poisson regression models the log of the expected count, so its predictions are always positive; it also captures that busy hours vary more than quiet ones, and it has lower test MSE even though it was not trained on squared error. Two quantile regressions give an 80% prediction band that covers 78.6% of test hours, with misses split almost evenly above and below: an interval forecast, which no mean model provides.

spot check 5.3

Why can a least-squares model predict negative bike counts, while Poisson regression cannot?

  • Least squares models the mean as a linear function, which can go below zero; Poisson regression models the log of the mean as linear, so the mean itself is an exponential and always positive.
  • Poisson regression uses better features.
  • Least squares is trained on negative counts.
  • Poisson regression is fitted by a special algorithm that forbids negatives.
What is \(\mu\) as a function of the linear score in each model?
Poisson regression sets \(\mu=e^{w^\top x}\), the log link; logistic regression sets \(\mu=\sigma(w^\top x)\), the logit link. Each maps a linear score into the range the outcome lives in. Models built this way are generalised linear models, and their gradients all have the form \(X^\top(\mu-y)\) (Exercise 2).

4 — Classification: the 0–1 loss and its surrogates

For classification the loss we care about is usually the 0–1 loss. With labels \(y=\pm1\) and score \(s=f(x)\), write the margin \(m=ys\): positive when the prediction is correct, large when it is correct and confident. The 0–1 loss is \(\mathbf 1[m\le0]\). It is a step function: flat almost everywhere, so its gradient is zero and gradient descent cannot move; and minimising it over linear classifiers is NP-hard in general. So we train on a convex surrogate \(\varphi(m)\) that upper-bounds the step and has useful gradients.

definition 5.3 — margin losses
  • Hinge \(\max(0,1-m)\) (support vector machines, Lecture 7);
  • logistic \(\log_2(1+e^{-m})\) (logistic regression, rescaled to equal 1 at \(m=0\));
  • exponential \(e^{-m}\) (AdaBoost, Lecture 8);
  • squared \((1-m)^2\);
  • perceptron \(\max(0,-m)\) (Lecture 4).

Which surrogates are safe? Fix \(x\) with \(\eta=P(Y=1\mid x)\). The best score at \(x\) minimises the conditional \(\varphi\)-risk \(C_\eta(s)=\eta\,\varphi(s)+(1-\eta)\,\varphi(-s)\). A surrogate is classification-calibrated if, for every \(\eta\ne\tfrac12\), this minimiser has the same sign as \(2\eta-1\): it points the same way as the Bayes classifier.

Surrogate losses and what they learn

Left: losses against the margin \(m=ys\); toggle them on and off. Right: for the selected loss and a point with \(P(Y=1\mid x)=\eta\), the conditional risk \(C_\eta(s)\) and its minimiser \(s^*\) (rose).

theorem 5.5 — classification-calibration of convex losses (Bartlett, Jordan & McAuliffe, 2006)

A convex loss \(\varphi\) is classification-calibrated if and only if it is differentiable at 0 with \(\varphi'(0)<0\).

For such \(\varphi\) there is an increasing function \(\psi\) with \(\psi(0)=0\) such that, for every classifier \(f\),

$$\psi\big(R_{0\text{-}1}(f)-R^*\big)\ \le\ R_\varphi(f)-R_\varphi^*,$$

so driving the surrogate risk to its minimum drives the 0–1 risk to the Bayes risk.

If. Take \(\eta>\tfrac12\). Then

$$C_\eta'(0)=\eta\varphi'(0)-(1-\eta)\varphi'(0)=(2\eta-1)\varphi'(0)<0,$$

so \(C_\eta\) is strictly decreasing at 0. A convex function decreasing at 0 takes its values on \(s\le0\) no lower than \(C_\eta(0)\), which is strictly above its infimum; so every near-minimiser has \(s>0\), the sign of the Bayes classifier. The case \(\eta<\tfrac12\) is symmetric.

Only if. If \(\varphi\) is not differentiable at 0, or \(\varphi'(0)\ge0\), one can find \(\eta\ne\tfrac12\) for which \(s=0\) (or a wrong-signed \(s\)) minimises \(C_\eta\); the perceptron loss is the standard example, with \(s^*=0\) for every \(\eta\).

The construction of \(\psi\) is in the paper (Bartlett et al., 2006, Section 2). \(\square\)

Hinge, logistic, exponential and squared losses all qualify; the perceptron loss does not (try it in the widget: \(s^*=0\) whatever \(\eta\), so it never learns which way to lean). The surrogates also differ in what \(s^*\) means. For logistic loss \(s^*=\log\frac{\eta}{1-\eta}\), the log-odds, so the score can be turned back into a probability; exponential loss gives half the log-odds; squared loss gives \(2\eta-1\). The hinge loss gives only \(s^*=\operatorname{sign}(2\eta-1)\): it learns the Bayes classifier but throws away the probability, which is why support vector machines do not output probabilities without extra calibration.

spot check 5.4

Which of these losses is not classification-calibrated?

  • the perceptron loss \(\max(0,-m)\)
  • the hinge loss \(\max(0,1-m)\)
  • the logistic loss
  • the exponential loss \(e^{-m}\)
Look at the slope at \(m=0\).
The perceptron loss has a kink at 0, with right-hand slope 0. For any \(\eta\), \(C_\eta(s)\) is minimised at \(s=0\), which says nothing about which class is more likely. The perceptron still works on separable data because it never reaches the population minimum: it stops when the training mistakes run out.

5 — Scoring probabilities: proper scoring rules

When the output is a probability, the loss should reward telling the truth. Suppose a forecaster believes the chance of rain is \(p\). A good scoring rule should make reporting \(p\) the best strategy, in expectation, for every \(p\).

definition 5.4 — proper scoring rule

A scoring rule \(S(q,y)\) charges a forecaster who reports probability \(q\) for an event that then happens (\(y=1\)) or not (\(y=0\)).

It is proper if, for every true probability \(p\), the expected charge

$$\mathbb E_{Y\sim\text{Bern}(p)}S(q,Y)$$

is minimised at \(q=p\), and strictly proper if \(q=p\) is the unique minimiser.

The Brier score is

$$S(q,y)=(q-y)^2;$$

the log score is

$$S(q,y)=-y\log q-(1-y)\log(1-q).$$
theorem 5.6 — the Brier and log scores are strictly proper

If \(Y\sim\mathrm{Bern}(p)\), then for the Brier score and the log score respectively

$$\mathbb E\,S(q,Y)=(q-p)^2+p(1-p),\qquad\mathbb E\,S(q,Y)=H(p)+\mathrm{KL}\big(\mathrm{Bern}(p)\,\|\,\mathrm{Bern}(q)\big).$$

Both are uniquely minimised at \(q=p\).

Brier:

$$\mathbb E(q-Y)^2=p(q-1)^2+(1-p)q^2=q^2-2pq+p=(q-p)^2+p(1-p).$$

Log:

$$\mathbb E\,S=-p\log q-(1-p)\log(1-q)$$

is the cross-entropy of the two Bernoulli distributions, which equals entropy plus KL divergence (Definition 5.2) and is uniquely minimised at \(q=p\) by Gibbs' inequality. \(\square\)

Not every natural-looking score is proper. The absolute score \(|q-y|\) has expected value \(p(1-q)+(1-p)q\), linear in \(q\), so it is minimised by reporting 0 or 1: it rewards exaggeration (Exercise 6). Proper scores combine two virtues. Calibration: among all occasions forecast at 70%, the event happens about 70% of the time. Sharpness (or resolution): forecasts are far from the base rate, so they actually distinguish occasions. Always forecasting the base rate is perfectly calibrated and useless.

code — scoring 63,157 probability forecasts 
nba = pd.read_csv('data/nbaallelo.csv')
games = nba[nba['_iscopy'] == 0]                    # one row per game (the other row is the opponent's view)

6 — Bias and variance, precisely

Lecture 1 decomposed squared error at a single point. Averaging over test points gives the version used in practice. Fix a learning algorithm; let \(\hat f_S\) be its fit on a random training set \(S\), and \(\bar f(x)=\mathbb E_S\hat f_S(x)\) its average fit.

theorem 5.7 — bias–variance decomposition

If \(Y=f(X)+\varepsilon\) with \(\mathbb E[\varepsilon\mid X]=0\) and \(\mathrm{Var}(\varepsilon\mid X)=\sigma^2\), and the test point \((X,Y)\) is independent of \(S\), then

$$\mathbb E_{S,X,Y}\big[(Y-\hat f_S(X))^2\big]=\sigma^2+\underbrace{\mathbb E_X\big[(\bar f(X)-f(X))^2\big]}_{\text{bias}^2}+\underbrace{\mathbb E_X\big[\mathrm{Var}_S\,\hat f_S(X)\big]}_{\text{variance}}.$$

Condition on \(X=x\). Proposition 1.2 gives

$$\mathbb E\big[(Y-\hat f_S(x))^2\mid X=x\big]=\sigma^2+(\bar f(x)-f(x))^2+\mathrm{Var}_S\hat f_S(x),$$

using the independence of \(S\) and the test noise. Averaging over \(X\) gives the result. \(\square\)

To see the trade-off, simulate. The truth is \(f(x)=\sin2\pi x\) on \([0,1]\), each training set has 30 points with noise standard deviation 0.3, and the learner fits a polynomial of degree \(d\) by least squares.

For ridge regression the two terms can be written down exactly, using the singular value decomposition of Lecture 2.

theorem 5.8 — bias and variance of ridge regression

Let the design \(X\) (\(n\times p\)) be fixed, with SVD

$$X=\sum_i\sigma_iu_iv_i^\top,$$

and \(y=X\beta+\varepsilon\) with \(\mathrm{Cov}(\varepsilon)=\sigma^2I\). The ridge fit \(\hat y_\lambda=X\hat\beta_\lambda\) has, averaged over the \(n\) training inputs,

$$\text{bias}^2(\lambda)=\frac1n\sum_i\Big(\frac{\lambda}{\sigma_i^2+\lambda}\Big)^2\big(u_i^\top X\beta\big)^2,\qquad\text{variance}(\lambda)=\frac{\sigma^2}{n}\sum_i\Big(\frac{\sigma_i^2}{\sigma_i^2+\lambda}\Big)^2.$$

Bias² increases and variance decreases as \(\lambda\) grows. At \(\lambda=0\) the variance is \(\sigma^2p/n\).

From Proposition 3.5,

$$\hat y_\lambda=\sum_is_i\,u_iu_i^\top y$$

with shrinkage factors \(s_i=\sigma_i^2/(\sigma_i^2+\lambda)\). Its mean is \(\sum_is_iu_iu_i^\top X\beta\); since \(X\beta\) lies in the span of the \(u_i\), \(X\beta=\sum_iu_iu_i^\top X\beta\), so the mean error is

$$-\sum_i(1-s_i)u_iu_i^\top X\beta,$$

with squared length \(\sum_i(1-s_i)^2(u_i^\top X\beta)^2\), and \(1-s_i=\lambda/(\sigma_i^2+\lambda)\).

The fluctuation \(\sum_is_iu_iu_i^\top\varepsilon\) has covariance

$$\sigma^2\sum_is_i^2u_iu_i^\top,$$

whose trace is \(\sigma^2\sum_is_i^2\). Divide both by \(n\). Each \(1-s_i\) increases and each \(s_i\) decreases in \(\lambda\). \(\square\)

code — ridge bias and variance from the formula, checked by simulation 
rng = np.random.default_rng(1)
n, p_, sig = 50, 10, 3.0

proposition 5.9 — bias and variance of kNN regression (fixed design)

With fixed training inputs \(x_1,\dots,x_n\) and \(y_i=f(x_i)+\varepsilon_i\), the kNN prediction at \(x_0\) has

$$\text{bias}=\frac1k\sum_{i\in N_k(x_0)}f(x_i)-f(x_0),\qquad\text{variance}=\frac{\sigma^2}{k}.$$

With the inputs fixed, the neighbour set \(N_k(x_0)\) is fixed. The prediction

$$\frac1k\sum_{i\in N_k}(f(x_i)+\varepsilon_i)$$

has mean \(\frac1k\sum f(x_i)\) and, the \(\varepsilon_i\) being independent with variance \(\sigma^2\), variance \(\sigma^2/k\). \(\square\)

This is the dartboard of Lecture 1 made exact: variance falls like \(1/k\), while bias grows as the neighbourhood reaches points where \(f\) differs.

bias and variance for 0–1 loss

The clean additive decomposition is special to squared loss. For classification with 0–1 loss, several competing definitions exist, and none adds up neatly. Worse, variance can help: at a point where the average classifier is on the wrong side of the Bayes boundary, random fluctuations sometimes push it to the right side (Friedman, 1997; James, 2003). This is one reason classifiers can tolerate a lot of variance in their scores, and one reason ensembles that average many unstable classifiers (Lecture 8) work so well.

7 — Approximation, estimation, and choosing complexity

Bias and variance describe one algorithm averaged over training sets. A second decomposition, useful for reading theory papers, splits the excess risk of the predictor we actually get. Let \(R^*\) be the Bayes risk, \(h^*_{\mathcal H}\) the best predictor in the hypothesis class \(\mathcal H\), \(\hat h\) the exact empirical risk minimiser, and \(\tilde h\) what our optimiser actually returns. Then

$$R(\tilde h)-R^*=\underbrace{R(h^*_{\mathcal H})-R^*}_{\text{approximation}}+\underbrace{R(\hat h)-R(h^*_{\mathcal H})}_{\text{estimation}}+\underbrace{R(\tilde h)-R(\hat h)}_{\text{optimisation}}.$$

A richer class lowers the approximation error and raises the estimation error, the analogue of bias and variance; Lecture 6 bounds the estimation error in terms of the class's complexity. The optimisation term is a modern addition (Bottou & Bousquet, 2008): with huge datasets it can be better to optimise a rich model roughly than a poor one exactly.

In practice we choose complexity by cross-validation. A refinement guards against noise in the CV curve: the one-standard-error rule picks the simplest model whose CV error is within one standard error of the minimum.

code — the one-standard-error rule 
from sklearn.model_selection import KFold

rng = np.random.default_rng(7)
x = rng.uniform(0, 1, 40); y = f(x) + rng.normal(0, sigma, 40)
rows = []
for deg in range(1, 13):
    errs = []
    for a_, b_ in KFold(10, shuffle=True, random_state=0).split(x):
        c = legendre.legfit(2 * x[a_] - 1, y[a_], deg)
        errs.append(np.mean((legendre.legval(2 * x[b_] - 1, c) - y[b_]) ** 2))
    rows.append((deg, np.mean(errs), np.std(errs) / np.sqrt(len(errs))))   # CV estimate and its standard error
best = min(rows, key=lambda r: r[1])
one_se = min(r[0] for r in rows if r[1] <= best[1] + best[2])           # simplest model within one SE of the best
for deg, m, se in rows[:9]:
    print(f'degree {deg:2d}: CV MSE {m:.4f} (SE {se:.4f})')
print('lowest CV error at degree', best[0], '; one-standard-error rule picks degree', one_se)

spot check 5.8

Why choose the simplest model within one standard error of the best CV score, rather than the best score itself?

  • CV estimates are noisy; differences smaller than their standard error are not reliable, and among models that perform equally well, the simpler one has lower variance and is easier to interpret.
  • The simpler model always has lower test error.
  • The rule is guaranteed to choose the Bayes-optimal model.
  • It avoids using a validation set.
How large are the differences between degrees 3, 4 and 5 compared with their standard errors?
The rule is a guard against chasing noise, popularised by Breiman et al. (1984) for tree pruning and by Hastie, Tibshirani and Friedman for the lasso.

The U-curve is not the end of the story. Very flexible models such as large neural networks often have more parameters than training points, interpolate the training data exactly, and still generalise well. As complexity grows past the interpolation point, test error can fall again: double descent. Lecture 6 shows it happening and explains part of why.

causal — a loss for something you never observe

A treatment effect for one person, \(Y_i(1)-Y_i(0)\), is never observed, so there is no label to compute a loss against. Nie and Wager's (2021) R-learner manufactures one from the Frisch–Waugh–Lovell idea of Lecture 3. With \(\hat m(x)\) predicting the outcome and \(\hat e(x)\) predicting treatment (the propensity score of Lecture 4), estimate the effect function \(\tau(x)\) by minimising

$$\frac1n\sum_i\Big[\big(Y_i-\hat m(X_i)\big)-\big(T_i-\hat e(X_i)\big)\,\tau(X_i)\Big]^2+\text{penalty}(\tau).$$

This "R-loss" is minimised in expectation by the true effect function under unconfoundedness, so any method that minimises a loss (lasso, boosting, neural networks) can be turned into a heterogeneous-effect estimator. Designing the right loss is often the whole problem.

quantum — the loss decides whether a quantum circuit can learn at all

Variational quantum algorithms train a parameterised circuit by minimising a cost function estimated from measurements. Cerezo et al. (2021) showed that the choice of cost matters dramatically. A global cost, such as one minus the overlap between the output state and a target state, has gradients that vanish exponentially in the number of qubits even for shallow circuits: a "barren plateau" where the landscape is flat everywhere the optimiser can see. A local cost, averaging terms that each involve one or a few qubits, has gradients that shrink only polynomially for shallow circuits, and is trainable. Both costs have the same minimiser. The lesson matches this lecture's: two losses with the same target can behave completely differently during training.

big picture — choosing the loss is choosing the product

Retail and logistics forecasters train neural networks on the pinball loss at several quantiles at once, because stocking decisions depend on the 90th or 95th percentile of demand, not its mean; Amazon's MQ-RNN (Wen et al., 2017) is a published example. Object detectors use the focal loss (Lin et al., 2017) because nearly every candidate box in an image is background. Every large language model is pre-trained on next-token cross-entropy, a proper scoring rule, which is part of why their raw probabilities are reasonably calibrated before fine-tuning. Chat models are then tuned with a Bradley–Terry loss on human preference comparisons. And weather centres verify their probability forecasts with proper scores, a practice that began with Glenn Brier's 1950 paper.

9 — Summary, exercises, projects, reading

summary
  • The optimal action minimises expected loss under the predictive distribution (Theorem 5.1); with costs \(c_{\text{FN}},c_{\text{FP}}\), predict positive when $$\eta\ge c_{\text{FP}}/(c_{\text{FP}}+c_{\text{FN}}).$$
  • Squared, absolute and pinball losses estimate the mean, median and quantiles (Theorem 5.2); on skewed data these differ greatly. Huber loss is a robust compromise. Each model wins on its own loss.
  • Losses are negative log-likelihoods: Gaussian → squared, Laplace → absolute, Bernoulli → cross-entropy, Poisson → Poisson deviance (Proposition 5.3). Cross-entropy = entropy + KL divergence, and KL ≥ 0 (Proposition 5.4).
  • Classifiers train on convex surrogates of the 0–1 loss; a convex surrogate recovers the Bayes classifier iff \(\varphi'(0)<0\) (Theorem 5.5). Logistic loss also recovers probabilities; hinge loss does not.
  • Proper scoring rules (Brier, log) make honest probabilities optimal (Theorem 5.6); good forecasts are calibrated and sharp.
  • Expected test MSE = noise + bias² + variance (Theorem 5.7). For ridge both terms have closed forms in the singular values (Theorem 5.8); for kNN the variance is \(\sigma^2/k\) (Proposition 5.9).
  • Excess risk = approximation + estimation + optimisation error. Choose complexity by cross-validation, with the one-standard-error rule as a guard against noise.

exercises 5
  1. For the data \(\{1,2,3,10\}\), find every constant minimising the squared, absolute and pinball (\(\tau=0.75\)) losses.

    Squared: the mean, 4. Absolute: any \(c\in[2,3]\) (both middle values are medians). Pinball at 0.75: \(c\) with \(P(Y<c)\le0.75\le P(Y\le c)\); \(P(Y\le c)\ge0.75\) needs \(c\ge3\), and \(P(Y<c)\le0.75\) needs \(c\le10\), so any \(c\in[3,10]\). Small samples often have intervals of minimisers.

  2. Poisson regression sets \(\mu_i=\exp(w^\top x_i)\) and minimises \(\sum_i(\mu_i-y_i\log\mu_i)\). Show that its gradient is \(\sum_i(\mu_i-y_i)x_i\), the same "prediction minus label" form as least squares and logistic regression.

    With \(s_i=w^\top x_i\), each term is \(e^{s_i}-y_is_i\), whose derivative in \(s_i\) is \(e^{s_i}-y_i=\mu_i-y_i\); multiply by \(\partial s_i/\partial w=x_i\). This happens for every generalised linear model with its "canonical" link, a property of exponential families.

  3. A missed fraud costs a bank $500 and investigating a legitimate transaction costs $10. Above what fraud probability should a transaction be investigated?

    \(c_{\text{FP}}/(c_{\text{FP}}+c_{\text{FN}})=10/510\approx0.0196\). Investigate anything with at least a 2% chance of fraud. Thresholds this low are common, which is one reason fraud systems produce so many alerts.

  4. Show that the conditional hinge risk $$C_\eta(s)=\eta\max(0,1-s)+(1-\eta)\max(0,1+s)$$ is minimised at \(s=1\) when \(\eta>\tfrac12\) and at \(s=-1\) when \(\eta<\tfrac12\). Why does this mean an SVM score is not a probability?

    \(C_\eta\) is piecewise linear with kinks at \(\pm1\). On \([-1,1]\) it equals \(\eta(1-s)+(1-\eta)(1+s)=1+(1-2\eta)s\), decreasing when \(\eta>\tfrac12\); outside \([-1,1]\) it increases. So the minimum is at \(s=1\) (or \(-1\)), the same for \(\eta=0.51\) and \(\eta=0.99\): the minimiser carries the sign of \(2\eta-1\) and nothing else. To get probabilities from an SVM one fits a separate calibration map (Platt scaling).

  5. Show that \(\mathrm{KL}\big(\mathrm{Bern}(0.6)\,\|\,\mathrm{Bern}(0.5)\big)\approx0.0201\) nats, and that the expected excess log score from reporting \(q\) when the truth is \(p\) equals \(\mathrm{KL}(\mathrm{Bern}(p)\|\mathrm{Bern}(q))\).

    \(0.6\log1.2+0.4\log0.8=0.1094-0.0893=0.0201\). The expected log score is \(H(p)+\mathrm{KL}(p\|q)\) (Theorem 5.6), and honest reporting scores \(H(p)\), so the excess is exactly the KL divergence.

  6. Show that the absolute score \(|q-y|\) is not proper: for \(p>\tfrac12\) its expected value is minimised by reporting \(q=1\).

    \(\mathbb E|q-Y|=p(1-q)+(1-p)q=p+q(1-2p)\), linear in \(q\) with slope \(1-2p<0\) when \(p>\tfrac12\), so it is minimised at \(q=1\). A forecaster judged this way should exaggerate every forecast to 0 or 1.

  7. Show that a forecaster who always reports the base rate \(\bar y\) has Brier score \(\bar y(1-\bar y)\). Check against the NBA output.

    \(\frac1n\sum_i(\bar y-y_i)^2\) is the variance of the 0/1 outcomes, \(\bar y-\bar y^2=\bar y(1-\bar y)\). With \(\bar y=0.622\): \(0.622\times0.378\approx0.2351\), matching the printed 0.2350 (the difference is rounding of \(\bar y\)). In Murphy's decomposition, Brier = reliability − resolution + uncertainty, this is the uncertainty term; a forecaster beats it by having resolution.

  8. In one dimension with an orthonormal design, ridge returns \(z/(1+\lambda)\) where \(z\sim\mathcal N(\beta,\sigma^2)\). Find the \(\lambda\) minimising mean squared error, and interpret it as a prior variance.

    MSE \(=\big(\tfrac\lambda{1+\lambda}\big)^2\beta^2+\tfrac{\sigma^2}{(1+\lambda)^2}\). Differentiating, the minimum is at \(\lambda=\sigma^2/\beta^2\). In Bayesian terms (Theorem 3.7) \(\lambda=\sigma^2/\tau^2\), so the best penalty corresponds to a prior variance \(\tau^2=\beta^2\): a prior whose scale matches the true effect size.

  9. Using Proposition 5.9, at what \(k\) does kNN regression on evenly spaced points \(x_i=i/n\) with \(f(x)=x^2\) balance bias² and variance at \(x_0=0.5\)? (Take the neighbours symmetric about \(x_0\), \(n=100\), \(\sigma=0.3\).)

    The neighbours are \(x_0\pm j/n\) for \(j\) up to about \(k/2\). Averaging \(f(x_0+t)=x_0^2+2x_0t+t^2\) over symmetric \(t\) leaves bias \(\approx\overline{t^2}\approx\frac{(k/2n)^2}{3}=\frac{k^2}{12n^2}\). Bias² \(=k^4/(144n^4)\) and variance \(=\sigma^2/k\). Minimising the sum: \(4k^3/(144n^4)=\sigma^2/k^2\), so \(k^5=36\sigma^2n^4\), giving \(k=(36\cdot0.09\cdot10^8)^{1/5}\approx50\): half the data. With a gently curved function and noisy data, very wide neighbourhoods pay off; with sharper curvature \(k\) would be much smaller.

  10. In the bias–variance widget, set the degree to 9 and raise \(n\) from 30 to 120. By roughly what factor does the variance fall? Why is it more than the factor of 4 that \(\sigma^2/n\) scaling would suggest?

    It falls by far more than 4 (by one to two orders of magnitude). Variance of a high-degree polynomial fit is dominated by a few training sets in which the points near the ends of the interval happen to be sparse, giving wild extrapolation; with more points such gaps become rare. The \(\sigma^2p/n\) rule holds for a fixed, well-spread design, not for random designs near the interpolation threshold, a first glimpse of why Lecture 6's double descent happens.

method
Brier and log scores, reliability diagrams, Murphy's decomposition into reliability, resolution and uncertainty; bootstrap confidence intervals; compare forecasters on the same events.
outcome
A scored league table of forecasters with honest uncertainty, and a clear demonstration of how improper scores would have changed the ranking.

3. Robust losses for messy civic data

question
How much do a few extreme records distort least-squares models of New York City service times, and do Huber or quantile losses fix it?
data
NYC 311 service requests (time to close by agency and complaint type) or NYC taxi trip durations from the Taxi and Limousine Commission.
method
Fit squared, absolute, Huber and quantile models; measure coefficient stability when the top 0.1% of durations are removed or corrupted; evaluate on held-out data with each loss.
outcome
Practical guidance on loss choice for heavy-tailed administrative data, a common situation in public-sector analytics.
