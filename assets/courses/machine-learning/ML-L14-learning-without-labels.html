Every method so far has been given pairs \((x,y)\) and asked to predict \(y\). Most data come without the \(y\): taxi trips without a note of which days were unusual, images without captions. Part IV asks what can be learned from \(x\) alone. This lecture covers density estimation, the curse of dimensionality, anomaly detection, energy-based networks and self-supervision, and ends with the question that runs through Part IV: without labels, how do we know we have learned anything?

By the end you should be able to build and tune kernel density estimates and derive the \(n^{-1/5}\) bandwidth rate; prove that distances concentrate in high dimensions; detect anomalies by seasonal baseline, isolation forest, local outlier factor and nearest-neighbour distance; prove that a Hopfield network's energy never increases and describe Boltzmann machines; state the InfoNCE bound; and evaluate unsupervised results without ground truth.

1 — What "structure" means

With unlabelled data \(x_1,\dots,x_n\) drawn from \(p(x)\), the only thing to learn is \(p\) itself, or a feature of it. A probability density \(p(x)\) is a histogram at infinite resolution: high where points crowd together, and its area over a range is the probability of landing there. Four features of \(p\) organise Part IV:

  • Density. Where are the data concentrated? An estimate \(\hat p\) answers every other question in principle (Section 2).
  • Clusters. Do the data fall into dense groups separated by sparse regions? Lecture 15.
  • Low-dimensional shape. Do the data lie near a curve, a surface or a subspace? Lecture 16.
  • Anomalies. Which points lie where \(p\) is small? Section 4.

Mixture models (Lecture 17a) combine the first three: a density built from clusters, each with a low-dimensional shape. Copulas (Lecture 17b) take a joint density apart instead, into one distribution per variable and a description of how the variables depend on one another. On top sits a newer idea, self-supervised learning (Section 6): hide part of each example and predict it. It is how every large language and vision model is now pretrained.

idea — unsupervised learning is estimating \(p(x)\), in whole or in part

A density estimate is the most complete answer and the hardest to get. A clustering, a projection or an anomaly score each estimates one feature of \(p\) (modes, directions, tails), needs less data, and so remains feasible in high dimensions (Section 3).

2 — Histograms and kernel density estimation

The histogram cuts the line into bins of width \(h\) and divides each count by \(nh\), so the bars have total area 1. Its shape depends on where the bins start, and it is a step function even when the density is smooth. The kernel density estimate (KDE) fixes both by centring a small bump on every data point and adding the bumps up.

idea — piles of sand

Tip an identical pile of sand on each data point; crowded points merge into a dune, whose height is the KDE. The kernel is the shape of a pile and the bandwidth \(h\) its spread. Narrow piles reproduce every accident of sampling; wide ones flatten everything into one mound.

definition 14.1 — kernel density estimate

A kernel is a function \(K\ge0\) with

$$\int K=1,\qquad \int uK(u)\,du=0\qquad \mu_2=\int u^2K(u)\,du<\infty.$$

Given data \(x_1,\dots,x_n\in\mathbb R\) and a bandwidth \(h>0\), the kernel density estimate is

$$\hat f_h(x)=\frac1{nh}\sum_{i=1}^nK\!\left(\frac{x-x_i}{h}\right).$$

Common kernels:

  • Gaussian: \(K(u)=e^{-u^2/2}/\sqrt{2\pi}\);
  • Epanechnikov: \(K(u)=\tfrac34(1-u^2)\) on \([-1,1]\);
  • box: \(K(u)=\tfrac12\) on \([-1,1]\).

In \(d\) dimensions one uses \(h^{-d}K(\lVert x-x_i\rVert/h)\) with a radial kernel, or a bandwidth matrix.

The estimate is itself a density (substitute \(u=(x-x_i)/h\)). The kernel matters little; \(h\) matters a great deal.

data — Old Faithful eruptions

Durations of 272 eruptions of the Old Faithful geyser in Yellowstone, in minutes, with the preceding waiting times, as distributed with R (datasets::faithful, from Härdle, 1991; see also Azzalini and Bowman, 1990), via Vincent Arel-Bundock's Rdatasets on GitHub. # Old Faithful: histograms, kernel density estimates and three ways to choose the bandwidth
import numpy as np, pandas as pd
x = pd.read_csv('data/faithful.csv').eruptions.to_numpy()        # 272 eruption durations (minutes)
n = len(x); s = x.std(ddof=1); iqr = np.subtract(*np.percentile(x, [75, 25]))
print(f"n = {n}, mean {x.mean():.2f} min, sd {s:.2f}, IQR {iqr:.2f}")

def kde(grid, data, h):                    # Gaussian-kernel density estimate on a grid
    u = (grid[:, None] - data[None, :]) / h
    return np.exp(-0.5 * u**2).sum(1) / (len(data) * h * np.sqrt(2 * np.pi))

def loo_loglik(data, h):                   # leave-one-out log-likelihood per point
    u = (data[:, None] - data[None, :]) / h
    K = np.exp(-0.5 * u**2) / (h * np.sqrt(2 * np.pi)); np.fill_diagonal(K, 0)
    return np.mean(np.log(K.sum(1) / (len(data) - 1)))

def lscv(data, h):                         # least-squares cross-validation (estimates ISE - const)
    d = data[:, None] - data[None, :]; m = len(data)
    phi = lambda v, s: np.exp(-0.5 * (v / s)**2) / (s * np.sqrt(2 * np.pi))
    term1 = phi(d, h * np.sqrt(2)).sum() / m**2
    K = phi(d, h); np.fill_diagonal(K, 0)
    return term1 - 2 * K.sum() / (m * (m - 1))

hs = np.exp(np.linspace(np.log(0.03), np.log(1.5), 400))
h_silv = 0.9 * min(s, iqr / 1.34) * n**(-1/5)                   # Silverman's rule of thumb
h_ll = hs[np.argmax([loo_loglik(x, h) for h in hs])]
h_ls = hs[np.argmin([lscv(x, h) for h in hs])]
for name, h in [("Silverman's rule", h_silv), ("leave-one-out likelihood", h_ll), ("least-squares CV", h_ls)]:
    g = np.linspace(1, 6, 1001); f = kde(g, x, h)
    modes = g[1:-1][(f[1:-1] > f[:-2]) & (f[1:-1] > f[2:])]

for h in [0.05, 1.0]:

    print(f"h = {h:4.2f}: {((f[1:-1] > f[:-2]) & (f[1:-1] > f[2:])).sum():2d} modes, LOO log-lik {loo_loglik(x, h):+.3f}")
for bins in [5, 20, 80]:                                         # histograms: same dial, coarser

Silverman's rule of thumb,

$$h=0.9\min(s,\mathrm{IQR}/1.34)\,n^{-1/5},$$

is the normal-theory choice (Theorem 14.1), made robust by the interquartile range. It gives \(h=0.335\) minutes and two modes, at 1.98 and 4.38; the geyser is far from normal, so it oversmooths. Two data-driven selectors agree on \(h=0.102\):

  • Leave-one-out likelihood scores the density that the estimate from the other 271 points puts on each held-out point. Too small an \(h\) leaves it in a gap; too large spreads density too thin.
  • Least-squares cross-validation estimates the integrated squared error \(\int(\hat f_h-f)^2\) up to a constant.

The bandwidth dial

A KDE of the 272 eruptions (rose; rug below) over a histogram with bin width \(2h\). Slide the bandwidth and watch the modes and the leave-one-out log-likelihood. Try the waiting times and the box kernel.

The bandwidth trades bias for variance, as \(k\) did in \(k\)-nearest neighbours (Lecture 1). A wide kernel shaves peaks and fills valleys (bias of about \(h^2f''\)); a narrow one averages over only about \(nh\) points (variance). The mean integrated squared error (MISE) adds the two, integrated along the axis.

theorem 14.1 — bias and variance of the kernel density estimate

Let \(x_1,\dots,x_n\) be independent with a density \(f\) that has two bounded continuous derivatives, and let \(K\) be a kernel as in Definition 14.1 with \(R(K)=\int K^2<\infty\). As \(n\to\infty\) and \(h\to0\) with \(nh\to\infty\),

$$\mathbb E\,\hat f_h(x)-f(x)=\tfrac12h^2\mu_2f''(x)+o(h^2),$$ $$\operatorname{Var}\hat f_h(x)=\frac{f(x)R(K)}{nh}+o\!\left(\frac1{nh}\right).$$

Integrating squared bias plus variance over \(x\) gives the asymptotic mean integrated squared error

$$\mathrm{AMISE}(h)=\frac{h^4\mu_2^2R(f'')}{4}+\frac{R(K)}{nh},$$

which is minimised by

$$h^*=\left(\frac{R(K)}{\mu_2^2R(f'')\,n}\right)^{1/5}\propto n^{-1/5},\qquad \mathrm{AMISE}(h^*)\propto n^{-4/5}.$$

Bias. Each term of \(\hat f_h\) has the same mean, so

$$\mathbb E\,\hat f_h(x)=\frac1h\int K\!\left(\frac{x-t}{h}\right)f(t)\,dt=\int K(u)f(x-hu)\,du.$$

By Taylor's theorem,

$$f(x-hu)=f(x)-huf'(x)+\tfrac12h^2u^2f''(x)+o(h^2).$$

Integrating against \(K\): the first term gives \(f(x)\), the second vanishes because \(\int uK=0\), and the third gives \(\tfrac12h^2\mu_2f''(x)\).

Variance. The terms are independent, so the variance is \(1/n\) times the variance of one term, which is at most its second moment:

$$\frac1{nh^2}\int K^2\!\left(\frac{x-t}{h}\right)f(t)\,dt=\frac1{nh}\int K^2(u)f(x-hu)\,du=\frac{f(x)R(K)}{nh}+o\!\left(\frac1{nh}\right).$$

Subtracting the squared mean changes this only by \(O(1/n)\), which is smaller.

Optimum. Integrate \(\text{bias}^2+\text{variance}\) over \(x\), using \(\int f=1\), to get the AMISE. Setting its derivative to zero,

$$h^3\mu_2^2R(f'')-\frac{R(K)}{nh^2}=0$$

gives \(h^*\), at which both terms are of order \(n^{-4/5}\). Passing from the pointwise expansions to the integrated one needs uniformly small remainders, which holds under the stated conditions; see Wasserman (2006, §6.3) and Wand and Jones (1995, ch. 2). \(\square\)

For a normal density with standard deviation \(\sigma\) and a Gaussian kernel, the formula gives (Exercise 2)

$$h^*=(4/3)^{1/5}\sigma n^{-1/5}\approx1.06\,\sigma n^{-1/5},$$

the origin of Silverman's rule. The error falls like \(n^{-4/5}\), slower than the \(n^{-1}\) of a correct parametric model: the price of assuming nothing about the shape. And \(h^*\) depends on the unknown roughness \(R(f'')\), so it must be estimated, as cross-validation does. To check the rates, the code averages the exactly computable integrated squared error over 40 standard normal samples per \(n\) and finds the best \(h\) on a grid.

code — the rates of Theorem 14.1 by simulation 
# Theorem 14.1 by simulation: the MISE-optimal bandwidth shrinks like n^(-1/5), the error like n^(-4/5)
rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
phi = lambda v, s: np.exp(-0.5 * (v / s)**2) / (s * np.sqrt(2 * np.pi))
def ise(data, h):            # exact integrated squared error of a Gaussian KDE when the truth is N(0,1)
    d = data[:, None] - data[None, :]; m = len(data)
    return phi(d, h * np.sqrt(2)).sum() / m**2 - 2 * phi(data, np.sqrt(1 + h**2)).mean() + 1 / (2 * np.sqrt(np.pi))
ns, hopt, mise = [50, 100, 200, 400, 800, 1600], [], []
hgrid = np.exp(np.linspace(np.log(0.05), np.log(1.2), 60))
for m in ns:
    reps = [rng.standard_normal(m) for _ in range(40)]
    M = np.array([np.mean([ise(r, h) for r in reps]) for h in hgrid])
    hopt.append(hgrid[M.argmin()]); mise.append(M.min())
    print(f"n = {m:5d}: best h = {hopt[-1]:.3f} (theory 1.06 n^-1/5 = {1.06 * m**-0.2:.3f}), MISE = {M.min():.5f}")
b_h = np.polyfit(np.log(ns), np.log(hopt), 1)[0]; b_m = np.polyfit(np.log(ns), np.log(mise), 1)[0]
print(f"log-log slopes: bandwidth {b_h:.3f} (theory -0.2), MISE {b_m:.3f} (theory -0.8)")

spot check 14.2

A KDE with a well-chosen bandwidth is fitted to 1,000 points. You collect 32,000 points from the same source. By what factor should the bandwidth be multiplied, according to Theorem 14.1?

\(h^*\propto n^{-1/5}\), and \(n\) grows by a factor of 32.
\(32^{-1/5}=2^{-1}=0.5\): halve the bandwidth. The error falls by \(32^{-4/5}=1/16\): 32 times the data buys a 16-fold reduction in MISE, not a 32-fold one.
Bayes — density estimation is the generative half of Bayes' rule

Bayes' rule writes a classifier as \(P(y\mid x)\propto\pi_y\,p(x\mid y)\). A generative classifier estimates the class-conditional densities \(p(x\mid y)\) and priors \(\pi_y\); naive Bayes (Lectures 4 and 13) uses products of one-dimensional densities, and a KDE per class gives a nonparametric version that can draw any decision boundary. The same densities detect points unlike any class, sample new data, and survive a shift in base rates (new \(\pi_y\), not new densities). A KDE is also a mixture with one equally weighted Gaussian on every point: the extreme case of the mixtures of Lecture 17a.

history — Rosenblatt, Parzen and the window

Murray Rosenblatt (Chicago) introduced the kernel estimate, with its bias and variance, in "Remarks on some nonparametric estimates of a density function" (Annals of Mathematical Statistics, 1956). Emanuel Parzen (Stanford) gave the general theory in "On estimation of a probability density function and mode" (1962), hence the Parzen window. Evelyn Fix and Joseph Hodges had proposed nearest-neighbour discrimination in a 1951 US Air Force report. Bernard Silverman's Density Estimation for Statistics and Data Analysis (1986) made the rule of thumb standard, using Old Faithful (a different sample of 107 eruptions) throughout.

3 — The curse of dimensionality

A KDE needs a few hundred points in one dimension and millions in ten. The "curse of dimensionality" (coined by Richard Bellman in 1957 for dynamic programming) names facts about high-dimensional space that defeat low-dimensional intuition. The two most useful:

theorem 14.2 — volume in a thin shell; concentration of distances

(a) Thin shell. For \(0<\varepsilon<1\), the fraction of the volume of a ball of radius \(r\) in \(\mathbb R^d\) lying within \(\varepsilon r\) of its surface is

$$1-(1-\varepsilon)^d\;\longrightarrow\;1\qquad(d\to\infty).$$

(b) Concentration. Let \(X,Y\in\mathbb R^d\) be independent, each with independent coordinates distributed like \(Z\), where \(Z\) has variance \(\sigma^2\) and a finite fourth moment. Let \(v=\operatorname{Var}\big((Z_1-Z_2)^2\big)\) for independent copies \(Z_1,Z_2\). Then

$$\mathbb E\lVert X-Y\rVert^2=2d\sigma^2,\qquad \operatorname{Var}\lVert X-Y\rVert^2=d\,v.$$

Hence the squared distance divided by its mean has standard deviation

$$\frac{\sqrt v}{2\sigma^2\sqrt d}\;\longrightarrow\;0$$

and for every \(\delta>0\),

$$P\left(\left|\frac{\lVert X-Y\rVert^2}{2d\sigma^2}-1\right|>\delta\right)\le\frac{v}{4\sigma^4\delta^2d}\to0.$$

(a) Volume in \(\mathbb R^d\) scales as the \(d\)-th power of length, so the inner ball of radius \((1-\varepsilon)r\) has \((1-\varepsilon)^d\) times the volume of the whole ball. The shell is the rest.

(b) Write \(\lVert X-Y\rVert^2=\sum_{k=1}^dD_k\) with \(D_k=(X_k-Y_k)^2\). The \(D_k\) are independent and identically distributed, with mean

$$\mathbb E(X_k-Y_k)^2=\operatorname{Var}X_k+\operatorname{Var}Y_k=2\sigma^2$$

and variance \(v\), finite because \(Z\) has a fourth moment. Means add and, by independence, so do variances, giving \(2d\sigma^2\) and \(dv\).

Chebyshev's inequality with deviation \(\delta\cdot2d\sigma^2\) gives the bound

$$\frac{dv}{4d^2\sigma^4\delta^2}.\qquad\square$$

Most of a high-dimensional orange is peel (a). And a squared distance is a sum of \(d\) independent terms, which concentrates, so the nearest neighbour is barely nearer than the farthest (b); the same holds for a fixed query point in place of \(Y\). Methods built on "nearby points are similar" (the KDE, nearest neighbours, the detectors of Section 4) lose their grip. Neighbourhoods also empty out: to hold 10% of uniform points in a unit cube, a sub-cube needs side 0.1 in one dimension, \(0.1^{1/10}\approx0.79\) in ten and \(0.1^{1/100}\approx0.98\) in a hundred. The code also tests a KDE with bandwidth \(h=n^{-1/(d+4)}\) on held-out data.

code — distances, shells and density estimation as the dimension grows 
# The curse of dimensionality: distances concentrate, volume moves to the shell, neighbourhoods empty
import numpy as np
rng = np.random.default_rng(1)
print("   d   mean dist   sd/mean   (max-min)/min   P(in 10% shell)   KDE log-lik gap")

    shell = 1 - 0.9**d                                        # fraction of a d-ball within 10% of its surface
    # a Gaussian KDE (Scott's bandwidth) fitted to 500 N(0,I) points, scored on 500 new points,
    # compared with the true log-density: how much likelihood per dimension does the KDE lose?
    Z, Zt = rng.standard_normal((500, d)), rng.standard_normal((500, d))

    true_ll = (-0.5 * (Zt**2).sum(1) - 0.5 * d * np.log(2 * np.pi)).mean()

Real data often escape the curse because their intrinsic dimension, the number of coordinates needed to say where the points lie, is far below the number recorded; a crumpled sheet of paper is still two-dimensional. 4 — Anomaly detection

An anomaly is a point that the bulk of the data makes unlikely: flag \(x\) when \(\hat p(x)\) is small. Three families of cheaper stand-ins:

  • Model-based: fit what "normal" looks like (a seasonal profile, a regression, a forecast) and score each point by its residual.
  • Distance-based: score a point by its distance to its neighbours, a density estimate in disguise.
  • Isolation-based: ask how easily random splits cut a point off from the rest.

All three fit "normal" from the same unlabelled data and assume anomalies are too rare to distort it. Two cautions. An anomaly is not an error: a blizzard is real, merely unusual. And rarity means false alarms: if one day in a thousand is unusual and the detector wrongly flags one ordinary day in a hundred, most flagged days are ordinary, the base-rate problem of Lecture 13.

data — NYC taxi passengers (Numenta Anomaly Benchmark)

New York City taxi passengers per half-hour from 1 July 2014 to 31 January 2015 (10,320 values), aggregated from Taxi and Limousine Commission trip records by Numenta for the Numenta Anomaly Benchmark (NAB; Lavin and Ahmad, 2015), file realKnownCause/nyc_taxi.csv on GitHub (AGPL-3.0). NAB labels five anomalies, "the NYC marathon, Thanksgiving, Christmas, New Year's Day, and a snow storm", each inside a window several days wide where detections count as correct (labels/combined_windows.json). The labels are used only to judge results, never to fit.

Taxi demand has a strong weekly cycle, so the first detector's baseline for each half-hour is the median of all values at that half-hour of the week (the median resists the anomalies). The residual is the log ratio to baseline; a day's score is its mean absolute residual over 48 half-hours.

code — a seasonal baseline and the days that do not fit 
# NYC taxi demand: a seasonal baseline, residuals, and the days that do not fit
import pandas as pd, json
df = pd.read_csv('data/nyc_taxi.csv', parse_dates=['timestamp'])        # passengers per 30 minutes
win = json.load(open('data/combined_windows.json'))['realKnownCause/nyc_taxi.csv']
win = [(pd.Timestamp(a).normalize(), pd.Timestamp(b).normalize()) for a, b in win]   # NAB's 5 windows
slot = (df.timestamp.dt.dayofweek * 48 + df.timestamp.dt.hour * 2 + df.timestamp.dt.minute // 30).to_numpy()
base = df.value.groupby(slot).transform('median').to_numpy()          # typical value for this half-hour of the week
df['r'] = np.log(df.value / base)                                      # log residual: 0.1 is about 10% above normal
df['date'] = df.timestamp.dt.normalize()

definition 14.2 — isolation forest

An isolation tree is grown on a random subsample of \(\psi\) points by recursive splitting:

  • at each node, pick a feature at random and a split value uniformly between that feature's minimum and maximum in the node;
  • stop when a node holds one point, or a depth limit is reached.

The path length \(h(x)\) is the number of edges from the root to the leaf containing \(x\), with a correction for leaves that still hold several points. An isolation forest (Liu, Ting and Zhou, 2008) averages over many trees and reports

$$s(x)=2^{-\mathbb E[h(x)]/c(\psi)},\qquad c(\psi)=2H(\psi-1)-\frac{2(\psi-1)}{\psi},$$

where \(H(i)\approx\ln i+0.5772\) is the harmonic number. The normaliser \(c(\psi)\) is the average path length of an unsuccessful search in a binary search tree on \(\psi\) keys, used as the typical depth.

  • \(s\) near 1: anomaly;
  • \(s=0.5\): typical depth;
  • \(s\) well below 0.5: ordinary.

Anomalies are "few and different", so random splits cut them off early, as "taller than 1.9 m?" singles out the one two-metre person in twenty questions. The trees (Lecture 8) have no target; only depth matters. With no distances, the method suffers less from Theorem 14.2, and it scales to millions of points because each tree sees a small subsample.

5 — Energy-based models: Hopfield networks and Boltzmann machines

Physics describes many systems as a ball rolling into a valley. An energy \(E(s)\) scores every state \(s\), the system moves towards lower energy, and at temperature \(T\) state \(s\) has Boltzmann probability

$$p(s)=\frac{e^{-E(s)/T}}{Z}.$$

Near \(T=0\) the system sits in the lowest valleys; at high \(T\) it wanders almost uniformly. To model data, choose an energy whose valleys sit where the data are, so \(p(s)\propto e^{-E(s)}\); learning means shaping the landscape. The first neural networks to learn without labels worked this way.

definition 14.3 — Hopfield network

A Hopfield network has \(N\) binary units \(s_i\in\{-1,+1\}\) and symmetric weights with zero diagonal:

$$W_{ij}=W_{ji},\qquad W_{ii}=0.$$

Its energy is

$$E(s)=-\tfrac12\,s^\top Ws=-\sum_{i<j}W_{ij}s_is_j.$$

An asynchronous update chooses one unit \(i\) and sets it to the sign of its local field \(h_i=\sum_jW_{ij}s_j\), with \(+1\) when \(h_i=0\).

To store patterns \(\xi^1,\dots,\xi^P\in\{-1,+1\}^N\), the Hebb rule sets

$$W=\frac1N\sum_{\mu=1}^P\xi^\mu(\xi^\mu)^\top\quad\text{with the diagonal set to zero.}$$

The Hebb rule strengthens connections between units that agree in the stored patterns ("cells that fire together wire together"). With one pattern \(\xi\), the field at \(s=\xi\) is \(h_i=\frac{N-1}N\xi_i\), so \(\xi\) is a fixed point, and from a copy with fewer than half its bits wrong every update moves towards it. This is a content-addressable memory: as the first bars of a song recall the rest, a corrupted pattern rolls down into its valley. But why does the network settle at all?

theorem 14.3 — the Hopfield energy never increases

Under asynchronous updates:

  • each update leaves the energy unchanged or lowers it;
  • from any starting state, the network reaches a fixed point (a state no update changes) after finitely many flips.

Suppose unit \(i\) changes from \(s_i\) to \(s_i'\), and let \(\Delta=s_i'-s_i\in\{-2,+2\}\).

Using \(W_{ij}=W_{ji}\) and \(W_{ii}=0\), the terms of \(E\) involving \(s_i\) are \(-s_i\sum_{j\ne i}W_{ij}s_j=-s_ih_i\), and \(h_i\) does not involve \(s_i\). Hence

$$\Delta E=-\Delta\,h_i.$$

A flip happens only when \(s_i'=\operatorname{sign}(h_i)\ne s_i\), so either \(\Delta\) has the sign of \(h_i\), giving \(\Delta E=-2|h_i|<0\); or \(h_i=0\), giving \(\Delta E=0\), and by the tie rule the flip is from \(-1\) to \(+1\).

The state space is finite, so the energy can strictly decrease only finitely often. Between strict decreases every flip turns a \(-1\) into a \(+1\), which can happen at most \(N\) times in a row. So only finitely many flips occur, and once none is possible the state is a fixed point. \(\square\)

Symmetry and asynchrony are both essential: otherwise the network can cycle or oscillate (Exercise 4). The theorem guarantees some minimum, not the right one. Too many stored patterns create crosstalk, stored patterns stop being minima and spurious mixtures appear. For random patterns the limit is near \(P\approx0.138N\) with small errors allowed (Amit, Gutfreund and Sompolinsky, 1985), and about \(N/(2\ln N)\) for perfect recall (McEliece et al., 1987).

import numpy as np
rng = np.random.default_rng(2)
def hebb(pats):                                   # W = (1/N) sum_mu xi xi^T, zero diagonal
    W = pats.T @ pats / pats.shape[1]; np.fill_diagonal(W, 0); return W
def energy(W, s): return -0.5 * s @ W @ s
def recall(W, s, rng, sweeps=20):                 # asynchronous updates in random order
    s = s.copy(); E = [energy(W, s)]

        changed = 0

            new = 1 if W[i] @ s >= 0 else -1
            if new != s[i]: s[i] = new; changed += 1; E.append(energy(W, s))
        if changed == 0: break
    assert np.all(np.diff(E) <= 1e-9)             # Theorem 14.3: the energy never goes up
    return s

A Hopfield network has three units with \(W_{12}=1\), \(W_{13}=-2\), \(W_{23}=1\), and is in state \(s=(+1,+1,+1)\). Unit 1 is updated. What is the change in energy?

\(h_1=1-2=-1\), so unit 1 flips to \(-1\) and \(\Delta=-2\); then \(\Delta E=-(-2)(-1)=-2\). Directly: \(E\) goes from \(-(1-2+1)=0\) to \(-(-1+2+1)=-2\).

A Hopfield network stores given patterns but learns no distribution. The Boltzmann machine (Ackley, Hinton and Sejnowski, 1985) does: its stochastic units sample states with Boltzmann probabilities, and hidden units let the energy capture dependencies the visible units alone cannot.

definition 14.4 — Boltzmann machine and restricted Boltzmann machine

A Boltzmann machine on binary units \(s=(v,h)\), split into visible units \(v\) and hidden units \(h\), assigns

$$p(s)=\frac{e^{-E(s)}}{Z},\qquad E(s)=-\tfrac12s^\top Ws-b^\top s,\qquad Z=\sum_se^{-E(s)},$$

and models the data by the marginal \(p(v)=\sum_hp(v,h)\).

A restricted Boltzmann machine (RBM) connects only visible to hidden units, with \(v,h\in\{0,1\}\) and

$$E(v,h)=-v^\top Wh-a^\top v-b^\top h.$$

The hidden units are then conditionally independent given \(v\), and vice versa:

$$P(h_j=1\mid v)=\sigma\Big(b_j+\sum_iW_{ij}v_i\Big),\qquad P(v_i=1\mid h)=\sigma\Big(a_i+\sum_jW_{ij}h_j\Big).$$
proposition 14.4 — the Boltzmann learning rule

For a Boltzmann machine as in Definition 14.4, the gradient of the log-likelihood of one visible vector is

$$\frac{\partial\log p(v)}{\partial W_{ij}}=\langle s_is_j\rangle_{\text{data}}-\langle s_is_j\rangle_{\text{model}},$$

where

  • the first average is over \(p(h\mid v)\) with \(v\) clamped to the data;
  • the second is over the model's joint distribution \(p(s)\).

For the RBM, \(s_is_j\) is \(v_ih_j\).

Write

$$\log p(v)=\log\sum_he^{-E(v,h)}-\log Z.$$

Since \(\partial(-E)/\partial W_{ij}=s_is_j\) (for \(i\ne j\), counting the symmetric pair once),

$$\frac{\partial}{\partial W_{ij}}\log\sum_he^{-E(v,h)}=\frac{\sum_hs_is_je^{-E(v,h)}}{\sum_he^{-E(v,h)}}=\sum_hp(h\mid v)\,s_is_j.$$

In the same way,

$$\frac{\partial\log Z}{\partial W_{ij}}=\sum_sp(s)\,s_is_j.\qquad\square$$

The rule is local: strengthen connections between units on together when the network sees data ("wake"), weaken those on together when it dreams freely ("sleep"); learning stops when dreams match the data's statistics. The model term needs samples, in general from a Markov chain run to equilibrium. Gibbs sampling resamples units from their conditional probabilities, a whole RBM layer at once. Contrastive divergence (Hinton, 2002) starts the chain at the data and runs only one or a few steps: enough to see which way the model drifts. The persistent variant (Tieleman, 2008), used by scikit-learn, keeps its chains between updates.

quantum — Boltzmann machines are Ising models

With spins \(s_i=\pm1\), couplings \(J_{ij}=W_{ij}\) and fields \(b_i\), the Boltzmann energy is an Ising model of a magnet; a Hopfield network is one at zero temperature, and its capacity analysis came from spin-glass theory. A quantum annealer (such as D-Wave's) encodes an Ising energy in superconducting qubits, adds a transverse field that lets the state tunnel, and slowly removes it. Its imperfect, low-energy readouts have served as approximate Boltzmann samples for the "model" term of Proposition 14.4 (Adachi and Henderson, 2015); a quantum Boltzmann machine puts the transverse field in the model (Amin et al., 2018). The samples' effective temperature is hard to control, and no training advantage over good classical samplers has been demonstrated.

history — from Hebb to the 2024 Nobel Prize

Donald Hebb proposed in The Organization of Behavior (1949) that a connection between two neurons strengthens when they fire together. Shun-ichi Amari (1972), William Little (1974) and others built associative memories on the idea, but John Hopfield's "Neural networks and physical systems with emergent collective computational abilities" (PNAS, 1982) made the energy function central and brought physicists in. Geoffrey Hinton and Terrence Sejnowski, with David Ackley, made it the stochastic Boltzmann machine (1983–85); Paul Smolensky's "harmonium" (1986) was the restricted version; contrastive divergence (2002) and deep belief networks (2006) made it trainable and deep. In October 2024 Hopfield and Hinton received the Nobel Prize in Physics "for foundational discoveries and inventions that enable machine learning with artificial neural networks".

The energy view lives on. "Modern" Hopfield networks with a softmax energy store exponentially many patterns, and their one-step update is the attention of Lecture 12 (Ramsauer et al., 2021). Diffusion models learn the "score" \(\nabla_x\log p(x)=-\nabla_xE(x)\), which avoids \(Z\) altogether.

6 — Self-supervised learning: inventing labels from the data

Modelling the whole input distribution, as the Boltzmann machine does, is expensive and not obviously useful. Since about 2018 a different idea has taken over: hide part of an example and predict it from the rest, so that training examples are as plentiful as raw data. This pretext task matters only for what the network must learn to solve it. Two main kinds:

  • Masking and prediction. GPT (Lecture 12) predicts the next character. BERT (Devlin et al., 2019) predicts 15% hidden tokens from both sides; masked autoencoders (He et al., 2022) reconstruct the pixels of 75% hidden image patches.
  • Contrastive learning. Make two randomly altered views of each example (crops, flips, colour changes; nearby audio segments; an image and its caption for CLIP) and train an encoder to map views of the same example close together and of different examples far apart, picking a view's partner out of many candidates.
definition 14.5 — the InfoNCE loss

Let \(f(x,y)\) be a score ("critic") for how well \(y\) matches \(x\). Given \(x\), draw

  • one positive \(y^+\) from \(p(y\mid x)\), and
  • \(K-1\) negatives independently from \(p(y)\).

The InfoNCE loss is the cross-entropy of picking out the positive among the \(K\) candidates:

$$\mathcal L_{\text{InfoNCE}}=-\,\mathbb E\left[\log\frac{e^{f(x,y^+)}}{\sum_{j=1}^Ke^{f(x,y_j)}}\right].$$

In SimCLR (Chen et al., 2020):

  • a batch of \(B\) images gives \(2B\) views \(z_1,\dots,z_{2B}\), passed through an encoder and a small projection head and normalised to unit length;
  • the critic is \(f(z_i,z_k)=z_i^\top z_k/\tau\), with a temperature \(\tau\);
  • each view's partner is its positive and the other \(2B-2\) views are its negatives, so \(K=2B-1\).

The loss averaged over all \(2B\) anchors is called NT-Xent.

The augmentations declare what should not matter, as you recognise a friend in a cropped, darker photo; \(\tau\) sets how sharply near-misses are separated from clear mismatches. After training, the projection head is discarded and the encoder is tested by a linear probe: a linear classifier trained with a few labels on its frozen outputs. Our version fits one CPU core: a two-layer convolutional network with 128-dimensional output; views by shifting, flipping, erasing a square patch, changing brightness and adding noise; batches of 256 and 1,500 steps, about six passes over the 60,000 unlabelled training images.

The mutual information \(I(X;Y)\) measures how much knowing one quantity reduces uncertainty about the other (zero if independent), here in nats (one nat is about 1.44 bits).

theorem 14.5 — InfoNCE bounds the mutual information

For any critic \(f\) and \(K\) candidates drawn as in Definition 14.5,

$$I(X;Y)\;\ge\;\log K-\mathcal L_{\text{InfoNCE}}.$$

The bound is tightest for the critic

$$f(x,y)=\log\frac{p(y\mid x)}{p(y)}+c(x),$$

and even then it can never exceed \(\log K\).

(Statement; proofs in van den Oord, Li and Vinyals, 2018, and Poole et al., 2019.)

Minimising the contrastive loss thus maximises a lower bound on the views' mutual information. The \(\log K\) ceiling holds because a \(K\)-way cross-entropy is non-negative (Exercise 5): a \(K\)-option test can certify at most \(\log K\). The code checks both halves on correlated Gaussians, where the mutual information and best critic are known.

code — the InfoNCE bound for correlated Gaussians 
# Theorem 14.5 numerically: InfoNCE never exceeds the mutual information, nor log K
rng = np.random.default_rng(3); d, rho = 10, 0.8
I_true = -0.5 * d * np.log(1 - rho**2)                         # exact mutual information (nats)
def f_opt(u, w):        # best possible critic: log p(y|x)/p(y) for the Gaussian pair, up to a constant
    return (-((w[None, :, :] - rho * u[:, None, :])**2).sum(-1) / (2 * (1 - rho**2))
            + (w[None, :, :]**2).sum(-1) / 2)
print(f"d = {d}, correlation {rho}: true I(X;Y) = {I_true:.3f} nats")
for K in [2, 8, 64, 512, 4096]:
    est = []
    for _ in range(max(1, 20000 // K)):
        u = rng.standard_normal((K, d)); w = rho * u + np.sqrt(1 - rho**2) * rng.standard_normal((K, d))
        S = f_opt(u, w)                                         # S[i, j] = f(u_i, w_j); diagonal = positive pairs
        est.append(np.mean(np.diag(S) - (np.log(np.mean(np.exp(S - S.max(1, keepdims=True)), 1)) + S.max(1))))
    print(f"  K = {K:5d}: InfoNCE estimate {np.mean(est):.3f}   log K = {np.log(K):.3f}")

big picture — every foundation model is pretrained without labels

The large models of 2026 all begin with self-supervision at enormous scale. Language models (the GPT, Claude, Gemini, Llama and DeepSeek families) are pretrained on next-token prediction over trillions of tokens, then fine-tuned on human examples and preferences (Lecture 22). Vision models use masking (MAE) or self-distillation between views (DINOv2, 142 million curated images, no labels). CLIP trains image and text encoders with the InfoNCE loss on web image–caption pairs; such encoders steer most text-to-image generators. Speech (wav2vec 2.0), proteins (ESM) and single-cell "foundation models" mask or contrast in the same way. The reason is largely economic: labels are scarce, raw data abundant, and one encoder adapts to many tasks.

7 — Evaluating without ground truth

Without labels there is no error to measure, yet every unsupervised method returns something: a density, anomalies, features, a clustering (Lecture 15). Five tools help decide whether it is real:

  • Held-out likelihood. Score unseen data, as Section 2 did for the bandwidth. It measures the model itself, but needs a normalised density, which isolation forests and contrastive encoders lack.
  • Labels used only for evaluation. The NAB windows and linear probes. The most convincing check, but it judges one task, and labels have errors (unlabelled holidays, a marathon window that mostly contains a clock change).
  • Stability. A result that changes when the seed, a subsample or a tuning parameter changes slightly is not a property of the data.
  • Agreement between methods. Different assumptions, same answer, some reassurance. The Jaccard index (overlap over union) compares two detectors' flagged days.
  • Label-free diagnostics. For an embedding, alignment (views of one example map close together) and uniformity (embeddings spread over the sphere rather than collapse) (Wang and Isola, 2020).

idea — stability is necessary, not sufficient

A bug that ignores the data is perfectly stable and perfectly wrong: stability rules results out, never in. Methods sharing an assumption ("a day is a point in 48-dimensional space") can agree on the same mistake. The strongest evidence is a stable result, agreement between independent methods, and a check against information they did not use, itself examined for errors.

8 — Summary, exercises, projects, reading

summary
  • Without labels, learning means estimating the distribution \(p(x)\) or a feature of it: its density, clusters, low-dimensional shape or tails.
  • The kernel density estimate (Definition 14.1) has one important knob, the bandwidth, which trades bias \(\propto h^2\) for variance \(\propto1/(nh)\); the best bandwidth shrinks like \(n^{-1/5}\) and the error like \(n^{-4/5}\) (Theorem 14.1). On Old Faithful, cross-validation chooses a third of Silverman's bandwidth.
  • In high dimensions volume moves to a thin shell and pairwise distances concentrate, with relative spread \(\propto1/\sqrt d\) (Theorem 14.2); density estimation and distance-based methods need data exponential in the dimension, unless the data lie near a low-dimensional set.
  • Anomaly detection fits "normal" robustly and scores departures from it: seasonal residuals, nearest-neighbour distance, the local outlier factor and isolation forests (Definition 14.2) all find the January 2015 blizzard, New Year, Christmas and Thanksgiving in the taxi data. Their "false alarms" are unlabelled holidays, and the "marathon" anomaly is mostly the end of daylight saving time.
  • Hopfield networks store patterns as minima of an energy that asynchronous updates never increase (Theorem 14.3); capacity is about \(0.14N\) for random patterns and far less for correlated ones. Boltzmann machines learn by matching data and model correlations (Proposition 14.4); an RBM's features barely beat pixels here.
  • Self-supervised learning invents labels by masking or by contrasting views; the InfoNCE loss (Definition 14.5) lower-bounds mutual information but saturates at \(\log K\) (Theorem 14.5). In our small run, contrastive features beat an untrained network (0.772 against 0.732 with 1,000 labels) but not raw pixels (0.789) or a network trained on the same labels (0.830): self-supervision pays at scale.
  • Unsupervised results must be checked by held-out likelihood, labels used only for evaluation (with their own errors examined), stability, agreement and label-free diagnostics; none is sufficient alone.
exercises
  1. Two data points lie at \(-1\) and \(+1\). Compute the Gaussian kernel density estimate at \(x=0\) and at \(x=1\), first with bandwidth \(h=1\) and then with \(h=0.5\). How many modes does each estimate have?

    With \(h=1\):

    $$\hat f(0)=\tfrac12\big(\varphi(1)+\varphi(1)\big)=\varphi(1)=0.242,\qquad \hat f(1)=\tfrac12\big(\varphi(0)+\varphi(2)\big)=0.227.$$

    The centre is higher than the data points: the two bumps, two standard deviations apart, merge into a single flat-topped mode at 0.

    With \(h=0.5\), \(\hat f(x)=\varphi\big(2(x+1)\big)+\varphi\big(2(x-1)\big)\), so

    $$\hat f(0)=2\varphi(2)=0.108,\qquad \hat f(1)=\varphi(4)+\varphi(0)=0.399:$$

    two modes, near \(\pm1\). The number of modes depends on the bandwidth as much as on the data, which is why the third Old Faithful mode needs care.

  2. For a normal density with standard deviation \(\sigma\) and a Gaussian kernel, show that the AMISE-optimal bandwidth of Theorem 14.1 is \(h^*=(4/3)^{1/5}\sigma n^{-1/5}\approx1.06\,\sigma n^{-1/5}\). You may use \(R(f'')=\int f''(x)^2dx=3/(8\sqrt\pi\,\sigma^5)\).

    For the Gaussian kernel \(\mu_2=1\) and \(R(K)=\int\varphi^2=1/(2\sqrt\pi)\). Then

    $$h^*=\left(\frac{1/(2\sqrt\pi)}{3n/(8\sqrt\pi\sigma^5)}\right)^{1/5}=\left(\frac{4\sigma^5}{3n}\right)^{1/5}=\left(\tfrac43\right)^{1/5}\sigma n^{-1/5},$$

    and \((4/3)^{1/5}=1.059\). Silverman's 0.9 instead of 1.06, with the interquartile range, is a deliberate undersmoothing that copes better with skewed and bimodal densities.

  3. What fraction of the cube \([-1,1]^{10}\) lies inside its inscribed unit ball? (The volume of the unit ball in \(\mathbb R^{10}\) is \(\pi^5/5!\).) What does this say about sampling a ball by rejection from a cube in high dimensions?
    $$\frac{\pi^5/120}{2^{10}}=\frac{2.550}{1024}=0.00249.$$

    Only one sample in about 400 would be accepted in ten dimensions, and the ratio keeps falling faster than exponentially. The volume of a cube sits in its corners, at distance up to \(\sqrt d\) from the centre, while the ball reaches only 1.

  4. A Hopfield network of two units has \(W_{12}=W_{21}=-1\) and starts in \(s=(+1,+1)\). Show that if both units update simultaneously the network oscillates for ever, but under asynchronous updates it stops after one flip. Which assumption of Theorem 14.3 does the synchronous rule violate?

    Synchronously: each unit sees field \(-1\cdot(+1)=-1\), so both flip to \((-1,-1)\); there each sees \(+1\), and both flip back. The energy \(-W_{12}s_1s_2=s_1s_2\) is \(+1\) in both states, so it does not decrease.

    Asynchronously: updating unit 1 gives \((-1,+1)\) with energy \(-1\); unit 2 then sees field \(+1\) and stays, and unit 1 sees \(-1\) and stays: a fixed point.

    The proof of Theorem 14.3 computes \(\Delta E\) for a change in one unit with the others fixed; when two units change together, the cross term \(-W_{12}\Delta_1\Delta_2\) is not controlled.

  5. Show that \(\log K-\mathcal L_{\text{InfoNCE}}\le\log K\) for any critic, so InfoNCE can never certify more than \(\log K\) nats of mutual information, and that a critic which ignores its input (a constant \(f\)) gives exactly zero. Why does SimCLR use large batches?

    Each term of the loss is

    $$-\log\frac{e^{f(x,y^+)}}{\sum_je^{f(x,y_j)}},$$

    minus the log of a probability, so it is at least 0; hence \(\log K-\mathcal L\le\log K\).

    A constant critic gives every candidate probability \(1/K\), so \(\mathcal L=\log K\) and the bound is 0.

    Once the positive is picked out with near certainty, there is nothing more to gain. Larger batches give more negatives, a harder task and a higher ceiling, which is one reason SimCLR used batches of several thousand (and MoCo a queue of negatives).

  6. (Code.) Change the seasonal baseline in the first anomaly code box from the median to the mean of each half-hour of the week, and rerun it. Which days move, and why does the choice matter most for the weeks that contain the anomalies?

    The mean is pulled towards the anomalies themselves. Each half-hour of the week has only about 30 values, so one blizzard night noticeably lowers the mean baseline for that Monday–Tuesday slot, and the blizzard's own residual shrinks. Holidays on the same weekday (Christmas and New Year's Day were both Thursdays in this period) pull down each other's baselines.

    The median ignores a few extreme values, so it keeps the baseline at "normal" demand. The general rule: the model of normal must be fitted robustly, because the training data contain the anomalies.

1. Anomalies in New York subway ridership

question
Which hours and days of unusual subway ridership do unsupervised detectors find, and how many of them match known events (storms, outages, parades, holidays, major games)?
data
MTA Subway Hourly Ridership (New York State open data portal, data.ny.gov), by station complex, from 2020; NOAA daily weather for Central Park; an event timeline built by the students from MTA service alerts, NYC Emergency Management notices and the city's event permits.
method
A robust seasonal baseline per station, isolation forests and LOF on daily station profiles, as in Section 4; precision and recall against the event timeline, with careful treatment of holidays and clock changes; spatial patterns (which stations react to which events).
outcome
A labelled benchmark of ridership anomalies for the largest transit system in the United States, with an honest comparison of detectors, and a list of detections that the timeline does not explain.

2. How many labels does contrastive pretraining save?

question
On a small image set from a specialised domain, how many labelled examples does a supervised network need to match a linear probe on a contrastively pretrained encoder?
data
A domain set with few labels, such as research-grade iNaturalist observations from New York City parks (photographs licensed for reuse, labelled by species) or a public medical image collection from MedMNIST.
method
SimCLR as in Section 6, with augmentations chosen for the domain; linear probes and supervised baselines at 1, 3, 10, 30 and 100% of the labels; repeated over seeds with confidence intervals; an ablation of each augmentation.
outcome
A "label-efficiency curve" for the domain and a statement such as "pretraining is worth \(m\) labels per class", with an analysis of which augmentations matter there.

3. Where does New York complain about noise?

question
Where and when are noise complaints concentrated, and how much do the "hot spots" depend on the bandwidth used to find them?
data
NYC 311 Service Requests (NYC Open Data), complaint types beginning "Noise", with coordinates and timestamps; census-tract population from the American Community Survey for normalisation.
method
Two-dimensional kernel density estimation with bandwidths chosen by rule of thumb and by likelihood cross-validation, separately by hour and season; densities per resident versus per square kilometre; stability of the top hot spots across bandwidths and years.
outcome
Maps of complaint density with an honest account of how much each conclusion depends on the bandwidth, and a comparison of complaint density with population, which separates noisy places from places where people complain.
