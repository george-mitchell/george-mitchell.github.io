Linear regression is the oldest method in this course and still among the most used. It is also the cleanest place to see, with nothing hidden, ideas that return in every later lecture. Fitting a model is minimising a loss. The minimiser has a geometric meaning, here a projection. The same estimate can be read as a maximum-likelihood estimate or as a Bayesian posterior. Penalising complexity trades bias for variance. And a coefficient that predicts well need not tell you what would happen if you intervened.

By the end of this lecture you should be able to derive the least-squares estimate from the normal equations and explain it as an orthogonal projection; state the Gaussian model behind it, compute standard errors and confidence intervals, and state and prove the Gauss–Markov theorem; build non-linear fits from linear models with basis expansions; explain ridge and lasso as penalised least squares, derive the soft-thresholding formula, and say why the lasso produces exact zeros; derive the posterior of Bayesian linear regression and connect it to ridge; and use the omitted-variable-bias formula and the Frisch–Waugh–Lovell theorem to reason about what a regression coefficient means.

1 — Fitting a line

Start with one feature. Given pairs \((x_i,y_i)\), we want the line \(y=b_0+b_1x\) that best predicts \(y\) from \(x\). Following Lecture 1, "best" means smallest empirical risk under squared loss: choose \(b_0,b_1\) to minimise the residual sum of squares

$$\mathrm{RSS}(b_0,b_1)=\sum_{i=1}^n\big(y_i-b_0-b_1x_i\big)^2.$$

Each term is the area of a square drawn on the vertical gap between a point and the line. Try it yourself before any formula.

Fit a line by hand

Twelve Gentoo penguins: flipper length against body mass. Drag the two amber handles to move your line, and drag any point to move the data. The rose squares are your squared residuals; the moss dashed line is the least-squares line.

However you move it, your total area stays above the least-squares value. For one feature the minimiser has a familiar closed form: setting both partial derivatives of RSS to zero gives

$$\hat b_1=\frac{\sum_i(x_i-\bar x)(y_i-\bar y)}{\sum_i(x_i-\bar x)^2},\qquad\hat b_0=\bar y-\hat b_1\bar x.$$

The slope is the projection coefficient of Lecture 2 applied to the centred data, and the line always passes through the point of means \((\bar x,\bar y)\).

history — comets, a lost planet and tall parents

Adrien-Marie Legendre published the method of least squares in 1805, in an appendix to a book on computing the orbits of comets. Carl Friedrich Gauss published it in 1809, claimed to have used it since 1795, and gave it a probabilistic justification (Section 3). Gauss had used it to predict where the dwarf planet Ceres, discovered in January 1801 and then lost behind the Sun, would reappear; astronomers found it that December almost exactly where he said. The word "regression" came later, from Francis Galton's 1886 study of the heights of parents and children: very tall parents tended to have children closer to average height, which he called "regression towards mediocrity". (Galton also founded the eugenics movement; the statistical idea long outlived its original motivation.)

2 — Least squares is projection

With \(p\) features, collect them into an \(n\times p\) design matrix \(X\) (its first column is usually all ones, for the intercept). The model predicts \(\hat y=X\beta\), and least squares minimises \(\|y-X\beta\|^2\). Lecture 2 projected a vector onto a line. Here the predictions \(X\beta\) range over the whole column space of \(X\), a \(p\)-dimensional flat subspace of \(\mathbb R^n\), and the best prediction is the point of that subspace closest to \(y\).

theorem 3.1 — the normal equations

\(\hat\beta\) minimises \(\|y-X\beta\|^2\) if and only if it solves the normal equations

$$X^\top X\,\hat\beta=X^\top y,$$

equivalently, the residual \(r=y-X\hat\beta\) is orthogonal to every column of \(X\).

If \(X\) has full column rank, the solution is unique,

$$\hat\beta=(X^\top X)^{-1}X^\top y,$$

and the fitted values are \(\hat y=Hy\) with the hat matrix

$$H=X(X^\top X)^{-1}X^\top,$$

the orthogonal projection onto the column space of \(X\).

Suppose \(X^\top(y-X\hat\beta)=0\). For any \(\beta\), write

$$y-X\beta=(y-X\hat\beta)+X(\hat\beta-\beta).$$

The two pieces are orthogonal, because the first is orthogonal to every column of \(X\) and the second is a combination of them. So by Pythagoras

$$\|y-X\beta\|^2=\|y-X\hat\beta\|^2+\|X(\hat\beta-\beta)\|^2\ \ge\ \|y-X\hat\beta\|^2.$$

Hence \(\hat\beta\) is a minimiser.

Conversely, at a minimiser the gradient \(-2X^\top(y-X\beta)\) (Lecture 2, Example 2.1) must vanish. If \(X\) has full column rank then \(X^\top X\) is invertible (Exercise 6 of Lecture 2), so the solution is unique, and \(\hat y=X\hat\beta=Hy\).

\(H\) is symmetric and

$$H^2=X(X^\top X)^{-1}X^\top X(X^\top X)^{-1}X^\top=H:$$

an orthogonal projection. \(\square\)

Now all eight California features. In practice nobody inverts \(X^\top X\): numerical libraries solve least squares through a QR or singular value decomposition, which avoids squaring the condition number (Lecture 2, Section 3).

quantum — solving linear systems exponentially faster, with fine print

Least squares reduces to solving a linear system, \(X^\top X\beta=X^\top y\). In 2009 Harrow, Hassidim and Lloyd (HHL) gave a quantum algorithm that "solves" an \(N\times N\) system \(Ax=b\) in time growing only like \(\log N\), and Wiebe, Braun and Lloyd (2012) adapted it to least-squares fitting. This looked like an exponential speed-up for regression. Scott Aaronson's 2015 essay "Read the fine print" lists the catches:

  • the vector \(b\) must be loaded into a quantum state quickly, which needs special data structures;
  • \(A\) must be sparse and well conditioned: the running time grows with the condition number \(\kappa\), the same quantity that slows gradient descent;
  • the output is a quantum state \(|x\rangle\) whose amplitudes are the solution, not the solution itself. Reading out all \(N\) entries takes about \(N\) repetitions, which destroys the speed-up. It helps only when you need a few summary numbers such as \(x^\top Mx\).

Later work found classical randomised algorithms that match some of these quantum algorithms when the data matrix has low rank ("dequantisation", Lecture 16). Quantum linear algebra remains an active field, but the honest summary is that a speed-up for practical regression has not been demonstrated.

3 — The probabilistic view: noise, likelihood and uncertainty

So far least squares is a recipe. Gauss's justification was a model of where the data come from: the truth is linear, and each observation adds independent Gaussian noise,

$$y=X\beta+\varepsilon,\qquad\varepsilon_1,\dots,\varepsilon_n\overset{\text{iid}}{\sim}\mathcal N(0,\sigma^2).$$
proposition 3.2 — least squares is maximum likelihood

Under the Gaussian linear model, the maximum-likelihood estimate of \(\beta\) is the least-squares estimate, and the maximum-likelihood estimate of \(\sigma^2\) is \(\mathrm{RSS}/n\).

The density of \(y_i\) is

$$(2\pi\sigma^2)^{-1/2}\exp\!\big(-(y_i-x_i^\top\beta)^2/2\sigma^2\big),$$

so the log-likelihood is

$$\ell(\beta,\sigma^2)=-\frac n2\log(2\pi\sigma^2)-\frac1{2\sigma^2}\|y-X\beta\|^2.$$

For every fixed \(\sigma^2\), maximising over \(\beta\) means minimising \(\|y-X\beta\|^2\), so \(\hat\beta\) is the least-squares estimate. Setting

$$\partial\ell/\partial\sigma^2=-\frac n{2\sigma^2}+\frac{\mathrm{RSS}}{2\sigma^4}=0$$

gives \(\hat\sigma^2=\mathrm{RSS}/n\). \(\square\)

The model also tells us how much \(\hat\beta\) would vary if we collected new data, which is what a standard error measures.

theorem 3.3 — sampling distribution of the least-squares estimate

Under the linear model with \(\mathbb E[\varepsilon]=0\) and \(\mathrm{Cov}(\varepsilon)=\sigma^2I\), and \(X\) fixed with full column rank:

$$\mathbb E[\hat\beta]=\beta,\qquad\mathrm{Cov}(\hat\beta)=\sigma^2(X^\top X)^{-1},\qquad\mathbb E\Big[\frac{\mathrm{RSS}}{n-p}\Big]=\sigma^2.$$

If the noise is Gaussian,

$$\hat\beta\sim\mathcal N\big(\beta,\sigma^2(X^\top X)^{-1}\big),$$

and \((\hat\beta_j-\beta_j)/\widehat{\mathrm{se}}_j\) has a \(t\) distribution with \(n-p\) degrees of freedom, where

$$\widehat{\mathrm{se}}_j^2=\hat\sigma^2\big[(X^\top X)^{-1}\big]_{jj}.$$

Substitute \(y=X\beta+\varepsilon\):

$$\hat\beta=(X^\top X)^{-1}X^\top(X\beta+\varepsilon)=\beta+(X^\top X)^{-1}X^\top\varepsilon.$$

So \(\mathbb E[\hat\beta]=\beta\), and with \(A=(X^\top X)^{-1}X^\top\),

$$\mathrm{Cov}(\hat\beta)=A\,\sigma^2I\,A^\top=\sigma^2(X^\top X)^{-1}.$$

For the residuals, \(r=(I-H)y=(I-H)\varepsilon\) because \((I-H)X=0\). So \(\mathrm{RSS}=\varepsilon^\top(I-H)\varepsilon\) (using \((I-H)^2=I-H\)), and

$$\mathbb E[\mathrm{RSS}]=\sigma^2\operatorname{tr}(I-H)=\sigma^2\big(n-\operatorname{tr}H\big)=\sigma^2(n-p),$$

since \(\mathbb E[\varepsilon^\top M\varepsilon]=\sigma^2\operatorname{tr}M\) and

$$\operatorname{tr}H=\operatorname{tr}\big((X^\top X)^{-1}X^\top X\big)=p.$$

Dividing by \(n-p\) removes the bias: each fitted parameter uses up one dimension of the noise.

The \(t\) distribution follows from the independence of \(\hat\beta\) and RSS under Gaussian noise (see ESL, Section 3.2). \(\square\)

Least squares has a second justification that does not need Gaussian noise at all.

theorem 3.4 — Gauss–Markov

Under the linear model with \(\mathbb E[\varepsilon]=0\), \(\mathrm{Cov}(\varepsilon)=\sigma^2I\) and \(X\) of full column rank, the least-squares estimate is the best linear unbiased estimator: for any other estimator \(\tilde\beta=Cy\) that is linear in \(y\) and unbiased for every \(\beta\),

$$\mathrm{Cov}(\tilde\beta)-\mathrm{Cov}(\hat\beta)$$

is positive semi-definite.

In particular every coefficient, and every linear combination \(a^\top\beta\), has variance at least as large under \(\tilde\beta\).

Write

$$C=(X^\top X)^{-1}X^\top+D.$$

Unbiasedness for every \(\beta\) means \(\mathbb E[Cy]=CX\beta=\beta\) for all \(\beta\), so \(CX=I\), which forces \(DX=0\). Then

$$\mathrm{Cov}(\tilde\beta)=\sigma^2CC^\top=\sigma^2\Big[(X^\top X)^{-1}+(X^\top X)^{-1}X^\top D^\top+DX(X^\top X)^{-1}+DD^\top\Big]$$ $$=\sigma^2(X^\top X)^{-1}+\sigma^2DD^\top,$$

since the cross terms contain \(DX=0\). \(DD^\top\) is positive semi-definite (\(a^\top DD^\top a=\|D^\top a\|^2\ge0\)), which proves the claim. \(\square\)

"Best" here is within a restricted class: linear and unbiased. Section 5 shows that giving up unbiasedness can reduce the overall error, which is the whole point of regularisation.

4 — Features and basis expansions

"Linear" in linear regression refers to the parameters, not the features. Any fixed transformations \(\phi_1(x),\dots,\phi_m(x)\) of the inputs can be used as columns of the design matrix, and the model \(\hat y=\sum_j\beta_j\phi_j(x)\) is still fitted by least squares. Common choices:

  • transformations of one feature, such as \(\log x\) for skewed quantities like population;
  • polynomials \(x,x^2,x^3,\dots\), as in Lecture 1;
  • interactions \(x_jx_k\), which let the effect of one feature depend on another;
  • indicator variables for categories (a column that is 1 for Gentoo penguins and 0 otherwise);
  • splines: piecewise polynomials joined smoothly at points called knots.
definition 3.1 — cubic spline

Given knots \(\kappa_1<\dots<\kappa_K\), a cubic spline is a function that is a cubic polynomial on each interval between consecutive knots, with the function and its first two derivatives continuous at each knot.

Cubic splines with \(K\) knots form a vector space of dimension \(K+4\) (or \(K+2\) once the outermost knots are treated as boundaries, as scikit-learn does), with a convenient basis of local "bump" functions called B-splines, each non-zero on only a few intervals.

Polynomials are global: changing the data near \(t=50\) ms changes the fit near \(t=5\) ms, which is why the high-degree polynomials of Lecture 1 swung wildly. A spline is local: each B-spline basis function lives on a few neighbouring intervals, so the fit can bend sharply where the data bend and stay flat elsewhere.

5 — Ridge and lasso

When there are many features relative to the number of observations, or when features are strongly correlated, least squares becomes unstable: \(X^\top X\) is nearly singular, coefficients become huge and of opposite signs, and the variance of \(\hat\beta\) (Theorem 3.3) explodes. Regularisation adds a penalty on the size of the coefficients.

definition 3.2 — ridge and lasso

For a penalty level \(\lambda\ge0\),

$$\hat\beta^{\text{ridge}}=\operatorname*{arg\,min}_\beta\ \frac12\|y-X\beta\|^2+\frac\lambda2\|\beta\|_2^2,$$ $$\hat\beta^{\text{lasso}}=\operatorname*{arg\,min}_\beta\ \frac12\|y-X\beta\|^2+\lambda\|\beta\|_1.$$

The intercept is usually left unpenalised, and features are standardised first so that one penalty treats them alike.

Software scales these objectives differently (scikit-learn's Lasso divides the squared error by \(n\)), so always check the convention before comparing values of \(\lambda\).

proposition 3.5 — ridge in closed form

For every \(\lambda>0\),

$$\hat\beta^{\text{ridge}}=(X^\top X+\lambda I)^{-1}X^\top y,$$

which exists even when \(X^\top X\) is singular.

In terms of the SVD \(X=\sum_i\sigma_iu_iv_i^\top\),

$$\hat\beta^{\text{ridge}}=\sum_i\frac{\sigma_i}{\sigma_i^2+\lambda}\,(u_i^\top y)\,v_i,$$

so ridge shrinks the least-squares solution most in the directions where \(X\) has small singular values, the poorly determined directions.

The gradient of the objective is \(-X^\top(y-X\beta)+\lambda\beta\); setting it to zero gives

$$(X^\top X+\lambda I)\beta=X^\top y.$$

The matrix \(X^\top X+\lambda I\) has eigenvalues \(\sigma_i^2+\lambda>0\), so it is invertible, and the objective is strictly convex, so this is the unique minimiser.

In the SVD basis,

$$X^\top X+\lambda I=V(\Sigma^2+\lambda I)V^\top$$

and

$$X^\top y=V\Sigma U^\top y,$$

which gives the sum. Least squares is the case \(\lambda=0\), with factors \(1/\sigma_i\), which blow up when \(\sigma_i\) is small. \(\square\)

The lasso has no closed form in general, but in one special case it does, and the formula shows why the lasso sets coefficients exactly to zero.

theorem 3.6 — soft thresholding (orthonormal design)

Suppose \(X^\top X=I\), and let \(z=X^\top y\) be the least-squares solution. Then

$$\hat\beta^{\text{ridge}}_j=\frac{z_j}{1+\lambda},\qquad\hat\beta^{\text{lasso}}_j=S_\lambda(z_j)=\operatorname{sign}(z_j)\,\max\big(|z_j|-\lambda,\,0\big).$$

Ridge shrinks every coefficient by the same factor and never reaches zero. The lasso subtracts \(\lambda\) from each coefficient's size and sets any coefficient smaller than \(\lambda\) to exactly zero.

Since \(X^\top X=I\), the projection identity from Theorem 3.1 gives

$$\frac12\|y-X\beta\|^2=\frac12\|y-Xz\|^2+\frac12\|\beta-z\|^2.$$

The first term does not depend on \(\beta\), so both problems separate into one-dimensional problems, one per coordinate.

Ridge: minimise \(\tfrac12(b-z_j)^2+\tfrac\lambda2b^2\); setting the derivative to zero gives \(b=z_j/(1+\lambda)\).

Lasso: minimise

$$g(b)=\frac12(b-z_j)^2+\lambda|b|.$$
  • For \(b>0\), \(g'(b)=b-z_j+\lambda\), zero at \(b=z_j-\lambda\), which is positive only if \(z_j>\lambda\).
  • For \(b<0\), \(g'(b)=b-z_j-\lambda\), zero at \(b=z_j+\lambda\), negative only if \(z_j<-\lambda\).
  • If \(|z_j|\le\lambda\), then \(g\) is decreasing for \(b<0\) and increasing for \(b>0\), so the minimum is at \(b=0\).

The three cases together are \(S_\lambda(z_j)\). \(\square\)

spot check 3.5

With an orthonormal design and \(\lambda=1\), a least-squares coefficient of \(-2.5\) becomes what lasso coefficient? (And what happens to a coefficient of 0.7?)

\(S_1(-2.5)=\operatorname{sign}(-2.5)\max(2.5-1,0)\).
\(-1.5\). The coefficient 0.7 is smaller than \(\lambda\), so it becomes exactly 0. Ridge with the same \(\lambda\) would give \(-1.25\) and \(0.35\): smaller, but never zero.

Geometrically, each penalised problem is equivalent to minimising the squared error inside a budget: \(\|\beta\|_2\le t\) for ridge (a disc) or \(\|\beta\|_1\le t\) for the lasso (a diamond). The solution is where the smallest contour of the squared error touches the budget region. A diamond has corners on the axes, and contours often touch there first, which puts the solution exactly on an axis: one coefficient is zero.

spot check 3.6

Why does the lasso produce coefficients that are exactly zero, while ridge does not?

  • The \(\ell_1\) penalty has a corner at zero (it is not differentiable there), so for a whole range of data the optimum sits exactly at the corner; the smooth \(\ell_2\) penalty has no corner.
  • The lasso uses a larger penalty than ridge.
  • The lasso objective is not convex.
  • Ridge does not penalise small coefficients.
Look at the one-dimensional problem in the proof of Theorem 3.6, or at the diamond's corners in the widget.
In one dimension, the lasso's slope just right of zero is \(\lambda-z_j\) and just left of zero is \(-\lambda-z_j\); when \(|z_j|\le\lambda\) these straddle zero, so the minimum is pinned at zero. Ridge's penalty has slope 0 at zero, so it can never hold a coefficient there.
history — from ill-posed problems to compressed sensing

Penalising the size of a solution goes back to Andrey Tikhonov's work in the 1940s and 1960s on "ill-posed" inverse problems in physics, where tiny measurement errors produce huge errors in the solution. Arthur Hoerl and Robert Kennard introduced ridge regression to statistics in 1970, for regressions with highly correlated predictors; the name echoes Hoerl's earlier "ridge analysis" of response surfaces. Robert Tibshirani's lasso (1996) arrived when statisticians began to face data with more variables than observations, such as gene-expression arrays. A decade later Emmanuel Candès, Justin Romberg, Terence Tao and David Donoho showed that \(\ell_1\) penalties can recover sparse signals from far fewer measurements than classical theory required ("compressed sensing"), an idea now used to speed up MRI scans.

6 — Bayesian linear regression

The Bayesian view treats \(\beta\) as uncertain and describes that uncertainty with a probability distribution: a prior before seeing data, updated by Bayes' theorem into a posterior. With a Gaussian prior and Gaussian noise everything stays Gaussian, and the update has a closed form.

theorem 3.7 — posterior and predictive distribution

Let \(y\mid\beta\sim\mathcal N(X\beta,\sigma^2I)\) with \(\sigma^2\) known, and prior \(\beta\sim\mathcal N(0,\tau^2I)\). Then the posterior is \(\beta\mid y\sim\mathcal N(m,S)\) with

$$S=\Big(\frac{X^\top X}{\sigma^2}+\frac{I}{\tau^2}\Big)^{-1},\qquad m=\frac{S\,X^\top y}{\sigma^2}.$$

The posterior mean equals the ridge estimate with \(\lambda=\sigma^2/\tau^2\).

For a new input \(x_*\), the posterior predictive distribution of \(y_*\) is

$$\mathcal N\big(x_*^\top m,\ \sigma^2+x_*^\top Sx_*\big):$$

noise plus parameter uncertainty.

By Bayes' theorem the posterior density is proportional to likelihood times prior, so its log is, up to a constant,

$$-\frac1{2\sigma^2}\|y-X\beta\|^2-\frac1{2\tau^2}\|\beta\|^2=-\frac12\beta^\top\Big(\frac{X^\top X}{\sigma^2}+\frac I{\tau^2}\Big)\beta+\frac{y^\top X\beta}{\sigma^2}+\text{const}.$$

A log-density that is a quadratic in \(\beta\) with matrix \(-\tfrac12S^{-1}\) and linear term \(b^\top\beta\) belongs to \(\mathcal N(Sb,S)\) (complete the square: \(-\tfrac12(\beta-Sb)^\top S^{-1}(\beta-Sb)\) expands to the same thing). Here \(b=X^\top y/\sigma^2\), giving \(m\).

Multiplying \(S^{-1}m=X^\top y/\sigma^2\) by \(\sigma^2\) gives

$$(X^\top X+\frac{\sigma^2}{\tau^2}I)m=X^\top y,$$

the ridge equations.

For prediction, \(y_*=x_*^\top\beta+\varepsilon_*\) is a sum of independent Gaussians:

$$x_*^\top\beta\sim\mathcal N(x_*^\top m,x_*^\top Sx_*)$$

and

$$\varepsilon_*\sim\mathcal N(0,\sigma^2).\qquad\square$$

bayes — priors are penalties, and penalties are priors

Theorem 3.7 is one instance of a general dictionary. Maximising a posterior is minimising \(-\log p(y\mid\beta)-\log p(\beta)\): negative log-likelihood plus a penalty equal to the negative log prior. A Gaussian prior gives the ridge penalty \(\|\beta\|_2^2/2\tau^2\). A Laplace prior, with density proportional to \(e^{-|\beta_j|/b}\), gives the lasso penalty \(\|\beta\|_1/b\), so the lasso is the MAP estimate under a Laplace prior (\(\lambda=\sigma^2/b\)). There is a twist: the full posterior under a Laplace prior puts zero probability on any coefficient being exactly zero, so "the lasso is Bayesian" is true of its mode only. Bayesians who want genuine sparsity use priors with a spike at zero, or heavy-tailed shrinkage priors such as the horseshoe (Carvalho, Polson & Scott, 2010).

7 — Coefficients are not causal effects

A regression coefficient \(\hat\beta_j\) compares units that differ in feature \(j\) but agree in the other features in the model. It is tempting to read it as "the effect of changing \(x_j\)". That reading is justified only when every other cause of \(y\) that is related to \(x_j\) is in the model. The algebra of what happens otherwise is exact.

proposition 3.8 — the omitted-variable-bias formula

Let \(\hat\beta,\hat\gamma\) be the least-squares coefficients from regressing \(y\) on \(X\) and \(Z\) together (the "long" regression), and \(\tilde\beta\) the coefficients from regressing \(y\) on \(X\) alone (the "short" regression).

Let \(\hat\delta=(X^\top X)^{-1}X^\top Z\) be the coefficients from regressing \(Z\) on \(X\). Then, exactly,

$$\tilde\beta=\hat\beta+\hat\delta\,\hat\gamma.$$

Leaving out \(Z\) shifts the coefficient on \(X\) by (how \(Z\) moves with \(X\)) times (how \(y\) moves with \(Z\)).

The long fit gives \(y=X\hat\beta+Z\hat\gamma+e\) with \(X^\top e=0\). Then

$$\tilde\beta=(X^\top X)^{-1}X^\top y=(X^\top X)^{-1}X^\top\big(X\hat\beta+Z\hat\gamma+e\big)$$ $$=\hat\beta+(X^\top X)^{-1}X^\top Z\,\hat\gamma+0.\qquad\square$$

The penguins of Lecture 1 illustrate it. Regress bill depth on bill length, with and without indicators for species:

The next theorem explains what "holding the other features fixed" means operationally, and it is the backbone of modern causal machine learning.

theorem 3.9 — Frisch–Waugh–Lovell

In the regression of \(y\) on \([X_1,X_2]\), the coefficient vector on \(X_2\) equals the coefficient from regressing \(M_1y\) on \(M_1X_2\), where

$$M_1=I-X_1(X_1^\top X_1)^{-1}X_1^\top$$

replaces a vector by its residuals after regressing on \(X_1\).

In words: partial \(X_1\) out of both \(y\) and \(X_2\), then regress residual on residual.

Write the full fit as

$$y=X_1\hat\beta_1+X_2\hat\beta_2+e$$

with \(e\) orthogonal to every column of \(X_1\) and \(X_2\). Apply \(M_1\), which kills \(X_1\) (\(M_1X_1=0\)) and leaves \(e\) unchanged (\(e\) is already orthogonal to \(X_1\)):

$$M_1y=M_1X_2\,\hat\beta_2+e.$$

The columns of \(M_1X_2=X_2-X_1(\cdots)\) lie in the span of \(X_1,X_2\), so \(e\) is orthogonal to them. By Theorem 3.1, \(\hat\beta_2\) therefore satisfies the normal equations for regressing \(M_1y\) on \(M_1X_2\). \(\square\)

Now a question where the answer matters. In the 1970s the National Supported Work (NSW) program gave disadvantaged workers guaranteed jobs and counselling, and assigned eligible applicants to the program or a control group at random. Randomisation makes the difference in mean 1978 earnings an unbiased estimate of the program's effect. Robert LaLonde (1986) asked a sharp question: if we had only observational data, comparing participants with non-participants from a national survey and adjusting by regression, would we recover the experimental answer?

data — the NSW job-training experiment

The Dehejia–Wahba (1999) subsample of LaLonde's (1986) data: 185 men assigned to the program and 260 to the control group, with age, years of education, race and ethnicity indicators, marital status, high-school degree, and real earnings in 1974, 1975 and 1978 (dollars). causal — from Frisch–Waugh–Lovell to double machine learning

Regression adjustment estimates a causal effect only under two assumptions: no unmeasured confounding (every variable that affects both treatment and outcome is in the model) and a correct functional form for how the covariates enter. The first is a claim about the world that no amount of data can confirm; the second is where machine learning helps. FWL suggests a recipe: predict the treatment from covariates, predict the outcome from covariates, and regress residual on residual. Double/debiased machine learning (Chernozhukov et al., 2018) does exactly this with flexible models (lasso, random forests, boosting, neural networks) for the two predictions, fitted on separate folds of the data, and shows that the final residual-on-residual coefficient is approximately normal with valid confidence intervals. Lecture 4 adds the propensity score, Lecture 8 causal forests, and Lecture 20 the question of how to find the confounders in the first place.

spot check 3.9

Why is the naive observational estimate (−$8,498) so far from the experimental one (+$1,794)?

  • Program participants differed systematically from survey respondents before the program (much lower prior earnings), and those differences, not the program, drive most of the gap in 1978 earnings.
  • The program genuinely harmed participants who joined voluntarily.
  • The survey sample is too small.
  • Differences in means are always biased.
Compare 1975 earnings of the two groups, or think about who signs up for a job-training program for the disadvantaged.
This is confounding: the omitted-variable-bias formula with "prior earnings" as \(Z\). Randomisation breaks the link between treatment and every pre-existing difference at once, measured or not; regression can only adjust for what is measured.
big picture — linear regression in 2026

Linear regression is everywhere in modern data science, often unannounced. Online experiments at technology companies use regression adjustment on pre-experiment behaviour to cut the variance of A/B-test estimates (the "CUPED" method of Deng et al., 2013), which lets them run shorter experiments. Interpretability research reads what a neural network has learned by fitting linear "probes" from its internal activations to properties of the input. The final layer of almost every neural network is a linear model on learned features. And the scaling laws that guide how large language models are trained (Kaplan et al., 2020) are straight lines fitted to log-loss against log-compute.

8 — Summary, exercises, projects, reading

summary
  • Least squares minimises \(\|y-X\beta\|^2\). The solution satisfies the normal equations \(X^\top X\hat\beta=X^\top y\) and makes the residual orthogonal to every column: \(\hat y\) is the orthogonal projection of \(y\) onto the column space (Theorem 3.1).
  • Under Gaussian noise, least squares is maximum likelihood (Proposition 3.2); \(\hat\beta\) is unbiased with covariance \(\sigma^2(X^\top X)^{-1}\), and \(\mathrm{RSS}/(n-p)\) estimates \(\sigma^2\) (Theorem 3.3). Among linear unbiased estimators it has the smallest variance (Gauss–Markov, Theorem 3.4).
  • Models are linear in parameters, not features: transformations, interactions, indicators and splines are all least squares.
  • Ridge \((X^\top X+\lambda I)^{-1}X^\top y\) shrinks poorly determined directions; the lasso soft-thresholds and sets coefficients exactly to zero (Theorem 3.6). Penalties pay off when features are many or correlated, and with flexible bases.
  • With a Gaussian prior, the posterior is Gaussian with mean equal to the ridge estimate at \(\lambda=\sigma^2/\tau^2\) (Theorem 3.7); predictions carry both noise and parameter uncertainty.
  • Coefficients are partial comparisons. Omitting a correlated cause shifts them by \(\hat\delta\hat\gamma\) (Proposition 3.8); "holding others fixed" means residual-on-residual regression (Frisch–Waugh–Lovell, Theorem 3.9). Causal readings need assumptions that regression alone cannot check.

    Symmetry and idempotence are in the proof of Theorem 3.1. \(\operatorname{tr}H=\operatorname{tr}\big(X(X^\top X)^{-1}X^\top\big)=\operatorname{tr}\big((X^\top X)^{-1}X^\top X\big)=\operatorname{tr}I_p=p\), using \(\operatorname{tr}(AB)=\operatorname{tr}(BA)\). For a projection the eigenvalues are 0 and 1, so the trace counts the dimension of the space projected onto. For ridge, \(\operatorname{tr}\big(X(X^\top X+\lambda I)^{-1}X^\top\big)=\sum_i\sigma_i^2/(\sigma_i^2+\lambda)\) is called the effective degrees of freedom: it falls smoothly from \(p\) to 0 as \(\lambda\) grows.

  3. Show that if the design matrix contains a column of ones, the least-squares residuals sum to zero and the fitted line passes through \((\bar x,\bar y)\).

    The normal equations say \(X^\top r=0\); the row corresponding to the column of ones reads \(\sum_ir_i=0\). Dividing \(\sum_i(y_i-\hat b_0-\hat b_1x_i)=0\) by \(n\) gives \(\bar y=\hat b_0+\hat b_1\bar x\).

  4. (Ridge as bias for variance.) In one dimension with orthonormal design, the least-squares estimate is \(z\sim\mathcal N(\beta,\sigma^2)\) and ridge returns \(cz\) with \(c=1/(1+\lambda)\). Show that its mean squared error is \((1-c)^2\beta^2+c^2\sigma^2\), and find the best \(c\). Why can ridge beat the Gauss–Markov "best" estimator?

    \(\mathbb E[(cz-\beta)^2]=(c\beta-\beta)^2+c^2\sigma^2\): squared bias plus variance (Lecture 1, Proposition 1.2). Minimising over \(c\) gives \(c^*=\beta^2/(\beta^2+\sigma^2)\), always less than 1. Gauss–Markov only compares unbiased estimators; ridge is biased, and accepting a little bias buys a larger drop in variance, especially when \(\beta\) is small relative to the noise.

  5. With an orthonormal design and \(\lambda=1\), the least-squares coefficients are \((3,-0.5,1.2)\). Compute the ridge and lasso coefficients (Definition 3.2 conventions).

    Ridge divides by \(1+\lambda=2\): \((1.5,-0.25,0.6)\). Lasso subtracts 1 from each size and floors at zero: \((2,0,0.2)\). Ridge keeps all three; the lasso drops the second.

  6. For the model \(y_i=wx_i+\varepsilon_i\) with \(\sigma^2=1\), prior \(w\sim\mathcal N(0,1)\), and data \(x=(1,2,2)\), \(y=(1,3,2)\), compute the posterior mean and variance and compare with least squares.

    \(\sum x_i^2=9\), \(\sum x_iy_i=11\). Posterior variance \(1/(9+1)=0.1\), posterior mean \(11/10=1.1\). Least squares gives \(11/9\approx1.222\): the prior pulls the estimate towards 0, by the factor \(9/10\).

  7. A study regresses wages on years of schooling and finds a positive coefficient. Ability, which is not measured, raises both schooling and wages. Use the omitted-variable-bias formula to predict the direction of the bias.

    With \(Z\) = ability: \(\hat\delta>0\) (more able people get more schooling) and \(\hat\gamma>0\) (ability raises wages), so \(\tilde\beta=\hat\beta+\hat\delta\hat\gamma\) is biased upwards: the schooling coefficient overstates the return to schooling. This is the classic "ability bias" argument in labour economics, and one reason economists look for natural experiments.

  8. Verify Frisch–Waugh–Lovell numerically on California housing: regress MedInc on the other seven features plus an intercept, regress Value on the same, and regress residual on residual. Compare with the MedInc coefficient in the code box of Section 2.

    The residual-on-residual slope is 0.4325, equal to the multiple-regression coefficient. (Regressing raw Value on the MedInc residuals gives the same slope too, because the MedInc residuals are orthogonal to the other columns; but the residuals of that regression are not the model's residuals, so its standard errors would be wrong.)

  9. Two features are exact copies, \(x_1=x_2\). Describe the least-squares, ridge and lasso solutions.

    Least squares is not unique: only \(\beta_1+\beta_2\) is determined. Ridge, minimising \(\beta_1^2+\beta_2^2\) for a fixed sum, splits the weight equally. The lasso's \(|\beta_1|+|\beta_2|\) is the same for any split with both coefficients of the same sign, so the lasso solution is not unique either, and algorithms return one arbitrary split (often putting all the weight on one copy). This is why the lasso "picks one of a correlated group", and why the elastic net (a mix of both penalties) was invented.

  10. Explain why the posterior predictive band in the Bayesian widget is narrowest near the middle of the data and widens towards the edges. Write the predictive variance for the model \(y=b_0+b_1x\) with a flat prior (\(\tau\to\infty\)) and centred \(x\).

    With \(\tau\to\infty\), \(S=\sigma^2(X^\top X)^{-1}\). For centred \(x\), \(X^\top X=\operatorname{diag}(n,\sum x_i^2)\), so the predictive variance at \(x_*\) is \(\sigma^2\big(1+\tfrac1n+\tfrac{x_*^2}{\sum x_i^2}\big)\). The intercept is uncertain by \(\sigma^2/n\) everywhere; the slope's uncertainty contributes a term growing with \(x_*^2\), the squared distance from the centre of the data.
