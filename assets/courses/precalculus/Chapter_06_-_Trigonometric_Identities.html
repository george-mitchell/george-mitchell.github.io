Chapter 6
Trigonometric identities

Lectures 22–23  |  many names for the same quantity

In this chapter

An identity is an equation that is true for every value of the variable. \(x^{2}-1=(x-1)(x+1)\) is one; \(x^{2}-1=0\) is not, since it only holds at two particular values. The distinction matters: an equation is something you solve, an identity is something you use.

Trigonometry has an unusual number of identities, and there is a reason. Sine and cosine are the coordinates of a point on a circle, and a circle is an extremely symmetric object — you can reflect it in either axis, rotate it, or turn it inside out, and it lands on itself. Every one of those symmetries becomes an equation. That is all an identity is: a symmetry of the circle, written in algebra.

Lecture 22 — Pythagorean identities and the symmetry of the circle

The identity you already have

The point \((\cos t,\sin t)\) lies on the unit circle, whose equation is \(x^{2}+y^{2}=1\). Substituting immediately gives the most-used identity in mathematics.

the Pythagorean identities $$\cos^{2}t+\sin^{2}t=1$$

Dividing through by \(\cos^{2}t\) gives \(1+\tan^{2}t=\sec^{2}t\); dividing instead by \(\sin^{2}t\) gives \(1+\cot^{2}t=\csc^{2}t\). The three are one fact in three costumes, and the second two are not worth memorising separately — derive them in the margin when you need them.

The notation \(\cos^{2}t\) means \((\cos t)^{2}\), not \(\cos(t^{2})\). It is a bad convention and it is universal.

the relations that come free

Reciprocal: \(\csc t=\dfrac{1}{\sin t}\), \(\sec t=\dfrac{1}{\cos t}\), \(\cot t=\dfrac{1}{\tan t}\).

Quotient: \(\tan t=\dfrac{\sin t}{\cos t}\), \(\cot t=\dfrac{\cos t}{\sin t}\).

Even and odd: \(\cos(-t)=\cos t\) and \(\sin(-t)=-\sin t\), so \(\tan(-t)=-\tan t\).

Cofunction: \(\sin\!\left(\tfrac{\pi}{2}-t\right)=\cos t\) and \(\cos\!\left(\tfrac{\pi}{2}-t\right)=\sin t\).

The cofunction relations are the two acute angles of a right triangle talking to each other: they add to \(\tfrac{\pi}{2}\), and what is opposite one is adjacent to the other. That is the whole proof, and it is why "co" appears in cosine, cotangent and cosecant — each is the function of the complementary angle.

Simplifying, and proving

Two different jobs use these identities, and it helps to know which one you are doing.

example 6.1 — simplifying

Simplify \(\dfrac{\sin t}{\csc t}+\dfrac{\cos t}{\sec t}\) and \(\dfrac{\sec t-\cos t}{\tan t}\).

For the first, the reciprocals collapse: \(\dfrac{\sin t}{1/\sin t}=\sin^{2}t\), and likewise the second term is \(\cos^{2}t\). So the whole thing is \(\sin^{2}t+\cos^{2}t=1\).

For the second, put the numerator over a common denominator: $$\frac{\frac{1}{\cos t}-\cos t}{\tan t}=\frac{\frac{1-\cos^{2}t}{\cos t}}{\frac{\sin t}{\cos t}}=\frac{\sin^{2}t}{\cos t}\cdot\frac{\cos t}{\sin t}=\sin t.$$

The working rule when an expression looks hopeless: write everything in terms of sine and cosine. Almost every simplification in this lecture yields to that one move followed by ordinary fraction algebra from Chapter 0.

example 6.2 — proving an identity

Prove that \(\dfrac{\cos t}{1-\sin t}=\dfrac{1+\sin t}{\cos t}\).

Start on the left and multiply top and bottom by \(1+\sin t\) — the conjugate trick from Chapter 0, chosen because it will create a difference of squares: $$\frac{\cos t}{1-\sin t}\cdot\frac{1+\sin t}{1+\sin t}=\frac{\cos t(1+\sin t)}{1-\sin^{2}t}=\frac{\cos t(1+\sin t)}{\cos^{2}t}=\frac{1+\sin t}{\cos t}.$$

Two rules of etiquette for proofs like this. Work on one side only and transform it until it becomes the other; do not treat the identity as an equation and operate on both sides, since that assumes what you are trying to show. And when stuck, look for the move that manufactures \(1-\sin^{2}t\) or \(1-\cos^{2}t\), because that is the doorway to the Pythagorean identity.

Solving trigonometric equations

Now the other job. Here the equation is not an identity, and the question is which values of \(t\) make it true. Two things go wrong for students, and both are avoidable.

the two-step method
  1. Use identities and algebra to reduce to something of the form \(\sin t=k\), \(\cos t=k\) or \(\tan t=k\) — factoring if necessary.
  2. Find all solutions in one full turn, using the symmetry of the circle, then add multiples of the period.

Your calculator's \(\sin^{-1}\) returns one answer. The circle has two points at most heights, and the second one is the one everybody forgets.

✓ Correct. Two: one in quadrant I and its mirror image in quadrant II. The calculator only reports the first.

example 6.3 — find every solution

Solve \(2\sin^{2}t-\sin t-1=0\) for \(0\le t<2\pi\).

It is a quadratic in \(\sin t\). Writing \(u=\sin t\): \(2u^{2}-u-1=(2u+1)(u-1)=0\), so \(\sin t=-\tfrac12\) or \(\sin t=1\).

\(\sin t=1\) at \(t=\tfrac{\pi}{2}\) only — the top of the circle is a single point.

\(\sin t=-\tfrac12\) means a height of \(-\tfrac12\), which happens at two points, in the third and fourth quadrants: \(t=\tfrac{7\pi}{6}\) and \(t=\tfrac{11\pi}{6}\).

So there are three solutions: \(\tfrac{\pi}{2},\ \tfrac{7\pi}{6},\ \tfrac{11\pi}{6}\). Drawing the horizontal line \(y=-\tfrac12\) across the circle and counting crossings is the reliable way to be sure you have them all.

example 6.4 — a modelling equation

The depth of water in a harbour is \(h(t)=2.4\cos\!\left(\tfrac{\pi}{6}t\right)+5.6\) metres, \(t\) hours after midnight. A ship needs 4.5 m of water. Between what times is it safe to enter, in the first twelve hours?

Safe when \(2.4\cos\!\left(\tfrac{\pi}{6}t\right)+5.6\ge4.5\), that is \(\cos\!\left(\tfrac{\pi}{6}t\right)\ge-0.4583\).

The cosine drops below \(-0.4583\) between the two angles whose cosine equals it. \(\cos^{-1}(-0.4583)=2.0466\) rad, and by symmetry the other is \(2\pi-2.0466=4.2366\) rad.

So the unsafe stretch is \(2.0466\le\tfrac{\pi}{6}t\le4.2366\), giving \(3.91\le t\le8.09\) hours.

The ship can enter before about 3:55 a.m. or after about 8:05 a.m. Notice that the answer came out as an interval, and that the symmetry of the circle — not the calculator — supplied the second endpoint.

Lecture 23 — Addition formulas, double angles, and substitution

Everything so far has related one angle to itself. The formulas in this lecture relate two angles, and they are what makes the whole subject usable: they are why a mixture of waves can be rewritten as a single wave, why two speakers can cancel each other in a particular seat of a room, and why the Fourier method of Lecture 18 works at all.

The first thing to be clear about is what is not true. \(\sin(\alpha+\beta)\) is not \(\sin\alpha+\sin\beta\). Test it with \(\alpha=\beta=\tfrac{\pi}{2}\): the left side is \(\sin\pi=0\) and the right side is \(2\). Sine is not a function that distributes over addition, and neither is any other interesting function.

the addition and subtraction formulas $$\sin(\alpha+\beta)=\sin\alpha\cos\beta+\cos\alpha\sin\beta$$ $$\cos(\alpha+\beta)=\cos\alpha\cos\beta-\sin\alpha\sin\beta$$

Replacing \(\beta\) by \(-\beta\) and using the even-odd relations flips the middle signs:

$$\sin(\alpha-\beta)=\sin\alpha\cos\beta-\cos\alpha\sin\beta,\qquad \cos(\alpha-\beta)=\cos\alpha\cos\beta+\sin\alpha\sin\beta$$

Note the cosine formulas carry the opposite sign to the one in the bracket. That trap is worth a moment's care every single time.

example 6.5 — exact values for new angles

Find the exact value of \(\cos15^{\circ}\) and of \(\sin75^{\circ}\).

\(15^{\circ}=45^{\circ}-30^{\circ}\), and both of those come from the special triangles: $$\cos15^{\circ}=\cos45^{\circ}\cos30^{\circ}+\sin45^{\circ}\sin30^{\circ} =\frac{\sqrt2}{2}\cdot\frac{\sqrt3}{2}+\frac{\sqrt2}{2}\cdot\frac12=\frac{\sqrt6+\sqrt2}{4}.$$ That is \(0.9659\), which a calculator confirms.

For \(75^{\circ}=45^{\circ}+30^{\circ}\): \(\sin75^{\circ}=\dfrac{\sqrt2}{2}\cdot\dfrac{\sqrt3}{2}+\dfrac{\sqrt2}{2}\cdot\dfrac12=\dfrac{\sqrt6+\sqrt2}{4}\) as well — the same number, as the cofunction relation promised, since \(75^{\circ}\) and \(15^{\circ}\) are complementary.

Double angles, from nothing new

Set \(\beta=\alpha\) in the addition formulas and three more identities fall out for free.

double-angle formulas $$\sin2\alpha=2\sin\alpha\cos\alpha$$ $$\cos2\alpha=\cos^{2}\alpha-\sin^{2}\alpha=2\cos^{2}\alpha-1=1-2\sin^{2}\alpha$$

The three versions of the cosine formula come from substituting \(\sin^{2}=1-\cos^{2}\) or the other way round. Which one to use depends on what you want left standing at the end.

example 6.6 — the best angle to throw at

A projectile launched at speed \(v\) and angle \(\theta\) travels a horizontal distance \(R=\dfrac{v^{2}}{g}\,2\sin\theta\cos\theta\). Rewrite this with a double angle and find the angle that throws farthest.

\(2\sin\theta\cos\theta\) is exactly \(\sin2\theta\), so $$R=\frac{v^{2}}{g}\sin2\theta.$$

Now the answer is visible without any calculation at all. \(\sin\) is largest when its input is \(90^{\circ}\), so \(2\theta=90^{\circ}\) and \(\theta=45^{\circ}\).

It also shows something less obvious: since \(\sin2\theta=\sin(180^{\circ}-2\theta)\), the angles \(\theta\) and \(90^{\circ}-\theta\) give the same range. A throw at \(30^{\circ}\) and a throw at \(60^{\circ}\) land in the same place, one flat and fast, the other high and slow. Rewriting the formula did the work that optimisation would otherwise have had to do.

Sums into products, and why rooms have quiet spots

sum to product $$\sin A+\sin B=2\sin\!\left(\frac{A+B}{2}\right)\cos\!\left(\frac{A-B}{2}\right)$$ $$\cos A+\cos B=2\cos\!\left(\frac{A+B}{2}\right)\cos\!\left(\frac{A-B}{2}\right)$$

Each follows by writing \(A=\tfrac{A+B}{2}+\tfrac{A-B}{2}\) and \(B=\tfrac{A+B}{2}-\tfrac{A-B}{2}\), applying the addition formulas, and adding.

idea — this is what a beat is

Lecture 16 showed two nearby frequencies producing a throb and left the explanation for later. Here it is. Adding two waves of frequencies \(f_1\) and \(f_2\) gives, by the first formula above, a wave at the average frequency \(\tfrac{f_1+f_2}{2}\) multiplied by a slow cosine at half the difference.

So the ear hears a single tone at the average pitch, whose loudness is being swelled and squashed by the slow factor. When the difference is small, that factor takes seconds to complete a cycle — and the beat is audible. The guitar tuner of Lecture 16 is listening to an identity.

The same formula explains the cartoon at the top of this lecture. Two speakers playing the same note reach a given seat with different delays, which is to say different phases. Where the phases differ by half a cycle, the sum-to-product factor is zero, and the seat is silent. Concert halls are designed to avoid this and noise-cancelling headphones are designed to cause it.

example 6.7 — one wave instead of two

Write \(3\sin x+4\cos x\) as a single sine wave \(A\sin(x+\varphi)\).

Expanding the target with the addition formula gives \(A\sin x\cos\varphi+A\cos x\sin\varphi\). Matching coefficients: $$A\cos\varphi=3,\qquad A\sin\varphi=4.$$

Squaring and adding kills \(\varphi\): \(A^{2}(\cos^{2}\varphi+\sin^{2}\varphi)=9+16=25\), so \(A=5\). Dividing gives \(\tan\varphi=\tfrac43\), so \(\varphi=0.927\) rad.

$$3\sin x+4\cos x=5\sin(x+0.927).$$

This is worth more than it looks. The left side is a sum of two things and its maximum is not obvious; the right side is a single wave, so its amplitude is 5, its maximum is 5, and you can see the whole of its behaviour at a glance. Note also that \(A\) came from \(\sqrt{3^{2}+4^{2}}\) — the same Pythagorean combination that gave the amplitude of a Fourier component in Lecture 18, for exactly the same reason.

Substitution: making a root disappear

One last use, and it is a preview. Expressions containing \(\sqrt{1-x^{2}}\) are awkward; the root refuses to simplify and the algebra goes nowhere. But the Pythagorean identity says \(1-\sin^{2}\theta=\cos^{2}\theta\), which is a perfect square. So substitute \(x=\sin\theta\) and the root evaporates: $$\sqrt{1-x^{2}}=\sqrt{1-\sin^{2}\theta}=\cos\theta.$$

spot check

With \(x=\sin\theta\) and \(\theta\) between \(-\tfrac{\pi}{2}\) and \(\tfrac{\pi}{2}\): if \(x=0.6\), what is \(\sqrt{1-x^{2}}\)?

decimals, fractions, pi and sqrt( ) are all fine

✓ Correct. \(\sqrt{1-0.36}=0.8\): the point \((0.8,0.6)\) is on the circle.
Not quite. It equals \(\cos\theta\), the width of the point at height 0.6 on the unit circle.
Hmm. That doesn’t look like a number I can read. A decimal like 0.75, a fraction like 3/4, or forms like 2pi/3 and sqrt(2) all work.
example 6.8 — the root evaporates

Simplify \(\dfrac{x}{\sqrt{1-x^{2}}}\) using the substitution \(x=\sin\theta\), and say what geometric object the substitution corresponds to.

With \(x=\sin\theta\) the expression becomes \(\dfrac{\sin\theta}{\cos\theta}=\tan\theta\).

Geometrically the substitution puts \(x\) on the unit circle: it says "let \(x\) be the height of a point at angle \(\theta\)", and then \(\sqrt{1-x^{2}}\) is automatically the width. The awkward root was only awkward because we were insisting on coordinates when the natural description was an angle.

In calculus this move is called trigonometric substitution, and it turns a whole family of otherwise impossible problems into routine ones. You will recognise it when it arrives: it is this, with one extra step.

Where this chapter leaves you

Identities are the circle's symmetry written down. Reflecting a point in an axis changes the sign of one coordinate, and that is a whole family of them; the equation of the circle itself is the Pythagorean identity; and building one triangle on another gives the addition formulas, from which double angles, sums-to-products and the single-wave form all follow without further ideas.

What they buy you is freedom of expression: the same quantity can be written in whichever form makes the next step easy. That is why a beat has an explanation, why the best throwing angle is obvious once the formula is rewritten, and why a root that blocked all progress can be made to vanish by choosing to describe a number as an angle.

Chapter 7 leaves the circle for the plane itself, where a vector is a journey, a matrix is a function that moves every point at once, and the rotation you have been doing by hand all chapter becomes a single object you can multiply.
