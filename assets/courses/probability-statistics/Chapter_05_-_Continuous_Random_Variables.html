Chapter 5
Continuous random variables

Lectures 12–14 · measuring what happens: densities, the Gaussian curve, waiting times and the other shapes of chance

Chapter 4 was about counting: how many passengers, how many fakes, how many calls. This chapter is about measuring: how tall, how long, how heavy, how concentrated. A person's height, the time until the next earthquake, the alcohol concentration in a blood sample, the age of a bone found at a crime scene. These quantities don't jump from one value to the next; they can take any value in an interval, and that changes the mathematics in a small but important way.

Here is the puzzle that drives the chapter. What is the probability that a randomly chosen man is exactly 70 inches tall? Not 70.1, not 69.99, but 70.000000... exactly. There are infinitely many possible heights, so the answer has to be zero. Yet every man has some height. How can every individual height have probability zero, when the probabilities must add up to 1? The answer is to stop asking about single values and start asking about intervals, and to measure probability as an area under a curve.

Here is the route through the chapter.

  • Sections 5.1 and 5.2 replace the PMF with a density curve, and redo expected value and variance with integrals instead of sums.
  • Section 5.3 meets the most famous curve in science, the Gaussian distribution, using Francis Galton's measurements of 481 Victorian men.
  • Section 5.4 is about waiting times: the exponential and gamma distributions, from mining disasters to radiocarbon dating.
  • Sections 5.5 and 5.6 meet the beta, log-Gaussian and other distributions, and Section 5.7 puts them side by side.

The calculus you need is in Section 0.4: areas under curves are integrals.

Densities serve the two goals of the course just as PMFs did. For prediction, a density fitted to past measurements tells us how often to expect future values in any range: how many sons will be over six feet, how long until the next mine disaster, how often an honest driver will be flagged by a breathalyser. For inference, we reverse the question: what does a measurement tell us about the hidden quantity behind it, such as a driver's true alcohol level, the age of a body, or a player's true shooting percentage? Along the way, watch for a third question that underlies both: has the mechanism that produced the data stayed the same? A model fitted to one era (the coal mines before 1890) can predict badly in another.

Think About It: Before You Start

A breathalyser reads 0.081 g/dL, just over the legal driving limit of 0.08. The device's readings are known to vary by a few thousandths around the true value. Should the driver be convicted? What would you need to know about the device to decide? Hold on to your answer: Example 5.7 works it out.

You'd want to know how big the measurement error typically is, and how likely a reading of 0.081 or more would be if the driver's true level were just below 0.08. If readings routinely vary by 0.005 either way, a reading of 0.081 is entirely consistent with a true level under the limit. Many jurisdictions deal with this by requiring two readings, or by subtracting a margin for error, and the size of that margin comes straight from the Gaussian distribution of measurement errors in Section 5.3.

5.1 From histograms to densities

Galton's heights

In the 1880s the English scientist Francis Galton (a half-cousin of Charles Darwin) collected the heights of nearly 1,000 adult children and their parents, to study how height is inherited. His data still survive, and they're a good place to see how a continuous distribution behaves.

Example 5.1: Galton's Sons ★ lecture example

From histogram to density curve

Probability density functions

The histogram shows the key idea. For a measurement, the probability of an interval is the area of the histogram over that interval. In the limit, the probability that X falls between a and b is the area under the density curve from a to b.

Definition: Continuous Random Variable and Density

A random variable X is continuous if there is a function f(x), called its probability density function (PDF, or just density), such that for all a≤b, Pr(a≤X≤b)=∫abf(x)dx. Every density satisfies

  1. f(x)≥0 for all x, and
  2. ∫−∞∞f(x)dx=1: the total area is 1.

Three consequences follow straight away, and each is a common source of confusion.

  • Single values have probability zero. Pr(X=a)=∫aaf(x)dx=0. So for a continuous random variable, Pr(a≤X≤b)=Pr(a<X<b): including the endpoints or not makes no difference, unlike the discrete case in Chapter 4.
  • A density is not a probability. f(x) can be bigger than 1 (a density on an interval of length 0.1 must reach at least 10). What it measures is probability per unit of x: for a tiny interval of width Δ, Pr(x≤X≤x+Δ)≈f(x)Δ. A taller curve means more probability packed into each inch.
  • Probability zero doesn't mean impossible. Every height has probability zero, yet one of them happens. (The dart in Section 3.3 made the same point.)

Example 5.2: Where Does the Dart Land? ★ lecture example

A dart lands at a uniformly random point on a round board of radius 1 (say, 1 foot). Let R be its distance from the centre. (a) Show that Pr(R≤r)=r2 for 0≤r≤1. (b) Find the density of R and check that it's a valid density. (c) Find the probability that the dart lands within half a foot of the centre, and the probability it lands between 0.5 and 0.6 feet out.

(a) Probability is area. "Uniformly random point" means the probability of landing in any region is that region's share of the board's area. The dart lands within r of the centre exactly when it lands in the circle of radius r, so Pr(R≤r)=area of circle of radius rarea of the board=πr2π⋅12=r2.

(b) The density is the rate at which this probability grows, its derivative: f(r)=ddrr2=2r,0≤r≤1, and f(r)=0 outside [0,1]. It's never negative, and its total area is ∫012rdr=[r2]01=1. Valid.

(c) Within half a foot: Pr(R≤0.5)=0.52=0.25. Between 0.5 and 0.6: Pr(0.5≤R≤0.6)=∫0.50.62rdr=0.62−0.52=0.36−0.25=0.11.

The surprise. Only a quarter of random darts land within half the radius of the centre. The density 2r rises towards the edge, because there is more board far out: a thin ring at distance r has circumference 2πr, so outer rings hold more area. Throw some darts:

The CDF, again

The cumulative distribution function F(x)=Pr(X≤x) is defined exactly as in Chapter 4. For a continuous random variable it is the area under the density to the left of x: F(x)=∫−∞xf(t)dt. By the Fundamental Theorem of Calculus, the density is the derivative of the CDF, f(x)=F′(x), which is how we got 2r from r2 in Example 5.2. Instead of a staircase, the CDF of a continuous random variable is a smooth curve rising from 0 to 1, and it is steepest where the density is highest.

The CDF is the area so far

Slide x from left to right. The shaded area under the density (top) is Pr(X ≤ x). The same number is the height of the CDF (bottom). Where the density is tall, the area grows fast and the CDF is steep; where the density is near zero, the CDF is flat.

The CDF also gives quantiles. The p-quantile of a continuous random variable is the value q with F(q)=p: a fraction p of the probability lies to its left. The median is the 0.5-quantile.

Example 5.3: The Median Dart ★ lecture example

For the dart in Example 5.2, find the median distance from the centre and the 90th percentile.

Solve F(q)=p with F(r)=r2: q2=p, so q=p.

  • Median: q=0.5≈0.707 feet. Half of all darts land more than 0.7 feet from the centre of a 1-foot board.
  • 90th percentile: q=0.9≈0.949 feet. One dart in ten lands in the outermost 5% of the radius.

So a "typical" random dart is much nearer the rim than the centre, which is why darts players aim, and why a random scatter of shots on a target looks emptier in the middle than you'd expect.

Think About It: How Precise Is a Measurement?

A forensic report says a footprint is "28.0 cm long". The probability that a true length is exactly 28.0 cm is zero, so what does this report actually tell a jury? How would you rewrite it to be honest about uncertainty?

It really means the length lies in an interval: perhaps 27.95 to 28.05 cm if it was rounded to the nearest millimetre, or wider still once the ruler's error and the stretch of the impression are counted. An honest report gives the interval or a measurement uncertainty: "28.0 cm ± 0.3 cm (95%)", say. This is exactly the shift this section makes: continuous quantities are described by probabilities of intervals, not of single values. Chapter 8 shows how to compute such intervals.

Key Takeaways

  • A continuous random variable has a density f(x)≥0 with total area 1; probabilities are areas: Pr(a≤X≤b)=∫abf(x)dx.
  • Single values have probability zero, so ≤ and < don't matter. A density is probability per unit, and can exceed 1.
  • F(x)=∫−∞xf(t)dt and f=F′. The p-quantile solves F(q)=p.

5.2 Expected value, variance and the uniform distribution

Every idea from Chapter 4 carries over, with sums replaced by integrals and the PMF replaced by the density.

Definition: Expected Value and Variance of a Continuous Random Variable

If X has density f, its expected value and variance are E[X]=∫−∞∞xf(x)dx,Var[X]=∫−∞∞(x−μ)2f(x)dx, where μ=E[X] (provided the integral of |x|f(x) is finite). More generally, E[g(X)]=∫g(x)f(x)dx.

All the rules carry over unchanged, with the same proofs (sums become integrals): linearity E[aX+b]=aE[X]+b; the shortcut Var[X]=E[X2]−(E[X])2; and Var[aX+b]=a2Var[X]. The interpretations do too: E[X] is the long-run average and the balance point of the density, and the standard deviation is a typical distance from the mean.

Example 5.4: The Average Dart ★ lecture example

For the dart distance R with density f(r)=2r on [0,1], find E[R], Var[R] and SD[R]. Compare the mean with the median from Example 5.3.

Mean. E[R]=∫01r⋅2rdr=∫012r2dr=[2r33]01=23≈0.667.

Variance, by the shortcut formula. First E[R2]=∫01r2⋅2rdr=∫012r3dr=[r42]01=12, so Var[R]=12−(23)2=12−49=118,SD[R]=1/18≈0.236.

The uniform distribution

The simplest continuous distribution spreads probability evenly over an interval.

Definition: Uniform Distribution

X has the uniform distribution on [a,b], written X∼Unif(a,b), if its density is constant on that interval: f(x)=1b−a,a≤x≤b, and 0 elsewhere. Then E[X]=a+b2 and Var[X]=(b−a)212.

The total area is a rectangle of width b−a and height 1b−a, which is 1. For the mean, ∫abxb−adx=b2−a22(b−a)=a+b2, the midpoint, as symmetry suggests. For the variance, E[X2]=∫abx2b−adx=b3−a33(b−a)=a2+ab+b23, and subtracting (a+b2)2 leaves (b−a)212 after a little algebra. ◼∎

For a uniform distribution, probabilities are just lengths: Pr(c≤X≤d)=d−cb−a for any [c,d] inside [a,b].

Example 5.5: Waiting for the Bus ★ lecture example

A bus comes every 12 minutes, like clockwork, and you arrive at the stop at a random time without checking the timetable. Let W be your wait. (a) What is the distribution of W? (b) What is your expected wait, and its standard deviation? (c) What is the probability you wait more than 10 minutes?

(a) Arriving "at a random time" makes every point in the 12-minute cycle equally likely, so W∼Unif(0,12), with density 112 on [0,12].

(b) E[W]=0+122=6 minutes, and SD[W]=12212=12≈3.46 minutes.

(c) Pr(W>10)=12−1012=16: the length of the interval [10,12], divided by 12.

R

Critical thinking. Real buses aren't like clockwork: they bunch up, and gaps vary. Surprisingly, irregular buses make your expected wait longer than half the average gap, because you're more likely to arrive during a long gap than a short one (long gaps take up more of the timeline). This is called the inspection paradox, and it's why waits always feel longer than the timetable suggests.

Simulation: turning uniform numbers into anything

Computers generate random numbers that are uniform on [0,1]; runif() in R. How does R produce Gaussian or exponential random numbers from them? The trick uses quantiles: if U∼Unif(0,1) and F is a continuous CDF, then X=F−1(U), the U-quantile, has CDF F.

For any x, X≤x exactly when F−1(U)≤x, that is, when U≤F(x) (since F is increasing). And Pr(U≤F(x))=F(x) because U is uniform. ◼∎

For the dart, F(r)=r2, so R=U simulates dart distances without throwing any darts. This is the inverse transform method.

Turning random numbers into any distribution

A computer's basic random numbers are uniform between 0 and 1. To get a draw from another distribution, pick a uniform number u on the vertical axis, go across to the CDF, and drop down to the horizontal axis: the x you land on is the u-quantile. Press draw to do this many times; the histogram of the results takes the shape of the target density, because steep parts of the CDF catch many u's.

Think About It: The Inspection Paradox in the Wild

A university reports that its average class size is 30. A student survey finds that the average student is in a class of 60. Both are correct (Chapter 2's "pooling in your own life" box raised the same puzzle; now we can put numbers on it). How? Which number would you use to predict the size of a class a randomly chosen student walks into, and which to plan the number of classrooms?

Big classes contain many students, so when you sample students, you hit big classes more often, just as a random arrival at a bus stop is more likely to fall in a long gap. If there are nine classes of 20 and one of 120, the average class has 300/10=30 students, but 120 of the 300 students are in the big class, so the average student's class has (180×20+120×120)/300=60 students. To predict a random student's experience, use the student-weighted average; to plan rooms, the class average. Both are predictions; they just predict different things. Before using any average, ask: averaged over what?

Key Takeaways

  • E[X]=∫xf(x)dx and Var[X]=E[X2]−μ2: the same rules as Chapter 4, with integrals.
  • Uniform Unif(a,b): flat density; mean a+b2, variance (b−a)212; probabilities are lengths.
  • If U is uniform, F−1(U) has CDF F: that's how computers simulate any distribution.

5.3 The Gaussian distribution

The bell curve

Galton's sons' heights, measurement errors in Michelson's speed of light, birth weights, exam scores: an astonishing number of measurements have the same symmetric, bell-shaped distribution. It has a formula, and it is the most important distribution in statistics.

Definition: Gaussian Distribution

X has the Gaussian distribution with mean μ and standard deviation σ>0, written X∼N(μ,σ2), if its density is f(x)=1σ2πexp(−(x−μ)22σ2),−∞<x<∞. Then E[X]=μ and Var[X]=σ2. The standard Gaussian distribution is N(0,1), and a standard Gaussian random variable is usually called Z.

(The notation follows the convention that the second number is the variance σ2. R's functions take the standard deviation instead: pnorm(x, mean, sd). Always check which one a formula or program wants.)

History: Why Gaussian and Not Normal?

Most textbooks, and R, call this distribution the normal distribution. We keep the standard notation N(μ,σ2), and R's functions dnorm, pnorm, qnorm and rnorm, where the "norm" stands for normal.

The word normal was applied to the curve by several scientists in the 1870s and 1880s, including Francis Galton, and Karl Pearson made it standard in the 1890s. It carried a message. In the 1830s and 1840s the Belgian astronomer Adolphe Quetelet had argued that the average of a human trait described an ideal "average man" (l'homme moyen), and that variation around it was like error in a measurement: nature missing its target. Galton used the bell curve to rank people into grades of "natural ability", and the language of a normal middle and abnormal tails fitted the eugenic project of sorting people into the fit and the unfit (the history box after Example 5.6 tells that story). Pearson himself came to regret the name. In 1920 he wrote:

"Many years ago I called the Laplace–Gaussian curve the normal curve, which name, while it avoids an international question of priority, has the disadvantage of leading people to believe that all other distributions of frequency are in one sense or another 'abnormal'."

The name Gaussian is already standard in physics, engineering and machine learning, and it describes the curve without judging anyone. It isn't a perfect name either. Abraham de Moivre found the curve in 1733, and Pierre-Simon Laplace developed its theory alongside Gauss. This is an example of what the statistician Stephen Stigler called his "law of eponymy": no scientific discovery is named after its original discoverer. But it is a neutral name, and it carries a useful reminder: most data are not Gaussian, and that isn't a defect.

When you read other books and papers, translate:

How to read the formula: the curve is highest at x=μ, where the exponent is zero. As x moves away from μ, the exponent becomes more and more negative quadratically, so the density falls off very fast: values more than 3 standard deviations from the mean are rare, and beyond 5 they are almost unheard of. The factor 1σ2π in front makes the total area 1 (a famous integral, proved in the exercises of Chapter 6), and the σ inside sets the width.

Standardizing

There is no formula for the area under a Gaussian curve; it has to be computed numerically. Fortunately, every Gaussian distribution is a stretched and shifted copy of the standard Gaussian, so one table (or one R function) covers them all.

Theorem: Standardizing a Gaussian Random Variable

If X∼N(μ,σ2), then Z=X−μσ∼N(0,1). Consequently Pr(X≤x)=Pr(Z≤x−μσ)=Φ(x−μσ), where Φ (capital phi) is the CDF of the standard Gaussian.

For any z, Pr(Z≤z)=Pr(X≤μ+σz)=∫−∞μ+σzf(x)dx, where f is the N(μ,σ2) density above. Substitute t=(x−μ)/σ, so dx=σdt and the upper limit becomes z: Pr(Z≤z)=∫−∞z12πe−t2/2dt, which is the standard Gaussian CDF. ◼∎

The quantity x−μσ is a z-score, just as for data in Chapter 2: the number of standard deviations x lies from the mean. Standardizing turns every Gaussian question into a question about Z.

Standardizing keeps the area

Choose a Gaussian distribution N(μ, σ²) and an interval from a to b. The top panel shades Pr(a ≤ X ≤ b). The bottom panel shows the same region after standardizing: subtract μ and divide by σ, turning a and b into their z-scores. The two shaded areas are always equal.

Idea: The 68–95–99.7 Rule

For any Gaussian distribution, the probability of falling within 1, 2 and 3 standard deviations of the mean is about 68%, 95% and 99.7%: Pr(|Z|≤1)≈0.683,Pr(|Z|≤2)≈0.954,Pr(|Z|≤3)≈0.997. This is where Chapter 2's rule of thumb comes from. The precise value for 95% is ±1.96 standard deviations, a number you'll meet again in Chapter 8.

History: Galton's Heights, and Why He Collected Them

The heights in this chapter come from a remarkable dataset. In 1884 Francis Galton (1822–1911), a half-cousin of Charles Darwin, collected the heights of the parents and adult children of 205 families, partly by offering cash prizes for detailed family records. From these data he found that tall parents tend to have tall children, but less extremely tall than themselves. He published this in 1886 as "Regression towards mediocrity in hereditary stature", and it is the origin of the words regression and regression to the mean (Section 4.7). Two years later he introduced correlation (Chapter 6). Every regression model fitted today, in medicine, economics or machine learning, descends from this work.

Galton collected the data for a reason. In 1883 he coined the word eugenics for a programme to improve the human population by encouraging people he considered "fit" to have more children and discouraging the "unfit". In Hereditary Genius (1869) he had ranked whole races by their supposed natural ability. Others turned the idea into policy:

  • more than 30 American states passed compulsory sterilization laws; the US Supreme Court upheld Virginia's law in Buck v. Bell (1927), and more than 60,000 people were sterilized under such laws;
  • eugenic arguments helped shape the US Immigration Act of 1924, which restricted immigration from southern and eastern Europe and barred it from Asia;
  • Nazi Germany's sterilization law of 1933 drew on American models.

In 2020 University College London, where Galton had endowed the chair of eugenics, removed his name and Pearson's from its buildings.

None of this followed from the mathematics. Regression and correlation measure how quantities vary together; they cannot say why. Galton read the similarity of parents and children as evidence that ability and character are mainly inherited. But families also share wealth, food, schooling and opportunity, which are confounders in the language of Chapter 1's DAGs, and his data could not separate these causes. The tools were sound; the inference was not.

The words carry the history too. "Regression" originally meant a drift back towards "mediocrity". That is one reason Aubrey Clayton, in Bernoulli's Fallacy (2021), proposes calling linear regression simply linear modelling, and avoiding "correlation" altogether. These notes keep the standard words, which you will need in order to read the scientific literature. But knowing their origin is part of being statistically literate. The same calculation can inform or mislead, and only a reader who understands where the numbers came from can tell which.

Causal Inference: Why Are So Many Things Gaussian?

Why should heights follow a bell curve? Think about what causes a person's height: hundreds of genes, each nudging height up or down a little, plus nutrition, childhood illnesses, sleep, and much else. Height is roughly the sum of many small, independent influences. The Central Limit Theorem of Chapter 7 says that such sums are approximately Gaussian, whatever the distributions of the individual pieces. That's the deep reason the bell curve is everywhere: measurement errors, test scores and birth weights are all sums of many small effects.

The same reasoning tells you when to expect a Gaussian distribution to fail. If one cause is large and dominant, the distribution follows that cause instead: adult heights for men and women together are a mixture of two bells, because sex has a big effect on height. Incomes, house prices and pollutant concentrations are shaped by causes that multiply rather than add, and are right-skewed (Section 5.6). Before assuming a Gaussian model, ask what produced the data: a DAG with many small arrows into the outcome suggests a bell; one big arrow suggests something else.

History: Ceres, Gauss and the Birth of the Bell Curve

On 1 January 1801, the Italian astronomer Giuseppe Piazzi discovered a new object in the night sky: Ceres, now known as a dwarf planet. He tracked it for about six weeks, and then it disappeared into the glare of the Sun. Astronomers had only a few, slightly inaccurate measurements, and no idea where it would reappear.

A 24-year-old German mathematician, Carl Friedrich Gauss, took on the problem. Using new methods, including what became the method of least squares, he found the orbit that best fitted the inaccurate observations. In December 1801 astronomers pointed their telescopes where Gauss said Ceres would be, and there it was.

In 1809 Gauss explained why least squares works. He assumed that the average of repeated measurements is the most probable value of the true quantity, showed that this forces measurement errors to follow a particular symmetric, bell-shaped curve, and showed that least squares then gives the most probable orbit (Chapter 8's maximum likelihood). That is why the curve carries his name, though Abraham de Moivre had already found it in 1733, as an approximation to the binomial.

The bell curve began as a model of errors in measuring the sky. Applying it to people came later, with Quetelet, who measured the chests of Scottish soldiers, and then Galton. Both moves are described in the history boxes earlier in this section.

5.4 Waiting times: exponential and gamma

The exponential distribution

In Chapter 4, the Poisson distribution counted rare events (calls, accidents, decays) in a window of time. Now ask a different question about the same events: how long until the next one? The answer is a continuous random variable, and if events occur independently at a constant rate λ, its distribution is exponential.

Definition: Exponential Distribution

X has the exponential distribution with rate λ>0, written X∼Exp(λ), if its density is f(x)=λe−λx,x≥0, and 0 for x<0. Its CDF is F(x)=1−e−λx, so Pr(X>x)=e−λx, and E[X]=1λ,Var[X]=1λ2,median=ln2λ.

You've already met this density: Example 0.4 showed its total area is 1 and Example 0.5 computed its mean, 1/λ. The CDF comes from integrating: ∫0xλe−λtdt=1−e−λx. Setting F(m)=12 gives the median m=ln2/λ≈0.69/λ, below the mean: the exponential is right-skewed, with many short waits and a few very long ones.

Theorem: Waiting Times in a Poisson Process

If events occur as a Poisson process with rate λ (Section 4.7), the waiting time T until the first event is Exp(λ).

The wait is longer than t exactly when there are no events in [0,t]. The number of events in that window is Pois(λt), so Pr(T>t)=Pr(no events in [0,t])=e−λt(λt)00!=e−λt, which is the exponential tail probability. ◼∎

So the Poisson and exponential distributions are two views of the same process: the Poisson counts events in a fixed time, the exponential measures time between events. With 911 calls at 4.5 per hour (Example 4.20), the gap between calls is Exp(4.5), with mean 14.5 hour ≈13.3 minutes, and Pr(gap>20 min)=e−4.5/3=e−1.5≈0.223, exactly the probability of no calls in 20 minutes that we found there.

Theorem: The Exponential Is Memoryless

If X∼Exp(λ), then for all s,t≥0, Pr(X>s+t∣X>s)=Pr(X>t).

{X>s+t} is contained in {X>s}, so Pr(X>s+t∣X>s)=Pr(X>s+t)Pr(X>s)=e−λ(s+t)e−λs=e−λt=Pr(X>t).◼∎

As for the geometric distribution (Section 4.5), past waiting tells you nothing about future waiting. If you've waited 30 minutes for a call, the remaining wait has the same distribution as if you'd just started. That's a strong assumption: it fits radioactive atoms and random arrivals, but not light bulbs or people, which wear out.

The gamma distribution

The exponential is the wait for the first event. The wait for the rth event is a sum of r independent exponential gaps, and has a gamma distribution, just as the negative binomial was a sum of geometric waits.

Definition: Gamma Distribution

X has the gamma distribution with shape α>0 and rate λ>0, written X∼Gamma(α,λ), if f(x)=λαΓ(α)xα−1e−λx,x>0, where Γ(α)=∫0∞tα−1e−tdt is the gamma function (Γ(r)=(r−1)! for whole numbers r). Then E[X]=α/λ and Var[X]=α/λ2. The exponential is the case α=1; for a whole number α=r, X is the waiting time for the rth event of a Poisson process with rate λ.

The gamma is also a flexible model for any positive, right-skewed measurement (rainfall totals, insurance claims, hospital stays), whether or not it's literally a waiting time.

The beta distribution's most important job is in Bayesian statistics, as a description of what we know about an unknown proportion.

Bayesian Inference: Learning a Free-Throw Percentage

In Chapter 4's Bayesian box, we learned a success probability p from a grid of 11 candidate values. The beta distribution lets p be any value between 0 and 1. And it has a remarkable property: if your prior for p is Beta(α,β) and you observe s successes and f failures in independent trials, your posterior is Beta(α+s,β+f). The prior's parameters act like "imaginary" earlier successes and failures, and data simply add to them. (The proof multiplies the prior density by the binomial likelihood ps(1−p)f: the result has the beta form, with the exponents increased by s and f.)

Example. A new basketball player makes 7 of her first 10 free throws. Starting from a uniform prior, Beta(1,1), her posterior is Beta(8,4):

R
p <- seq(0, 1, by = 0.005)
d <- rbind(data.frame(p, density = dbeta(p, 1, 1), which = "prior Beta(1, 1)"),
           data.frame(p, density = dbeta(p, 8, 4), which = "posterior Beta(8, 4)"))
ggplot(d, aes(p, density, linetype = which)) + geom_line(linewidth = 1) +
  labs(x = "free-throw success probability p", y = "density", linetype = NULL)
8 / (8 + 4)                         # posterior mean
1 - pbeta(0.5, 8, 4)                # Pr(p > 0.5 | data)
qbeta(c(0.025, 0.975), 8, 4)        # middle 95% of the posterior
output
[1] 0.6666667
[1] 0.8867188
[1] 0.3902574 0.8907366

Her posterior mean is 812≈0.67, a little below her observed 70%, pulled slightly towards the prior's 50%. There's an 89% probability that she's better than a 50% shooter, and her true percentage is 95% likely to lie between 0.39 and 0.89: after only 10 shots, we know surprisingly little. After 100 shots the posterior would be much narrower. This "credible interval" is the Bayesian cousin of Chapter 8's confidence interval.

Think About It: What Does the Prior Do?

Two scouts watch the same new player make 7 of 10 free throws. One starts from a uniform prior, Beta(1,1); the other, who knows that most professional players make 70% to 80%, starts from Beta(15,5). Find each posterior mean. Whose estimate would you trust more after 10 shots, and after 1,000?

Posteriors: Beta(8,4) with mean 8/12≈0.67, and Beta(22,8) with mean 22/30≈0.73. After 10 shots the second scout's estimate is probably better, because her prior contains real information (the equivalent of 20 earlier shots) and 10 shots are very few. After 1,000 shots, the data overwhelm both priors: with, say, 700 makes the posteriors are Beta(701,301) and Beta(715,305), with means 0.700 and 0.701. A prior matters when data are scarce, and it should be based on evidence that can be stated and checked; with plenty of data, reasonable priors agree.

5.6 More shapes of chance

Log-Gaussian: when causes multiply

Some quantities grow by percentages: a river gathers tributaries that each enlarge it, a city grows by a few percent a year, a pollutant's concentration is diluted by successive factors. When many independent effects multiply, the logarithm of the result is a sum of many effects, and so (by the reasoning of the causal box in Section 5.3) approximately Gaussian.

Definition: log-Gaussian Distribution

X has the log-Gaussian distribution if lnX∼N(μ,σ2). Then X is positive and right-skewed, with median eμ and mean eμ+σ2/2, which is larger than the median. In R: plnorm(x, meanlog = mu, sdlog = sigma).

Weibull: things that wear out

The exponential distribution says that a component's chance of failing next month doesn't depend on its age. Most things aren't like that: tyres, bones and hard drives wear out, so their failure rate rises with age; and some products fail early from manufacturing defects, so their failure rate falls. The Weibull distribution, with Pr(X>x)=e−(x/θ)k, handles both: shape k>1 means wearing out, k<1 means early failures, and k=1 is the exponential. Engineers use it for reliability, and medical researchers for survival times.

History: Student, Who Worked at a Brewery

The t distribution was discovered by William Sealy Gosset, a chemist at the Guinness brewery in Dublin. Guinness needed to judge the quality of barley and hops from very small samples, and Gosset worked out how much the Gaussian distribution's predictions had to be widened when the standard deviation was estimated from just a handful of measurements. Guinness didn't allow its scientists to publish under their own names (after an earlier employee's paper had given away trade secrets, the company banned such publications), so his 1908 paper appeared under the pen name "Student", and the distribution has been "Student's t" ever since.

Think About It: Which Scale Should We Predict on?

A toxicologist reports that the average concentration of a pollutant in 50 water samples is 12 µg/L, but the median is 5 µg/L and the legal limit is 10. Idea: Algorithm: Which Continuous Model?

  1. What values are possible? All real numbers (Gaussian, t); positive only (exponential, gamma, log-Gaussian, Weibull); between 0 and 1 (beta); a fixed interval (uniform).
  2. What mechanism produced it? Many small additive effects: Gaussian. Multiplicative effects: log-Gaussian. Waiting for random events: exponential or gamma. Wear-out: Weibull.
  3. Look at the data: a histogram, and a Q-Q plot against the candidate model.
  4. Check the claims you care about. A model can fit the middle and fail in the tails, which is often exactly where the important questions are.

Prediction and inference in this chapter

  • Prediction. 5.8 Interludes

Interlude (Optional): The Cauchy Distribution: When Averages Fail

A lighthouse stands 1 mile off a straight coast and rotates at a steady speed, sending out brief flashes at uniformly random angles. Where along the coast do the flashes hit? If the angle is Θ∼Unif(−π2,π2), the flash hits the coast at X=tanΘ. This X has the Cauchy distribution, with density f(x)=1π(1+x2),−∞<x<∞. It looks like a bell curve centred at 0, a little lower and wider. But its tails fall off so slowly (like 1/x2, not like e−x2) that the integral ∫|x|f(x)dx is infinite: the Cauchy distribution has no expected value. When the beam is nearly parallel to the coast, the flash lands enormously far away, and those rare enormous values dominate any average.

The consequence is startling: the Law of Large Numbers fails. The average of n Cauchy draws has exactly the same Cauchy distribution as a single draw, however large n is. Averaging doesn't help at all.

An average that never settles

Both distributions are symmetric bell-like curves centred at 0. The Gaussian running average settles towards 0, as the Law of Large Numbers promises. The Cauchy running average keeps lurching: every so often a single enormous value drags it somewhere new. Add more draws and see whether it ever calms down.

Why it matters: the Law of Large Numbers and the Central Limit Theorem need a finite mean (and variance). Real data with extremely heavy tails (insurance losses from catastrophes, the sizes of internet traffic bursts, losses in financial crashes) can behave more like the Cauchy than the Gaussian, and averages of such data are much less trustworthy than the Gaussian theory suggests.

Interlude (Optional): Families of Distributions

Many of this chapter's distributions come in families built from one basic shape.

  • Location–scale families. If Z has some standard distribution, then X=μ+σZ has the same shape, shifted by μ and stretched by σ. The Gaussian family is one (Z∼N(0,1)); so are the uniform, Cauchy and t families. Standardizing (Z=(X−μ)/σ) works for any location–scale family, and probabilities for every member come from one table for Z.
  • Exponential families. Most of the named distributions in Chapters 4 and 5 (binomial, Poisson, geometric, Gaussian, exponential, gamma, beta) can be written in a common form f(x∣θ)=h(x)exp(η(θ)T(x)−A(θ)), for suitable functions h, η, T and A. For the Poisson, for example, e−λλxx!=1x!exp(xlnλ−λ), with T(x)=x. This shared form is why they have so many properties in common: simple sufficient statistics (Chapter 8), conjugate Bayesian priors like the beta–binomial pair, and the unified "generalized linear models" used throughout applied statistics and machine learning (logistic regression is built on the Bernoulli family).

Why it matters: recognizing the family a distribution belongs to tells you, at once, many of the tools that will work for it.
