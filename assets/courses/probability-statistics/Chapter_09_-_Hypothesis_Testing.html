Chapter 9
Hypothesis testing

Lectures 23–25 · weighing evidence against a claim: null hypotheses, p-values, tests for means and proportions, errors and power, and testing in a world of many tests

In 1710, the Scottish physician John Arbuthnot studied 82 years of London's christening records, from 1629 to 1710. In every single year more boys had been christened than girls. If boys and girls were equally likely, he reasoned, each year would be like a coin toss, and the chance of boys winning all 82 tosses would be (12)82, about 2×10−25, a number with 24 zeros after the decimal point. So chance couldn't be the explanation. (Arbuthnot concluded that Divine Providence was at work, ensuring enough men to replace those lost to war and danger. Today we know that slightly more boys than girls are born almost everywhere, about 105 for every 100.) Arbuthnot's argument is often called the first significance test: assume a simple explanation, compute how surprising the data would be if it were true, and abandon it if the answer is "absurdly surprising".

This chapter makes that logic precise. Chapter 8 asked what values of a parameter are plausible. A hypothesis test asks whether one particular value, or one particular claim, is plausible: Is this die fair? Does the new drug do anything? Did smoking mothers have lighter babies? Has the filling machine drifted?

Testing is a tool of inference: it asks whether the data give evidence against a claim about the mechanism that produced them. It's also a tool for decisions: shut down the production line or not, approve the drug or not, launch the new version of a website or not. Those decisions are made under uncertainty, so they can be wrong in two different ways, and Section 9.5 counts the cost of each. Finally, in a world where thousands of tests are run every day, some "discoveries" are bound to be false alarms. Section 9.7 asks how many, and connects testing back to Bayes' theorem and to the causal questions of Chapter 1.

Here is the route through the chapter.

  • Sections 9.1 and 9.2 set out the logic of a test and the p-value.
  • Sections 9.3 and 9.4 give tests for means and proportions.
  • Section 9.5 counts the two kinds of error, and the power to detect a real effect.
  • Section 9.6 connects tests with confidence intervals, and statistical with practical significance.
  • Section 9.7 asks what happens when many tests are run, and why replication matters.

Think About It: Before You Start

A friend claims she can tell whether a coin is fair just by flipping it. She flips a coin 10 times, gets 8 heads, and announces: "This coin is biased towards heads." Is 8 heads out of 10 good evidence? What would you need to know to decide? Would 80 heads out of 100 be different?

With a fair coin, 8 or more heads in 10 flips happens with probability Pr(X≥8)=45+10+11024≈0.055, about 1 time in 18: unusual, but not shocking. If she also would have called the coin biased after 8 tails, the relevant probability doubles to about 0.11. So 8 out of 10 is weak evidence. But 80 heads in 100 flips is a different matter: that's 6 standard deviations above 50, which essentially never happens with a fair coin. The same proportion gives very different evidence depending on the sample size, which is the central lesson of Chapter 7, applied to testing.

9.1 The logic of a test

A hypothesis test is a structured argument by contradiction, with a probabilistic twist. A mathematical proof by contradiction assumes a claim and derives something impossible. A test assumes a claim and finds that the data would be very unlikely.

Definition: Null and Alternative Hypotheses

The null hypothesis H0 is the default claim about a parameter, usually "no effect", "no difference" or "nothing unusual": for example H0:μ=μ0. The alternative hypothesis H1 is the claim we're looking for evidence for. It can be

  • two-sided: H1:μ≠μ0;
  • right-sided: H1:μ>μ0; or
  • left-sided: H1:μ<μ0.

Hypotheses are statements about population parameters, never about the sample, and the choice of alternative should be made before looking at the data.

Definition: Test Statistic and Null Distribution

A test statistic T is a statistic that measures how far the data are from what H0 predicts. Its sampling distribution, computed assuming H0 is true, is the null distribution. Most test statistics have the form T=estimate−value claimed by H0standard error=signalnoise.

The courtroom is the best analogy, and it's worth keeping in mind throughout the chapter.

courtroom hypothesis test
the defendant is presumed innocent assume H0 is true
the prosecution presents evidence collect data and compute T
guilty only "beyond reasonable doubt" reject H0 only if the data would be very unlikely under it
"not guilty" doesn't mean "innocent" "fail to reject H0" doesn't mean H0 is true
convicting an innocent person a Type I error (Section 9.5)
acquitting a guilty person a Type II error

Example 9.1: Boys and Girls in 1710 ★ lecture example

Arbuthnot found more male than female christenings in each of 82 consecutive years. (a) State the null hypothesis he was testing. (b) How probable was his observation under it? (c) His null was rejected decisively, but was his conclusion justified?

(a) H0: each year, more boys than girls is a 50–50 event, independently of other years. In parameter terms, H0:p=12, where p is the probability that boys outnumber girls in a year.

(b) Under H0, the number of "boy years" in 82 is Bin(82,12), and Pr(all 82 years)=(12)82≈2×10−25.

R
0.5^82
binom.test(82, 82, p = 0.5, alternative = "greater")$p.value
output
[1] 2.067952e-25
[1] 2.067952e-25

(c) Rejecting H0 was right: boys really are slightly more likely than girls at birth. But the test says only that H0 is false, not why. Arbuthnot's conclusion (Divine Providence) is one of countless alternatives consistent with the data. A test can rule out a simple explanation; it can't by itself establish any particular other one. This is the same gap between data and mechanism that ran through Chapter 1.

Arbuthnot's data survive. In every year the ratio of boys to girls christened was above 1 (the dashed line), usually by a few percent:

Example 9.2: Has the Machine Drifted? ★ lecture example

Each vial from a filling machine should contain 500 mg of a drug. The fills vary with a known SD of σ=8 mg. An engineer weighs a random sample of 20 vials. Without doing a formal test yet, compare two results: (a) x‾=501 mg; (b) x‾=523 mg. What is at stake if she decides wrongly?

If the machine is on target, X‾∼N(500,82/20), with standard error 8/20≈1.79 mg.

(a) x‾=501 is 11.79≈0.56 standard errors above 500, entirely ordinary for a machine on target.

(b) x‾=523 is 231.79≈12.9 standard errors above 500. A calibrated machine would essentially never produce it.

Both means exceed 500, but only the second is surprising: what matters is the deviation relative to the standard error. That ratio is the test statistic.

What's at stake. Stopping the line when nothing is wrong (a false alarm) wastes money and time. Missing a real drift sends patients doses that are too high. The two errors have very different costs, and Section 9.5 shows how to balance them.

History: Popper and the Black Swan

The philosopher Karl Popper (1902–1994) pointed out an asymmetry in how evidence works. No number of white swans can prove that all swans are white, but a single black swan proves that not all are, and black swans (Cygnus atratus) were indeed found in Australia. Popper argued that a scientific claim must be falsifiable: it must say something that observation could contradict.

The null hypothesis is a falsifiable claim, set up to be knocked down. There's one important difference from Popper's logic. A black swan logically refutes "all swans are white", but in statistics nothing is logically impossible under H0; extreme data are only improbable. We reject H0 when keeping it would require believing in a very unlikely coincidence. And, as with the swans, failing to find evidence against H0 never proves it: not seeing a black swan this afternoon doesn't mean they don't exist.

Check Yourself

A researcher wants evidence that a new teaching method raises the mean exam score above the current 70. Which hypotheses should she use?

Hypotheses are about the population, not the sample.

Which claim is the default, and which do we want evidence for?

The default (no improvement) is the null; the claim we want evidence for is the alternative; both are about the population mean μ.

Think About It: Who Carries the Burden of Proof?

A company wants to sell a new food additive. Should the null hypothesis be "the additive is safe" or "the additive is harmful"? Who benefits from each choice, and what does each mean for a small, inconclusive study?

The null is the claim that stands unless the evidence overturns it. With H0: "safe", a small, noisy study that finds nothing lets the additive onto the market: failure to reject favours the company. With H0: "harmful" (or "not shown to be safe"), the company must produce strong evidence of safety, and an inconclusive study keeps the additive off the market. Regulators such as the US Food and Drug Administration generally put the burden on the manufacturer to demonstrate safety for new drugs and food additives. Which hypothesis is the null isn't a mathematical question: it's a decision about who bears the risk of error, and it should be made openly.

Key Takeaways

  • A test assumes H0 and asks whether the data would be surprising under it.
  • H0 and H1 are about parameters; choose one- or two-sided before seeing the data.
  • A test statistic is usually (estimate − null value)/standard error: signal over noise.
  • Rejecting H0 doesn't establish any particular alternative explanation.

9.2 p-values

Definition: p-Value

The p-value is the probability, computed assuming H0 is true, of getting a test statistic at least as extreme as the one observed, in the direction(s) of the alternative. For an observed value t:

  • right-sided: p=Pr(T≥t∣H0);
  • left-sided: p=Pr(T≤t∣H0);
  • two-sided: p=Pr(|T|≥|t|∣H0).

A small p-value means the data would be unusual if H0 were true, which is evidence against H0.

To turn a p-value into a decision, choose a significance level α in advance (0.05 is conventional, but not sacred). If p≤α, reject H0: the result is statistically significant at level α. If p>α, fail to reject H0. Equivalently, reject when the test statistic falls in the rejection region, the set of values beyond the critical value (such as |z|>1.96 for a two-sided test at the 5% level).

Test statistics, rejection regions and p-values

The curve is the null distribution: how the test statistic behaves if H0 is true. The red bars along the axis mark the rejection region: the statistic lands there with probability α if H0 is true. The orange area is the p-value: the probability, under H0, of a statistic at least as extreme as the one observed. Load one of the chapter's examples, or move the observed statistic yourself. Notice that we reject exactly when the observed statistic is in the red region, which is exactly when the p-value is at most α.

Watch Out: What a p-Value Is Not

A p-value is Pr(data at least this extreme∣H0). It is not:

  • the probability that H0 is true (that's Pr(H0∣data), the reverse conditional: Section 3.6's prosecutor's fallacy again);
  • the probability that the result happened "by chance";
  • a measure of how large or important the effect is (Section 9.6);
  • and 1−p is not the probability that H1 is true.

A large p-value doesn't show that H0 is true either: the study may simply have been too small to detect the effect (Section 9.5).

Example 9.3: Evidence for Precognition? ★ lecture example

In 2011 the psychologist Daryl Bem published nine experiments in a leading journal, claiming statistically significant evidence that people can sense future events. In a simplified version of one experiment, subjects guess which of two curtains hides a picture, with the picture's position chosen at random after the guess. With no precognition, each guess is right with probability 12. Suppose 53 of 100 guesses are right. (a) Test H0:p=0.5 against H1:p>0.5. (b) If 20 independent experiments like this were run, and precognition doesn't exist, how many would you expect to reach p<0.05? (c) What does this suggest about a published significant result?

(a) Under H0, the number correct is Bin(100,0.5), with mean 50 and SD 5, so z=53−505=0.6, and the one-sided p-value is Pr(Z≥0.6)≈0.27. The exact binomial p-value, Pr(X≥53), is 0.31. Either way, 53 correct is entirely consistent with guessing: we fail to reject H0.

R
1 - pnorm(0.6)                                               # Gaussian approximation
binom.test(53, 100, p = 0.5, alternative = "greater")$p.value  # exact
output
[1] 0.2742531
[1] 0.3086497

(b) If H0 is true, a p-value falls below 0.05 with probability (at most) 0.05 (that's what the threshold means), so among 20 experiments we'd expect 20×0.05=1 "significant" result by chance alone.

(c) A single significant result, especially when it's surprising and when several analyses or experiments were tried, is weak evidence. Bem's paper helped trigger psychology's "replication crisis" (Section 9.7): when other researchers repeated his experiments carefully, they found no effect. A p-value controls the false-alarm rate of one pre-planned test, not of a research programme that tries many.

Run the same study again and again

Each run draws a fresh sample of size n from a Gaussian population and carries out a two-sided t-test of H0: μ = 0. The true effect is the real mean, measured in standard deviations. With no effect, p-values are spread evenly between 0 and 1, and a fraction α of them fall below α purely by chance. With a real effect they pile up near 0, and the fraction below α is the power. Watch how much a single p-value jumps around between identical studies.

The widget shows something important: even for identical studies of a real effect, the p-value varies enormously from one study to the next. A p-value is a statistic, with its own sampling distribution, and when H0 is true (and the test statistic is continuous) it's uniformly distributed between 0 and 1.

Check Yourself

A two-sided z test gives z=2.0. Find the p-value, to 4 decimal places.

2(1−Φ(2)): 2 * (1 - pnorm(2)).

2×0.02275≈0.0455, just below 0.05.

A study reports p=0.01 for the effect of a supplement. Which statement is correct?

Is the p-value Pr(H0∣data) or Pr(data∣H0)?

Is 1−p the probability that H1 is true?

A p-value is computed assuming H0; it can't be the probability of H0. Turning it into one needs Bayes' theorem and a prior (Section 9.7's Bayes box).

Think About It: No Evidence of an Effect

A trial of a blood-pressure drug with 10 patients finds p=0.30. The lead investigator writes: "The drug has no effect on blood pressure." What's wrong with this conclusion, and how should it be worded?

Failing to reject H0 means the data are consistent with no effect, not that they show no effect. With only 10 patients, the trial may have had little chance of detecting even a useful reduction (low power, Section 9.5). The correct statement is "we found no statistically significant evidence of an effect", ideally with a confidence interval for the effect, such as "the reduction was 4 mmHg, 95% CI −4 to 12 mmHg", which shows that the data are consistent both with no effect and with a large one. "Absence of evidence is not evidence of absence."

Key Takeaways

  • p-value =Pr(a statistic at least as extreme as observed∣H0).
  • Reject H0 if p≤α, chosen in advance; otherwise "fail to reject", never "accept".
  • A p-value is not the probability that H0 is true, and it doesn't measure effect size.
  • p-values vary a lot between identical studies; under H0 they are uniform on [0,1].

9.3 Tests for a mean

The tests for a mean mirror the confidence intervals of Section 8.4: the same three cases, the same standard errors, the same critical values.

Theorem: Tests for a Population Mean

To test H0:μ=μ0 with a random sample of size n:

  1. Gaussian population, σ known: Z=X‾−μ0σ/n has a standard Gaussian null distribution.
  2. Gaussian population, σ unknown: T=X‾−μ0S/n has a t null distribution with n−1 degrees of freedom (the one-sample t test).
  3. Any population, large n: T is approximately standard Gaussian (by the CLT), and the t test is approximately valid.

The p-value is the tail area beyond the observed statistic, in the direction(s) of H1. In R, t.test(x, mu = mu0) carries out case 2 (and, approximately, case 3).

Example 9.4: Testing the Vials ★ lecture example

Return to the filling machine of Example 9.2 (σ=8 mg, n=20). This time the sample mean is x‾=503.5 mg. Test H0:μ=500 against H1:μ≠500 at the 5% level.

Test statistic: z=503.5−5008/20=3.51.789≈1.957.

p-value (two-sided): 2Pr(Z≥1.957)≈0.0504.

R
z <- (503.5 - 500) / (8 / sqrt(20))
c(z = z, p = 2 * (1 - pnorm(z)))
output
         z          p 
1.95655948 0.05039928

Decision. p=0.0504 is just above 0.05, so at the 5% level we fail to reject H0; equivalently, |z|=1.957<1.96.

Critical thinking. A result of p=0.0504 and one of p=0.0496 are, for all practical purposes, the same strength of evidence, yet one is "significant" and the other isn't. The 5% line is a convention, not a cliff. A sensible engineer would report the p-value (or better, the confidence interval for μ: 503.5±3.51=(499.99,507.01) mg, which just contains 500, as it must when p>0.05), weigh the cost of a drifting machine, and perhaps weigh a few more vials.

The p-value is an area: the probability in the two tails beyond the observed z, under the null distribution.

R
z <- seq(-4, 4, by = 0.01); d <- data.frame(z, f = dnorm(z))
ggplot(d, aes(z, f)) +
  geom_area(data = subset(d, z >= 1.957), fill = "orange", alpha = 0.7) +
  geom_area(data = subset(d, z <= -1.957), fill = "orange", alpha = 0.7) +
  geom_line(linewidth = 1) +
  geom_vline(xintercept = c(-1.96, 1.96), linetype = "dashed") +
  annotate("text", x = 3.05, y = 0.09, label = "p/2 = 0.025") + annotate("text", x = -3.05, y = 0.09, label = "p/2 = 0.025") +
  labs(x = "z (if H0 is true)", y = "density")

Comparing two groups

Many questions compare two independent groups: smokers and non-smokers, treatment and control. The same signal-over-noise idea applies, using Section 6.6's rule that variances of independent quantities add.

Theorem: The Two-Sample (Welch) T Test

For independent random samples of sizes n1 and n2 from two populations with means μ1 and μ2, to test H0:μ1=μ2 use T=X‾1−X‾2S12n1+S22n2. Under H0, T is approximately t-distributed, with degrees of freedom given by a formula that R computes (the Welch approximation); for large samples it's approximately standard Gaussian. In R: t.test(y ~ group).

The denominator is the standard error of X‾1−X‾2: each mean has variance σi2/ni, and the variance of a difference of independent quantities is the sum of their variances.

Example 9.6: Smoking and Birth Weight ★ lecture example

In the Baystate Medical Center data of Chapter 1 (MASS::birthwt), the 74 babies of mothers who smoked during pregnancy weighed on average 2,772 g (SD 660 g), and the 115 babies of non-smokers 3,056 g (SD 753 g). (a) Test whether the mean birth weights differ. (b) Find a 95% confidence interval for the difference. (c) Does this show that smoking causes lower birth weight?

(a) The difference in means is 3056−2772=284 g, with standard error 7532115+660274≈4931+5886≈104 g, so t=284/104≈2.73.

(c) Not by itself. The test shows that the difference is unlikely to be a fluke of sampling, not why it exists. Statistical significance answers "could this be chance?"; it doesn't answer "what caused it?".

For a two-sided t test at the 5% level with a sample of 10, what critical value must |t| exceed?

Think About It: One-Sided or Two-Sided?

A researcher planned a two-sided test, got p=0.08, then noticed that the effect went in the direction she'd expected and switched to a one-sided test, getting p=0.04. Is this legitimate?

No. A one-sided p-value is half the two-sided one when the effect goes in the predicted direction, so switching after seeing the data always makes a result look stronger, and it doubles the real false-alarm rate. The alternative must be chosen before the data are seen, based on the scientific question: one-sided only when an effect in the other direction would be treated exactly like no effect (for example, a quality check where only underfilling matters). Pre-registering the analysis plan (Section 9.7) is the cure for this temptation, and for many others like it.

Key Takeaways

  • One-sample tests for a mean: z=(x‾−μ0)/(σ/n) if σ is known; otherwise t=(x‾−μ0)/(s/n) on n−1 degrees of freedom.
  • Two independent groups: Welch's t=(x‾1−x‾2)/s12/n1+s22/n2. Paired data: test the differences.
  • A significant difference in observational data doesn't establish cause.

9.4 Tests for a proportion

Theorem: Tests for a Proportion

To test H0:p=p0 with x successes in n independent trials:

  1. Large sample (both np0 and n(1−p0) at least 10): under H0, Z=p̂−p0p0(1−p0)/n is approximately standard Gaussian. Note that the standard error uses the null value p0, since the null distribution is computed assuming H0.

  2. Exact: the p-value is a binomial tail probability computed with p0. In R: binom.test(x, n, p = p0).

The large-sample test is exactly the test that the Wilson interval of Section 8.5 inverts, which is one reason the Wilson interval is the better one.

Example 9.7: Is This Die Loaded? ★ lecture example

In Example 7.11, a die showed 120 sixes in 600 rolls. Test, at the 5% level, whether it favours six: H0:p=16 against H1:p>16.

p̂=120/600=0.2, and under H0 the standard error is 16⋅56/600≈0.0152, so z=0.2−0.16670.0152≈2.19,p=Pr(Z≥2.19)≈0.014. The exact binomial test gives p=Pr(X≥120)≈0.018 (Example 7.11 approximated it as 0.016 using a continuity correction).

R
z <- (120/600 - 1/6) / sqrt(1/6 * 5/6 / 600)
c(z = z, p = 1 - pnorm(z))
binom.test(120, 600, p = 1/6, alternative = "greater")$p.value
output
         z          p 
2.19089023 0.01422987 
[1] 0.01801076

Reject H0 at the 5% level: there is evidence that the die favours six. But notice what we haven't learned: how much it favours six. The 95% Wilson interval for p is about (0.17, 0.23), which runs from "barely loaded" to "noticeably loaded". And if a casino tested 100 dice and this was the most extreme, a p-value of 0.014 would be unremarkable (Section 9.7).

  labs(x = "sixes in 600 rolls of a fair die", y = "probability")

Key Takeaways

  • Proportion test: z=(p̂−p0)/p0(1−p0)/n, using p0 in the standard error; exact version binom.test.
  • A significant test tells you the null is implausible; a confidence interval tells you how big the effect might be.

9.5 Errors and power

Because a test is a decision made from random data, it can be wrong in two ways.

Definition: Type I and Type II Errors, and Power

H0 trueH₀ true H0 falseH₀ false
reject H0 Type I error (false alarm) correct (detection)
fail to reject H0 correct Type II error (missed effect)
  • α=Pr(reject H0∣H0 true), the significance level, is the Type I error rate.
  • β, the Type II error rate, is Pr(fail to reject H0) when a particular alternative is true.
  • The power of a test against an alternative is 1−β: the probability of detecting the effect if it's really there.

Choosing α fixes the false-alarm rate. The Type II error rate depends on four things: α, the sample size n, the noise σ, and how big the true effect is. Reducing α (being stricter) increases β, for a fixed n: you convict fewer innocent people but acquit more guilty ones. The only way to reduce both is more data, or less noise.

Example 9.9: How Likely Is the Engineer to Catch a Drift? ★ lecture example

The vial test of Example 9.4 uses n=20, σ=8 mg, and a two-sided test at the 5% level. (a) If the machine has really drifted to μ=505 mg, what is the power? (b) What if the engineer weighs 40 vials?

(a) Rejection region. We reject when |x‾−500|>1.96×1.789=3.51 mg, that is, when x‾>503.51 or x‾<496.49.

Probability of landing there if μ=505. Now X‾∼N(505,1.7892), so power=Pr(X‾>503.51)+Pr(X‾<496.49)=Pr(Z>503.51−5051.789)+Pr(Z<496.49−5051.789)=Pr(Z>−0.83)+Pr(Z<−4.76)≈0.798.power=Pr(X‾>503.51)+Pr(X‾<496.49)=Pr(Z>503.51−5051.789)+Pr(Z<496.49−5051.789)=Pr(Z>−0.83)+Pr(Z<−4.76)≈0.798. (b) With n=40, the standard error falls to 8/40=1.26 and the power rises to about 0.98.

Planning a study: how many observations?

Before collecting data, a researcher should ask: if there really is an effect of the size I care about, how likely is my study to detect it? A study with low power is usually a waste of time and money, and of the participants' goodwill.

Theorem: Sample Size for a Z Test

To detect a difference δ=|μ1−μ0| with power 1−β, using a two-sided test at level α with known σ, approximately n=((zα/2+zβ)σδ)2, where zβ is the upper-β point of the standard Gaussian (0.84 for 80% power, 1.28 for 90%).

Ignore the far rejection region, which contributes almost nothing. Power ≈Pr(Z>zα/2−δσ/n). For this to equal 1−β, we need zα/2−δnσ=−zβ, which rearranges to the formula. ◼∎

Example 9.10: How Many Vials?

How many vials must the engineer weigh to have 90% power to detect a 5 mg drift (σ=8, two-sided, α=0.05)?

n=((1.96+1.28)×85)2≈26.9, so 27 vials. For 80% power, (1.96+0.84)2×64/25≈20.1, so about 20 vials, matching Example 9.9. Notice the cost of certainty: going from 80% to 90% power needs a third more data, and halving the drift to be detected needs four times as much.

Think About It: Blackstone's Ratio

The English jurist William Blackstone wrote in the 1760s that "it is better that ten guilty persons escape than that one innocent suffer." Translate this into the language of Type I and Type II errors. What does it imply about the "significance level" of a criminal trial? Is a lower α always better?

With H0: "innocent", convicting an innocent person is a Type I error and acquitting a guilty one is a Type II error. Blackstone says a Type I error is at least ten times as costly as a Type II error, so the threshold for conviction should be strict (a small α), even at the price of more guilty people going free (a larger β). But α can't be pushed to zero without making conviction impossible, and acquitting the guilty has real costs to victims and to public safety. The right balance depends on the costs of each error, which is a moral and political judgment, not a statistical one. The same is true in medicine (approving a harmful drug vs. rejecting a useful one) and in forensic science (declaring a false match vs. missing a true one).

Key Takeaways

  • Type I error = false alarm (probability α); Type II error = missed effect (probability β); power = 1−β.
  • Power grows with the sample size, the size of the effect and α, and falls with the noise σ.
  • Plan the sample size before collecting data: n≈((zα/2+zβ)σ/δ)2.

9.6 Tests and intervals; statistical and practical significance

Tests and confidence intervals are two views of one calculation

A two-sided t test at level α rejects H0:μ=μ0 exactly when μ0 lies outside the 100(1−α)% t confidence interval for μ.

The test fails to reject when |x‾−μ0s/n|≤tn−1,α/2, that is, when x‾−tn−1,α/2sn≤μ0≤x‾+tn−1,α/2sn: exactly when μ0 is inside the interval. ◼∎

So a 95% confidence interval is the set of null values that a 5%-level test would not reject. In Example 9.5, the interval (0.70, 2.46) excludes 0, so the test rejects μd=0. The interval is usually more informative than the test, because it also shows how big the effect might be.

Statistical significance is not practical significance

With enough data, any difference from H0, however tiny, becomes statistically significant, because the standard error shrinks like 1/n while the effect stays the same size.

Example 9.11: A Significant Trivial Effect ★ lecture example

An online test-prep company tests 1,000,000 students and finds that their average score on an IQ-style test, scaled to have mean 100 and SD 15 in the population, is 100.08. Test H0:μ=100. Is the company's course worth paying for?

z=100.08−10015/1,000,000=0.080.015≈5.3,p≈10−7. Overwhelmingly significant. But the estimated effect is 0.08 points, about 1200 of a standard deviation: no student, teacher or employer could ever notice it. (And since these students weren't compared with a randomized control group, even that tiny difference might reflect who chose to take the course.)

Report the effect, not just the p-value. A confidence interval, here roughly 100.08±0.03, shows at once that the effect is precisely estimated and practically negligible. A common standardized measure of effect size is the difference in means divided by the SD (Cohen's d); here d≈0.005, where conventional benchmarks call 0.2 "small".

Watch Out: Significant Isn't Important, and Non-Significant Isn't Zero

  • A tiny p-value can come from a trivial effect measured on a huge sample (Example 9.11).
  • A large p-value can come from an important effect measured on a small sample (the blood-pressure trial in Section 9.2's think box).
  • Always report an estimate of the effect with a confidence interval, and judge its size against what matters in context.

A picture makes the four possibilities clear. Each line is a 95% confidence interval for an effect. The solid vertical line is "no effect"; the dashed line is the smallest effect that would matter in practice.

History: 0.05, Fisher, and the Debate Over Significance

Ronald Fisher's book Statistical Methods for Research Workers (1925) made the p-value and the 5% level standard tools of science. Fisher regarded 5% as a convenient rule of thumb for flagging results worth following up, not as a line between truth and falsehood. In 1928–1933 Jerzy Neyman and Egon Pearson (Karl Pearson's son) developed a different theory: fix α in advance, choose the test with the greatest power, and treat the outcome as a decision, with no interpretation of the p-value as evidence. Fisher and Neyman feuded for the rest of their lives, and the hybrid of the two approaches taught in most textbooks (including, in places, this one) is something neither would have fully endorsed.

Criticism has grown in recent decades. In 2016 the American Statistical Association issued its first-ever formal statement on a statistical method, warning that "a p-value, or statistical significance, does not measure the size of an effect or the importance of a result" and that "scientific conclusions ... should not be based only on whether a p-value passes a specific threshold." In Bernoulli's Fallacy (2021), Aubrey Clayton goes further: he argues that significance testing is logically flawed (it uses Pr(data∣H0) where we need Pr(H0∣data)), and that the framework's development by Galton, Pearson and Fisher was shaped by their eugenic projects, which needed methods that looked objective (Sections 2.3, 4.6 and 5.3 tell that history). Many statisticians who would not go as far as Clayton agree that p-values are overused and misread, and recommend reporting estimates and intervals, and thinking about prior evidence, alongside them.

Check Yourself

A 95% confidence interval for a mean difference is (−1.2, 3.8). What does a two-sided test of "no difference" at the 5% level conclude?

What matters is whether 0 is inside the interval.

Tests and intervals are two views of the same calculation.

The interval contains 0, so a 5%-level test would not reject. The interval also shows that the data are consistent with a fairly large positive difference: "not significant" doesn't mean "no difference".

Key Takeaways

  • A 95% CI contains exactly the null values that a 5%-level two-sided test would not reject.
  • Statistical significance depends on sample size; practical significance depends on the size of the effect.
  • Report estimates with intervals, not just p-values or "significant / not significant".

9.7 Testing in a world of many tests

Multiple comparisons

Each test at level 0.05 has a 5% chance of a false alarm when H0 is true. Run 20 independent tests of true nulls, and the chance of at least one false alarm is 1−0.9520≈0.64. Search through enough data and "significant" findings are guaranteed. We met this before with Paul the octopus (Section 4.4), cancer clusters (Section 4.7) and blood-test reference ranges (Section 5.3).

Example 9.12: The Dead Salmon ★ lecture example

In 2009, the neuroscientist Craig Bennett and colleagues placed a dead Atlantic salmon in an MRI scanner and showed it photographs of people in social situations, "asking" it to judge the people's emotions. They analysed the scan as a typical brain-imaging study would, testing each of thousands of small brain regions (voxels) separately for a response at a strict per-test threshold. Several voxels in the salmon's brain "lit up". (a) Why? (b) What should the analysis have done?

(a) A typical brain scan contains about 130,000 voxels. With that many tests, even a per-test false-alarm rate of 1 in 1,000 gives about 130 false alarms expected by chance. Some of them happened to cluster together in the salmon's brain cavity. The fish was not thinking.

(b) Correct for multiple comparisons. The simplest method, the Bonferroni correction, tests each of m hypotheses at level α/m, which guarantees that the chance of any false alarm is at most α (by the union bound of Section 3.3, extended to m events: Pr(any)≤∑Pr(each)=m×α/m). With corrections applied, nothing in the salmon was significant. The authors' point, which won them an Ig Nobel prize in 2012, was that many published brain-imaging studies at the time didn't correct properly.

R
1 - 0.95^20              # chance of at least one false alarm in 20 tests
0.05 / 20                # Bonferroni threshold for 20 tests
output
[1] 0.6415141
[1] 0.0025

How many significant results are true?

Suppose a field tests many hypotheses, of which only a fraction are real effects. Among the results that come out significant, what fraction are real? This is Bayes' theorem (Section 3.6), with "significant" playing the role of a positive test result.

How many significant results are real?

Imagine a field that tests 400 hypotheses. Only some of them are real effects. Each square is one study. Real effects are detected with probability equal to the power; true nulls come out significant with probability α. Among the significant results, what fraction are false alarms? This is Bayes' theorem from Chapter 3 in disguise: the answer depends on how many effects were real to begin with.

If 10% of tested hypotheses are true, tests have power 0.8, and α=0.05, then out of 1,000 hypotheses, 100 are real and 80 of those come out significant; 900 are null and 45 of those come out significant. So 45/125=36% of the significant results are false alarms. With power 0.3, typical of small studies, the false fraction rises to 45/75=60%. In 2005 the epidemiologist John Ioannidis used exactly this arithmetic in a famous paper titled "Why most published research findings are false".

The p-values themselves show what's happening. When every null hypothesis is true, p-values are spread evenly between 0 and 1, and 5% of them fall below 0.05 by chance. When some effects are real, those p-values pile up near 0, on top of the flat floor of false alarms:

p_mix <- 2 * (1 - pnorm(abs(z)))
d <- rbind(data.frame(p = p_null, world = "all nulls true"),
           data.frame(p = p_mix, world = "10% of effects real"))

History: The Replication Crisis

In 2015 the Open Science Collaboration, a group of 270 researchers, repeated 100 studies published in three leading psychology journals, following the original methods as closely as possible. Of the original studies, 97% had reported significant results; of the replications, only about 36% did, and the effects found were on average about half the size of the originals. Similar projects in cancer biology, economics and other fields have found related problems.

The causes are the ones in this chapter: small, underpowered studies; many analyses tried and only the significant ones reported (sometimes called "p-hacking"); journals that publish significant results and reject null ones; and the misreading of p<0.05 as proof. The remedies are also statistical: larger samples, pre-registration of hypotheses and analysis plans before the data are collected, publishing null results, reporting effect sizes with confidence intervals, and above all replication. One significant result is the start of an investigation, not the end.

Bayesian Inference: What Does P = 0.05 Really Tell You?

A p-value isn't the probability that H0 is true, but we can ask how much it could shift that probability. The statisticians Thomas Sellke, M. J. Bayarri and James Berger showed in 2001 that, under broad conditions, a p-value of p can change the odds on H0 by at most a factor of about −eplnp (the Bayes factor bound, for p<1/e). For p=0.05 that factor is about 0.41: if H0 and H1 start equally likely, the probability of H0 can fall no lower than about 29%.

R
bf_bound <- function(p) -exp(1) * p * log(p)
p <- c(0.05, 0.01, 0.005, 0.001)
data.frame(p, smallest_posterior_H0 = round(bf_bound(p) / (1 + bf_bound(p)), 3))
output
      p smallest_posterior_H0
1 0.050                 0.289
2 0.010                 0.111
3 0.005                 0.067
4 0.001                 0.018

So "p=0.05" is much weaker evidence than it sounds: at best, it moves 50–50 odds to about 70–30. That's one reason some statisticians have proposed requiring p<0.005 for claims of new discoveries, and why Bayesian analyses report the posterior probability of hypotheses directly.

Causal Inference: Randomized Trials and Observational Tests

A significance test answers "could this difference be due to sampling chance?". In a randomized controlled trial, where a coin toss decides who gets the treatment, that's the only alternative explanation left: randomization balances the confounders on average (Chapter 1), so a significant difference can be attributed to the treatment. In an observational study, significance rules out chance but not confounding.

The most famous cautionary tale is hormone replacement therapy (HRT) for women after menopause. Large observational studies in the 1980s and 1990s found that women taking HRT had significantly fewer heart attacks, and HRT was widely prescribed partly for that reason. But the women who chose HRT tended to be wealthier, more health-conscious and better cared for. In 2002, the Women's Health Initiative randomized trial, with about 16,000 women, was stopped early: the group given HRT had more heart attacks, strokes and breast cancers, not fewer.

The observational p-values were small, and the conclusion was wrong. A test is only as good as the comparison it tests.

Think About It: The Garden of Forking Paths

A researcher studying whether a new policing strategy reduced crime can choose which crimes to count (all, violent only, property only), which time window to use (6 months, 1 year, 2 years), and which comparison neighbourhoods to include (3 options). She tries every combination and reports the one with the smallest p-value, 0.03. Roughly how many analyses did she effectively run? How much should we trust the result, and what would make it trustworthy?

3×3×3=27 analyses. Even if the policy did nothing, the chance that at least one of 27 analyses gives p<0.05 is high (though the analyses use overlapping data, so it's less than 1−0.9527≈0.75). The reported p=0.03 is not the false-alarm rate of what she actually did, so it means very little. The statisticians Andrew Gelman and Eric Loken call this the "garden of forking paths": the problem arises even without any intent to deceive, whenever choices are made after seeing the data. The result would be trustworthy if the analysis had been pre-registered (chosen and published before seeing the data), if all 27 results were reported, or if the finding replicated in a new city with the same analysis fixed in advance.

Key Takeaways

  • Many tests produce false alarms: the chance of at least one among m independent true nulls is 1−(1−α)m. Bonferroni: test each at α/m.
  • The fraction of significant results that are real depends on power and on how many tested hypotheses were true to begin with (Bayes' theorem).
  • Only randomization rules out confounding; significance only rules out chance.
  • Replication and pre-registration are the best protection against false findings.

Prediction and inference in this chapter

  • Inference. A hypothesis test asks whether the data contradict a claim about the mechanism: a fair die, a machine on target, no difference between drugs. The p-value measures how surprising the data would be under the claim. We tested means (one and two groups) and proportions, and saw that a confidence interval contains the same information and more.
  • Decisions. Testing is also a decision rule, with two kinds of error. Power analysis predicts, before any data are collected, how likely a study is to detect an effect of a given size, which is a prediction about the study itself.
  • The limits. A significant result can be trivial, a non-significant one can hide a real effect, many tests guarantee false alarms, and in observational data significance doesn't rule out confounding. The salmon, the replication crisis and HRT all show what happens when these limits are forgotten.

9.8 Interludes

Interlude (Optional): The Neyman–Pearson Lemma: The Most Powerful Test

Among all tests with false-alarm rate α, which has the greatest power? For the simplest case, a null value θ0 against a single alternative θ1, Neyman and Pearson proved in 1933 that the best test rejects H0 when the likelihood ratio L(θ1)L(θ0) is large, with the cutoff chosen to make the false-alarm rate exactly α. In words: reject the null when the data are much better explained by the alternative than by the null. For the Gaussian mean, the likelihood ratio is large exactly when x‾ is far from μ0 in the direction of μ1, so the one-sided z test of Section 9.3 (in the direction of μ1) is the most powerful test. The likelihood (Section 8.3) thus underlies both estimation and testing.

Why it matters: the lemma explains why the standard tests are built the way they are, and the likelihood ratio is also the key quantity in Bayesian testing (it's the Bayes factor for two simple hypotheses) and in forensic science, where DNA and fingerprint evidence is increasingly reported as a likelihood ratio.

Interlude (Optional): Testing a Whole Distribution: Pearson's Chi-Squared

In Section 4.7 we compared the V-1 bomb hits on 576 squares of south London with a Poisson model by eye. Karl Pearson's chi-squared test (1900) makes the comparison formal. With observed counts Ok and expected counts Ek in each category, X2=∑k(Ok−Ek)2Ek has approximately a chi-squared distribution (Section 5.6) if the model is right, with degrees of freedom equal to the number of categories minus 1, minus the number of parameters estimated from the data.

R
obs <- c(229, 211, 93, 35, 8)                    # squares with 0, 1, 2, 3, 4+ hits
lambda <- 537 / 576
expected <- 576 * c(dpois(0:3, lambda), 1 - ppois(3, lambda))
round(expected, 1)
X2 <- sum((obs - expected)^2 / expected)
c(X2 = X2, p = 1 - pchisq(X2, df = 5 - 1 - 1))
output
[1] 226.7 211.4  98.5  30.6   8.7
       X2         p 
1.0175894 0.7969959

Here X2≈1.0 on 3 degrees of freedom, with p≈0.80: the data fit the Poisson model very well, which is the formal version of Clarke's conclusion that the bombs fell at random.

Why it matters: the chi-squared test checks whether a whole model fits, not just one parameter, and the same statistic tests independence in two-way tables such as hair and eye colour (Section 6.1). It's one of the oldest tests still in everyday use.

Interlude (Optional): Looking Back: What This Course Was for

The course began with two goals, prediction and inference, and with a warning that the tools of statistics have a complicated history. Prediction asked for good guesses with errors small enough to live with: an airline's overbooking, an elevator's capacity, a son's height, a casino's profit. Inference asked what the data say about the mechanism behind them: whether a die is loaded, whether a drug works, how many tanks an enemy has, whether bombs were aimed.

The same ideas kept returning. Variation is not a nuisance but the subject. Averages are predictable, individuals are not. Every estimate has a standard error, and every interval assumes no bias. Conditional probabilities run in two directions, and confusing them convicts innocent people. Correlation, regression and significance tests measure association, while the causes that produced the data have to come from the design of the study and from knowledge of the mechanism.

Some of these tools were first developed by people who used them to argue for ranking and controlling human beings. The mathematics was sound; the arguments were not, and they did real harm. The best protection against that misuse was never to abandon the tools but to understand them well enough to see what they can and cannot show. That understanding, statistical literacy, is what this course has tried to give you.

    Type I: reject H0 when it's true. Type II: fail to reject when it's false.

    (a) Type I: alarm with no fire (annoying); Type II: no alarm during a fire (deadly), so detectors are tuned to accept many false alarms. (b) Type I: declaring a match for an innocent person (wrongful conviction); Type II: missing the true source. The legal system treats Type I as worse. (c) Type I: searching an innocent passenger; Type II: missing a weapon. Screening accepts many false alarms, followed by a second, more careful check.

  9. Interpreting p. For each statement about a study that found p=0.03, say whether it's correct. (a) "The probability that the null hypothesis is true is 0.03." (b) "If the null hypothesis were true, results at least this extreme would occur about 3% of the time." (c) "The effect is large." (d) "If we repeat the study, we'll almost certainly get p<0.05 again."

    Recall the warning box in Section 9.2 and the p-value widget.

    (a) Wrong. (b) Correct. (c) Wrong: p doesn't measure effect size. (d) Wrong: p-values vary a lot; if the true effect is just big enough to give p=0.03, a replication has only about a 50–60% chance of p<0.05.

  10. Many tests. A researcher tests 50 food items for a link with a disease, at the 5% level, and finds 3 significant. If none of the foods truly matters, how many significant results would be expected? Is 3 surprising? What would the Bonferroni threshold be?

    Expected false alarms =50×0.05; the count is roughly Bin(50,0.05).

    2.5 expected; Pr(X≥3)≈0.46, so 3 is not surprising at all. Bonferroni: 0.05/50=0.001 per test.

  11. Duality. A 95% confidence interval for a mean is (4.2, 7.9). For which of these null values would a two-sided 5% test reject: 4, 5, 8? Explain.

    Reject exactly when the null value is outside the interval.

    Reject μ0=4 and μ0=8, both outside the interval; don't reject μ0=5.

  12. The false discovery rate. In a field where 20% of tested hypotheses are true, studies have power 0.5 and use α=0.05. What fraction of significant results are false? What if power rises to 0.9?

    Out of 1,000 hypotheses: 200 true (how many detected?) and 800 null (how many false alarms?).

    Power 0.5: 100 true detections and 40 false alarms, so 40/140≈29% false. Power 0.9: 180 and 40, so 40/220≈18%.

  13. Chi-squared for a die. A die is rolled 120 times, giving 1 to 6 with counts 15, 18, 22, 17, 21, 27. Use Pearson's chi-squared test (Section 9.8) to test whether the die is fair.

    Each expected count is 20; 6 categories, no parameters estimated, so 5 degrees of freedom. chisq.test(c(15, 18, 22, 17, 21, 27)).

    X2=(25+4+4+9+1+49)/20=4.6 on 5 df, p≈0.47. No evidence against fairness.

  14. A trial or an observational study? (Compare Chapter 1, Exercise 7.) A city finds that neighbourhoods with more street lights had significantly fewer night-time robberies (p=0.001). (a) Does this show that lighting reduces robbery? (b) Design a study that could.

    Think of confounders (Chapter 1) and of how to randomize.

    (a) No: wealthier neighbourhoods may have both more lights and fewer robberies; significance only rules out sampling chance. (b) Randomly choose which of a set of similar neighbourhoods get new lighting, and compare robberies before and after with the others. A study like this was carried out in New York City public housing developments, starting in 2016.
