0.1 What this course is about

Every day, people and machines make decisions without knowing what will happen next:

  • an airline decides how many tickets to sell;
  • a hospital decides how many nurses to schedule;
  • your phone decides which word you're about to type;
  • a forensic analyst decides whether two glass fragments could have come from the same window.

None of them can see the future. All of them use patterns in past data to make a good guess, and all of them have to live with being wrong some of the time.

This course is about doing that well. It has two big ideas.

  1. Prediction. Using what we have seen to make a good guess about what we haven't, with errors small enough to live with.
  2. Inference. Using data to learn about the mechanism that produced it: how strong a drug is, how often a test is wrong, whether a policy changed anything. Knowing the mechanism tells us how the world behaved in the past and how it will behave in the future.

The language for both is probability, so a large part of the course builds that language: events, random variables, distributions. Then we use it to estimate unknown quantities, say how sure we are, and test claims.

By the end of the course you should be able to:

  • choose a sensible probability model for a real situation, and say what has to be true for it to work;
  • describe and visualize data honestly, in R;
  • compute and interpret probabilities, expected values and standard deviations;
  • estimate a quantity from data, attach a margin of error, and test a claim, explaining in plain words what your answer means;
  • recognize when a pattern in data does, and does not, tell you about cause and effect.

The examples come from fields where these ideas matter every day: forensic science, toxicology, criminal justice, computer science and machine learning, and experimental science in general. Wherever we can, they use real data, with the source given so you can check it yourself.

Think About It: Prediction or Inference?

For each question, decide whether it is mainly about prediction (a good guess about a particular unseen case) or inference (learning about the mechanism that produces the data). (a) Will it rain in Manhattan tomorrow? (b) Does a new breath-test device read higher, on average, than the old one? (c) Which of this week's emails are spam? (d) Does adding street lighting reduce burglaries?

(a) Prediction: one unseen outcome, and a forecaster is judged by how often she's right, not by why. (b) Inference: the question is about the device's systematic behaviour, not any one reading. (c) Prediction: a spam filter only has to sort emails correctly; it can do that using patterns (such as the word "winner") without any theory of why spammers write what they write. (d) Inference, and a causal one: we want to know what lighting does, which is harder than noticing that well-lit streets have fewer burglaries (they may also be richer streets). The two goals often blend: knowing the mechanism usually improves prediction, and a good prediction rule can hint at a mechanism. But they need different kinds of evidence, and this course keeps asking which one a question is after.

History: Where Ideas Come from

History boxes tell the story of where an idea came from and who argued about it.

Some of that story is uncomfortable. Several of the people who built modern statistics, including Francis Galton, Karl Pearson and Ronald Fisher, were leading supporters of eugenics: the movement to "improve" the human population by controlling who had children. They developed some of their most famous tools while studying heredity for that purpose. These notes don't hide that history.

The tools themselves are among the most valuable ideas in science. Correlation, regression, the standard deviation and significance tests are now used to test vaccines, to free wrongly convicted people and to detect discrimination. The same tools were once used to give racist and classist policies a look of scientific authority, and they can be misused in the same way today. That is one of the best reasons to learn statistics well: people who understand how a number was produced are much harder to mislead with it.

One small change follows from this history. These notes call the bell-shaped distribution the Gaussian distribution, not by its more common name, the normal distribution; Section 5.3 explains why. Every other term is the standard one. Where a standard name has a troubling or confusing origin, a history box says so.

0.4 What you should know from calculus

This is a calculus-based course, but the calculus we use is a small, friendly part of it. Here is what you need, why you need it, and a worked example of each. If anything feels rusty, do the check-yourself questions at the end of this section before Chapter 3.

Exponentials and logarithms

The exponential function ex and the natural logarithm lnx are inverses: elnx=x for x>0, and ln(ex)=x for every x.

Theorem: Rules of Exponentials and Logarithms

For a,b>0 and any real r, s: er+s=eres,ln(ab)=lna+lnb,ln(ab)=lna−lnb,ln(ar)=rlna.er+s=eres,ln(ab)=lna+lnb,ln(ab)=lna−lnb,ln(ar)=rlna.

Where it shows up. The logarithm turns products into sums. In Chapter 8, the probability of a whole dataset is a product of many small probabilities. Taking logs turns it into a sum, which is far easier to work with.

Example 0.1: Logs Turn Products Into Sums

Simplify ln(p3(1−p)7) for 0<p<1.

The expression inside the log is a product of two factors, p3 and (1−p)7, so the product rule splits it into a sum of two logs. Then the power rule brings each exponent down in front: ln(p3(1−p)7)=ln(p3)+ln((1−p)7)(log of a product)=3lnp+7ln(1−p).(log of a power)ln(p3(1−p)7)=ln(p3)+ln((1−p)7)(log of a product)=3lnp+7ln(1−p).(log of a power) Check with numbers: at p=0.5, the left side is ln(0.510)=ln(0.000977)≈−6.93, and the right side is 10ln0.5≈10×(−0.693)=−6.93. In Chapter 8 this expression is the log-likelihood for a dataset with 3 successes and 7 failures. Without logs we would have to differentiate a product of powers; with logs, each piece is differentiated on its own.

Sums

We write long sums with sigma notation: ∑i=1nxi=x1+x2+⋯+xn. Read it as "add up xi as i runs from 1 to n". Two rules do most of the work: ∑i=1n(axi+b)=a∑i=1nxi+nb,∑i=1n(xi+yi)=∑i=1nxi+∑i=1nyi. The first rule says constants can be pulled out of a sum, and that adding b to each of n terms adds nb in total. For example, with x1,x2,x3=1,2,3, we get ∑i=13(2xi+5)=7+9+11=27, and the rule gives the same answer, 2×6+3×5=27. We will use these rules in Chapter 2 to show how the average changes when every value is rescaled, for example converting temperatures from °C to °F.

Two infinite sums appear again and again.

Theorem: Two Series You Will Meet Often

  1. Geometric series. If |r|<1, then ∑k=0∞rk=1+r+r2+⋯=11−r.
  2. Exponential series. For every real λ, ∑k=0∞λkk!=1+λ+λ22!+⋯=eλ.

For the geometric series, let Sn=1+r+⋯+rn. Then rSn=r+r2+⋯+rn+1, and subtracting, almost everything cancels: Sn−rSn=1−rn+1, so Sn=1−rn+11−r. If |r|<1 then rn+1→0 as n→∞, so Sn→11−r.

The exponential series is the Taylor series of eλ at 0: every derivative of ex is ex, which equals 1 at x=0. Taylor's theorem shows the series converges to eλ for every λ.∎

Where it shows up. Probabilities of all possible outcomes must add up to 1. To check that for distributions with infinitely many outcomes (Chapter 4), we need these two series.

Example 0.2: Probabilities That Add to One

In Chapter 4 we'll model the number of calls to a 911 dispatch centre in a minute by saying the chance of exactly k calls is e−λλk/k! for k=0,1,2,…. Show that these chances add up to 1.

The factor e−λ doesn't depend on k, so it comes out of the sum (the first sum rule, with a=e−λ), and what's left is the exponential series: ∑k=0∞e−λλkk!=e−λ∑k=0∞λkk!(pull out the constant)=e−λeλ(exponential series)=1.∑k=0∞e−λλkk!=e−λ∑k=0∞λkk!(pull out the constant)=e−λeλ(exponential series)=1. A numerical check helps make this feel real. With λ=2, the first few terms are e−2(1+2+2+1.333+0.667+⋯)≈0.135+0.271+0.271+0.180+0.090+⋯, and they already add to about 0.95 after five terms. The remaining terms make up the last 0.05.

Derivatives and finding the best value

You need the usual rules (power, product, quotient, chain) and the derivatives of xn, ex and lnx: ddxxn=nxn−1,ddxeax=aeax,ddxlnx=1x.

Where it shows up. In statistics we constantly look for the best value of something: the guess that makes the data most probable (Chapter 8), or the decision with the smallest expected cost. To find the highest point of a smooth curve, set its derivative to zero.

Example 0.3: Finding the Best Value

For 0<p<1, find the value of p that maximizes L(p)=p3(1−p)7.

Step 1: take logs. Maximizing L(p) is the same as maximizing lnL(p), because ln is an increasing function: whichever p makes L biggest also makes lnL biggest. The log is much easier to differentiate (Example 0.1): ℓ(p)=lnL(p)=3lnp+7ln(1−p).

Step 2: differentiate. The derivative of lnp is 1/p. For ln(1−p), the chain rule gives 11−p×(−1). So ℓ′(p)=3p−71−p.

Step 3: set the derivative to zero and solve. 3p−71−p=0⇒3p=71−p⇒3(1−p)=7p(cross-multiply)⇒3=10p⇒p=310.3p−71−p=0⇒3p=71−p⇒3(1−p)=7p(cross-multiply)⇒3=10p⇒p=310.

Step 4: check it's a maximum, not a minimum. Differentiate again: ℓ″(p)=−3p2−7(1−p)2. Both terms are negative for every p between 0 and 1, so the curve bends downwards everywhere, and the flat point must be a peak.

A picture confirms it:

R
p <- seq(0, 1, by = 0.001)
L <- p^3 * (1 - p)^7
ggplot(data.frame(p, L), aes(x = p, y = L)) +
  geom_line() +
  geom_vline(xintercept = 0.3, linetype = "dashed") +
  labs(x = "p", y = "L(p)")

So the best value is p=0.3: exactly the fraction of successes in the data, 3 out of 10. That is no coincidence, as Chapter 8 will show. Try the widget below with other numbers of successes and failures.

The top of a hill has slope zero

The curve is ℓ(p) = a ln p + b ln(1 − p) from Example 0.3, for a dataset with a successes and b failures. The straight line touches the curve at the point you choose; its steepness is the derivative ℓ′(p). Slide p along the curve. Where the line is uphill (positive slope) the top is further right; where it is downhill the top is to the left. The highest point is exactly where the line goes flat.

Integrals and areas

The integral ∫abf(x)dx is the area under the curve y=f(x) between a and b (counting area below the axis as negative). The Fundamental Theorem of Calculus says that if F′(x)=f(x), then ∫abf(x)dx=F(b)−F(a).

Where it shows up. For measurements like time, weight or concentration, probabilities are areas under a curve (Chapter 5). The probability that a measurement lands between a and b is ∫abf(x)dx.

An integral is a sum of thin rectangles

Pick a curve and an interval. The integral is the area under the curve, and we approximate it with rectangles. Add more rectangles and the approximation converges to the exact value. In this course, areas under curves will be probabilities: the first two curves are probability densities you'll meet in Chapter 5, so their total area is exactly 1.

You need three techniques: antiderivatives of the basic functions, substitution, and integration by parts, ∫udv=uv−∫vdu, plus improper integrals, where a limit is infinite: ∫0∞f(x)dx=limb→∞∫0bf(x)dx.

Example 0.4: A Total Area of One

In Chapter 5, the time (in hours) until a lab instrument fails is modelled by the curve f(x)=λe−λx for x≥0, where λ>0 is a constant. Show that the total area under the curve is 1.

Find an antiderivative. An antiderivative of λe−λx is −e−λx. Check by differentiating: the chain rule gives ddx(−e−λx)=−e−λx×(−λ)=λe−λx.

Handle the infinite upper limit. An integral up to ∞ means: integrate up to some large number b, then let b grow without bound. ∫0∞λe−λxdx=limb→∞[−e−λx]0b=limb→∞(−e−λb−(−e0))=limb→∞(1−e−λb)=1,∫0∞λe−λxdx=limb→∞[−e−λx]0b=limb→∞(−e−λb−(−e0))=limb→∞(1−e−λb)=1, because e−λb→0 as b→∞ (for λ>0). Notice that the answer doesn't depend on λ: whatever the failure rate, the total area is 1. That is what makes this curve a valid probability model, as Chapter 5 will explain. In the widget above, choose the exponential curve and move the to b slider all the way to the right to watch the area approach 1.

Example 0.5: Integration by Parts

Compute ∫0∞xλe−λxdx. (In Chapter 5 this will be the average time until failure.)

Choose the parts. Integration by parts helps when the integrand is a product of something that gets simpler when differentiated (x) and something easy to integrate (λe−λx). So take u=x,dv=λe−λxdx,sodu=dx,v=−e−λx.

Apply the formula ∫udv=uv−∫vdu: ∫0∞xλe−λxdx=[−xe−λx]0∞−∫0∞(−e−λx)dx=[−xe−λx]0∞+∫0∞e−λxdx.∫0∞xλe−λxdx=[−xe−λx]0∞−∫0∞(−e−λx)dx=[−xe−λx]0∞+∫0∞e−λxdx.

Evaluate each piece. The first term is 0: it is 0 at x=0, and xe−λx→0 as x→∞ because the exponential shrinks faster than x grows. The second integral is Example 0.4 divided by λ: ∫0∞e−λxdx=1λ∫0∞λe−λxdx=1λ×1=1λ. So the answer is 1λ. If an instrument fails at rate λ=0.01 per hour, it lasts 1/0.01=100 hours on average. That makes sense: a lower failure rate means a longer average lifetime.

Double integrals

When two measurements are uncertain at once (Chapter 6), probabilities become volumes under a surface, and we integrate twice: once in each variable, treating the other as a constant.

Example 0.6: Integrating Twice

Compute ∫01∫01(x+y)dxdy.

Work from the inside out. The inner integral is in x, so treat y as a fixed number, just like a constant c in ∫(x+c)dx: ∫01(x+y)dx=[x22+xy]x=0x=1=(12+y)−(0+0)=12+y.∫01(x+y)dx=[x22+xy]x=0x=1=(12+y)−(0+0)=12+y. The answer depends on y, as it should: we have integrated out x only.

The outer integral is now an ordinary integral in y: ∫01(12+y)dy=[y2+y22]01=12+12=1. Geometrically, this is the volume under the tilted roof z=x+y above the unit square. The roof's height ranges from 0 (at one corner) to 2 (at the opposite corner), and its average height is 1, so a volume of 1 makes sense.

Think About It: Why Bother with Calculus at All?

Many statistics courses use no calculus. Why might a course that wants you to understand where statistical methods come from need it? Think about Example 0.3: what would you have to do to find the best p without the derivative?

Without calculus you could only try values of p one by one (0.1, 0.2, 0.3, ...) and look for the biggest L(p). That works for one number, but not for proving that the answer is always the fraction of successes, whatever the data. Calculus turns "try lots of values" into a general formula, and a general formula is what lets us prove that a method works in every case, not just the ones we happened to try.

Key Takeaways

  • Logs turn products into sums; we'll use this to handle the probability of a whole dataset.
  • The geometric and exponential series let us add up infinitely many probabilities.
  • Setting a derivative to zero finds the best value of something; we'll use it to find best estimates.
  • For continuous measurements, probabilities are areas under curves, computed with integrals.

Think About It: Is It Safe to Ignore the Missing Days?

37 of the 153 days have no ozone reading. na.rm = TRUE simply drops them. When might that give a misleading average? Imagine why a reading might be missing.

Dropping missing values is harmless only if the missing days are like the others. If the monitor tended to fail on very hot days, or was switched off for maintenance at weekends when traffic is low, the remaining days would not be typical, and the average would be biased. This is the same question as "who ends up in the sample?" from Chapter 1: always ask why data are missing before deciding what to do about it.

Reading your own data

Most datasets you meet will be CSV files (comma-separated values), which any spreadsheet program can export. Read one with

R
mydata <- read.csv("lab_results.csv")

R looks for the file in its working directory. The easiest way to avoid "file not found" errors is to use an RStudio project (File → New Project) and keep your data in the project folder.

When things go wrong

Everyone gets errors. Most are one of these:

Error message Usual cause
object 'x' not found a typo, wrong capitalization (R is case-sensitive), or you haven't yet run the line that creates x
could not find function "ggplot" the package isn't loaded: run library(ggplot2)
unexpected ')' or unexpected symbol a missing or extra bracket, comma or quote
cannot open file 'data.csv' the file isn't in the working directory
the result is NA missing values in the data: add na.rm = TRUE

When you're stuck: read the error message slowly, check the line before the one that failed, and search the exact error message online.

Key Takeaways

  • Write code in a script, and run lines with Ctrl+Enter.
  • <- stores a value; c() makes a vector; most functions work on whole vectors.
  • A data frame has one row per individual and one column per variable; $ picks a column.
  • NA is a missing value; many functions need na.rm = TRUE.
  • Install a package once, then library() it every session.

0.7 Warm-up exercises

These are for checking your own readiness. If any of them feels hard, reread the matching part of Section 0.4 or 0.5 and try again.

Exercises

  1. Logs. Simplify ln(λne−nλ) for λ>0.

    Split the log of the product into two logs, then use ln(ar)=rlna and ln(er)=r.

    ln(λn)+ln(e−nλ)=nlnλ−nλ.

  2. Best value. Find the value of λ>0 that maximizes g(λ)=λ4e−2λ, and show it is a maximum.

    Maximize lng(λ)=4lnλ−2λ instead. Differentiate, set to zero, then check the sign of the second derivative.

    ddλ(4lnλ−2λ)=4λ−2=0 gives λ=2. The second derivative is −4/λ2<0, so it is a maximum.

  3. Series. Compute (a) ∑k=1∞(12)k and (b) ∑k=0∞0.2×0.8k.

    Both are geometric series. In (a) the sum starts at k=1, so subtract the k=0 term from 11−r. In (b), pull the constant 0.2 out of the sum first.

    (a) 11−1/2−1=2−1=1. (b) 0.2×11−0.8=0.2×5=1. Both add up to 1, so both could be lists of probabilities: we will meet (b) as the geometric distribution in Chapter 4.

  4. Areas. Let f(x)=3x2 for 0≤x≤1. Compute (a) ∫01f(x)dx and (b) ∫01xf(x)dx.

    An antiderivative of 3x2 is x3. For (b), the integrand is 3x3.

    (a) [x3]01=1. (b) ∫013x3dx=[34x4]01=34. In Chapter 5, (a) says f is a valid density and (b) is its average value.

  5. Parts twice. Show that ∫0∞x2λe−λxdx=2λ2.

    Use parts with u=x2 and dv=λe−λxdx. The boundary term vanishes, and what is left is 2∫0∞xe−λxdx. Relate that to Example 0.5.

    Parts gives [−x2e−λx]0∞+∫0∞2xe−λxdx=0+2λ∫0∞xλe−λxdx, which equals 2λ×1λ=2λ2, using Example 0.5.

  6. R practice. Using airquality, find (a) the average temperature in July (Month 7), and (b) the number of days on which the wind speed was above 15 mph.

    For (a), pick out the July temperatures with square brackets: airquality$Temp[airquality$Month == 7], then take the mean(). For (b), use sum() on a TRUE/FALSE comparison.

    (a) mean(airquality$Temp[airquality$Month == 7]) gives 83.9 °F. (b) sum(airquality$Wind > 15) gives 10 days. (Note the double equals sign == for "is equal to"; a single = would be read as setting an argument.)
