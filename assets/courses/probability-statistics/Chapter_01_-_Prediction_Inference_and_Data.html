Chapter 1
Prediction, inference and data

Lectures 1–2 · why we collect data, how we collect it, and why that decides what it can tell us

Count the predictions that touched your life today. Your phone guessed the next word you would type. A streaming service guessed what you'd want to watch. A fast-food app offered you a deal it predicted would bring you back this week. Somewhere, an airline sold more tickets for your flight than the plane has seats, predicting that some passengers wouldn't show up.

None of these systems can see the future. Each one found a pattern in past data and bet that the pattern would continue. And each one was designed knowing it would sometimes be wrong: the deal you ignore, the word you delete, the passenger who has to be bumped. The goal is never to be right every time. It is to keep the cost of being wrong at a level everyone can live with.

That, in one sentence, is what statistics is for. This chapter sets up the whole course: what prediction and inference are, where data come from, and why the way data are collected decides what they can and cannot tell you.

There is very little mathematics in this chapter, and that is deliberate. The ideas here are the ones that decide whether a calculation means anything at all. A perfectly computed average of badly collected data is still a bad answer, and no formula later in the course can rescue it. So read this chapter the way a detective reads a crime scene: before asking what the numbers say, ask where the numbers came from.

Here is the route through the chapter.

  • Sections 1.1 and 1.2 separate the two big goals of statistics: prediction (guessing what will happen) and inference (learning the mechanism that makes it happen).
  • Sections 1.3 and 1.4 are about the raw material: who or what we measure (populations and samples), and what we measure about them (variables).
  • Section 1.5 asks the hardest question in the chapter: when does an association in data reveal a cause?
  • Section 1.6 introduces the assumption behind almost every method in the course, independence.
  • Section 1.7 puts it all on one map.

1.1 Predicting under uncertainty

The seat that isn't empty

Airlines know that some ticket holders don't show up. Fly with empty seats and you lose money you could have earned. So they sell a few more tickets than there are seats. Sell too many, and more people show up than can fit: someone is bumped, and the airline pays compensation (and gets bad press). The airline must decide today how many tickets to sell, without knowing who will turn up.

Two things make this decision possible:

  1. A pattern. From years of past flights, the airline knows roughly what fraction of ticket holders show up.
  2. A cost for each kind of mistake. An empty seat costs one lost fare. A bumped passenger costs compensation, a hotel, perhaps a lost customer.

With those two ingredients, the airline can choose the number of tickets that keeps the average cost of its mistakes as small as possible. Try it.

How many tickets should the airline sell?

A plane has 100 seats. Every ticket holder independently shows up with the same probability. Choose how many tickets to sell and what each kind of mistake costs. The top panel shows how likely each number of show-ups is: orange bars are flights where someone gets bumped. The bottom panel shows the average cost of the mistakes for every number of tickets you could sell. Find the number that keeps the errors cheapest, then fly some flights.

Notice three things.

  • Even the best choice still makes mistakes on many flights. Uncertainty doesn't go away; it gets managed.
  • When bumping becomes more expensive, the best number of tickets goes down. The right prediction depends on the cost of being wrong, not just on the data.
  • If passengers become less predictable, the airline needs a bigger safety margin. Uncertainty itself has a price.

Think About It: Whose Costs?

In the widget, you chose what an empty seat and a bump cost. In real life, who decides those numbers? The airline counts a bump as a compensation payment; the bumped passenger counts a missed wedding or job interview. If the airline used the passenger's costs instead of its own, would it sell more tickets or fewer? Whenever someone tells you a prediction rule is "optimal", it is worth asking: optimal for whom?

The passenger's cost of being bumped is usually much larger than the compensation the airline pays, so with the passenger's costs the bump would be more expensive and the best number of tickets would go down. "Optimal" always means optimal for a particular choice of loss, and somebody chose it. Regulations (such as compulsory compensation for bumped passengers) are partly a way of making a company's costs closer to the public's.

The same structure is everywhere. A pharmacy predicts how many doses of a vaccine to stock. A hospital predicts how many nurses it needs on a Friday night. A forensic lab predicts how long its backlog of DNA samples will take to clear. In every case there is a pattern learned from data, an unknown future, and a cost for each way of being wrong.

Definition: Prediction and Loss

A prediction rule is a function f that takes information we have, x, and returns a guess f(x) for a quantity y that we don't know yet.

A loss function L(y,ŷ)≥0 gives the cost of guessing ŷ when the truth turns out to be y. It is 0 when the guess is perfect.

A good prediction rule is one whose loss is small on average, over the situations in which it will be used.

The definition has three ingredients, and it helps to name them in the airline story.

  • The unknown y is the number of passengers who will show up.
  • The information x is everything known when tickets go on sale: the route, the day of the week, past show-up rates.
  • The loss measures how bad each outcome is. Here the airline doesn't just guess y; it makes a decision (how many tickets to sell) whose cost depends on y. With cE the cost of an empty seat and cB the cost of bumping one passenger, the loss on a 100-seat flight is L(y)={cE(100−y),y<100,0,y=100,cB(y−100),y>100. The top line counts the cost of the 100−y empty seats, the bottom line the cost of the y−100 bumped passengers, and the middle line is a perfect fit.

For instance, with cE=$250 and cB=$1000, a flight on which 97 people show up costs 3×$250=$750, and a flight on which 102 show up costs 2×$1000=$2000. The loss is lopsided: being two over is much worse than being three under. That lopsidedness is exactly why the best number of tickets is not simply "whatever makes the average turnout equal 100". We will make "small on average" precise in Chapter 4, where it becomes the expected value of the loss.

Example 1.1: How Many Tickets? ★ lecture example

A regional airline flies a 100-seat plane. Past records suggest each ticket holder shows up with probability about 0.9, independently of the others. The airline is considering selling 110 tickets.

  1. On average, how many passengers will show up?
  2. Use a computer simulation of 10,000 flights to estimate how often someone is bumped, the average number of empty seats, and the average number of bumped passengers.
  3. Is 110 a good number of tickets? What else would you need to know?

Reading the problem. Before computing anything, pick out the pieces. The unknown is the number of passengers who show up on a given flight. The information is the number of tickets (110) and the show-up probability (0.9). The decision is whether 110 is a sensible number to sell. Part 3 is a warning that the data alone may not settle that.

  1. Think of each ticket holder as contributing "0.9 of a passenger" on average. Adding up over 110 ticket holders, average number who show up=110×0.9=99. That sounds perfect for a 100-seat plane. But an average describes the typical flight, and it hides the flights that go wrong. On some flights only 93 people will turn up; on others, 104 will. The question is how often the second kind happens.

  2. Working out these chances exactly needs the binomial distribution (Chapter 4). For now we let R simulate the flights instead. The function rbinom (which we study properly in Chapter 4) counts how many of 110 independent passengers show up when each has probability 0.9, and does this once per flight.

R
set.seed(301)        # makes the simulation repeatable
# number who show up on each of 10,000 simulated flights
shows <- rbinom(10000, size = 110, prob = 0.9)
mean(shows)                  # average number who show up
mean(shows > 100)            # fraction with someone bumped
mean(pmax(100 - shows, 0))   # average empty seats
mean(pmax(shows - 100, 0))   # average bumped passengers
output
[1] 99.0012
[1] 0.3273
[1] 1.7871
[1] 0.7883

Each line does one job:

  • shows > 100 is TRUE on flights where someone is bumped, and mean() of a TRUE/FALSE vector is the fraction of TRUEs.
  • pmax(100 - shows, 0) is the number of empty seats on each flight: 100−shows when that's positive, and 0 otherwise. (pmax takes the larger of the two values, flight by flight.)
  • pmax(shows - 100, 0) similarly counts the bumped passengers.

The average is 99 show-ups, as we predicted. But on about a third of all flights (32.7%) more than 100 people show up and someone is bumped. On average there are about 1.8 empty seats and 0.8 bumped passengers per flight. A picture of the 10,000 flights shows where that third comes from:

  geom_vline(xintercept = 100.5, linetype = "dashed") +
  scale_fill_manual(values = c("grey60", "orange"),

  labs(x = "passengers who show up", y = "number of flights", fill = NULL)

The hump is centred at 99, but it is several passengers wide on each side. Everything to the right of the dashed line is a flight with an unhappy passenger. Selling 110 tickets puts the centre of the hump just under 100, so a large slice of it spills over.

  1. It depends on the costs. With cE=$250 and cB=$1000, the average cost per flight is $250 times the average number of empty seats plus $1000 times the average number of bumped passengers. From the simulation that is roughly 250×1.79+1000×0.79≈1240 dollars. (The exact figure, computed in Chapter 4, is $1240.) The widget above shows that, with these costs, 108 tickets keeps the average cost lowest, at about $1040 per flight, so 110 is a little too many. If instead an empty seat cost as much as a bump, the best number would rise to 111, and 110 would be a fine choice. The data tell us what will happen; only the costs can tell us what we should do.

What else would you need to know? Whether the 0.9 is right for this route and day (business flights on a Monday may differ from holiday flights); whether passengers really act independently (a family of five either all turns up or all doesn't, which makes big misses more likely); and what bumping really costs, including lost future customers.

History: There Is No Average Pilot

In the late 1940s the US Air Force was losing planes to crashes that weren't explained by mechanical failure. Cockpits had been designed in 1926 to fit the average pilot, using body measurements of hundreds of men. In 1950 the Air Force measured 4,063 pilots, and a young researcher, Gilbert Daniels, took ten of the body dimensions, such as height, chest and arm length, and asked how many pilots were close to average (roughly the middle 30% of pilots) on all ten.

The answer, published in 1952, was none. A cockpit built for the average pilot fitted nobody. The Air Force changed its approach: equipment had to be adjustable to fit pilots across a wide range of sizes. Car seats, steering wheels and airline seat designs followed the same idea. The idea of an "average man" as the model human being goes back to the 1830s and the Belgian scientist Adolphe Quetelet (Section 5.3 tells that story). Designing for the average is a prediction about one imaginary person. Designing for the variation is a prediction about everybody. This course is about the second kind.

Prediction as classification

Sometimes the quantity to predict isn't a number but a category: spam or not spam, guilty or not guilty, headlamp glass or window glass. A prediction rule for a category is called a classifier. There are now two ways to be wrong, and they rarely cost the same.

Example 1.2: Two Ways to Be Wrong

A spam filter predicts, for each incoming email, spam or not spam. Describe its two kinds of mistake. Which is worse, and how should that change the way the filter is designed?

A classifier with two categories can end up in four situations, because the prediction can be either category and so can the truth. It helps to lay them out in a table every time:

truly spam truly genuine
filter says spam correct: spam caught false alarm: genuine email hidden
filter says genuine miss: spam in your inbox correct: email delivered

The two diagonal cells are correct; the other two are the two kinds of mistake.

  • False alarm: a genuine email (a job offer, say) is sent to the spam folder. You may never see it.
  • Miss: a spam email lands in your inbox. You delete it, mildly annoyed.

For most people a false alarm is much worse. So a filter should only call something spam when the evidence is strong, accepting that some spam will slip through. A filter that wanted zero misses would have to send everything remotely suspicious to the spam folder, and you would lose real email; a filter that wanted zero false alarms would let everything through. You can't have both, so you choose a balance. A good filter isn't the one with the fewest mistakes overall. It is the one whose mistakes cost the least.

Two words of caution, which will come back again and again in this course.

  • We judged the rule on the same data we used to invent it. It will probably do a little worse on new fragments. Honest evaluation needs fresh data.
  • Which mistake is worse depends on the use. If the rule is a first screen that tells the lab which fragments to analyse more carefully, misses are worse than false alarms. If it were used as evidence in court, a false alarm (wrongly linking a fragment to a vehicle) could be very serious.

Forensic scientists combine several measurements to do much better than one, and report the strength of evidence rather than a yes/no answer. Machine learning methods for problems like this are exactly what the field of classification is about.

They never used it, so the $2 was never paid out. The $2 is only wasted if it's spent and achieves nothing.

Key Takeaways

  • A prediction is a guess about something not yet known, made from information we have.
  • Every prediction is wrong sometimes. The aim is to make the cost of the mistakes, on average, as small as possible.
  • The best prediction depends on the data and on the costs of the different kinds of error.
  • For a classifier, lay out the four outcomes in a table. Moving a cut-off trades misses for false alarms; no cut-off removes both.
  • Judge a prediction rule on data it hasn't seen.

1.2 Inference: learning the mechanism

The comet that came back

Prediction asks what will happen? There is a deeper question: why does it happen? What is the mechanism that produced the data, and how does it work?

The universe seems to run on rules. Planets keep to their orbits, drugs act on the body in consistent ways, coins land heads about half the time no matter who flips them. The data we collect are produced by these rules, blurred by randomness. If we can learn the rule from the data, we gain something prediction alone never gives us: we can explain the past, predict the future, and predict what would happen if we changed something.

History: Halley's Comet

In 1705 the astronomer Edmond Halley studied the recorded paths of comets. He noticed that the bright comets of 1531, 1607 and 1682 had almost identical orbits, about 76 years apart. Rather than three comets, he inferred one comet on a long elliptical orbit around the Sun, obeying Newton's new law of gravitation. From that mechanism he predicted the comet would return in late 1758.

Halley died in 1742. On Christmas night of 1758 an amateur astronomer in Germany, Johann Palitzsch, saw the comet return, as predicted. The same mechanism also reached backwards: astronomers have since matched the comet to sightings going back over two thousand years, including the one stitched into the Bayeux Tapestry in 1066. Learning the mechanism explained the past and predicted the future.

Most mechanisms we study are less tidy than gravity. A drug lowers blood pressure, but by different amounts in different people. A spam filter's errors depend on what spammers try next. So we think of data as the output of a machine with two parts: a systematic part, the rule, and a random part, the noise.

Definition: Data-Generating Process

The data-generating process (DGP) is the mechanism, partly systematic and partly random, that produced the data we observe. Its unknown numerical features, such as the average effect of a drug or the fraction of fragments containing barium, are called parameters.

Statistical inference is the use of data to draw conclusions about the data-generating process, including how uncertain those conclusions are.

To make the picture concrete, here is one way to write a data-generating process for the birth weights we meet in Example 1.4: birth weight⏟what we observe=μgroup⏟rule+ε⏟noise. Here μgroup is the average birth weight for babies in a given group (say, mothers who smoked), a fixed but unknown parameter, and ε ("epsilon") is everything else that makes one baby heavier or lighter than its group's average: genes, the length of the pregnancy, luck. We never see μ or ε separately, only their sum. Inference is the art of pulling them apart using many observations: the rule is what stays the same from baby to baby, and the noise is what doesn't.

The last four words of the definition matter. A conclusion without a measure of its uncertainty is not inference, it's a guess. Here is a mechanism you can't see inside. Learn about it the only way scientists ever can: by watching what it produces.

Prediction or inference?

Example 1.4: Smoking and Birth Weight ★ lecture example

    • Predictive: "Given what we know about a pregnant patient, how likely is her baby to be born below 2,500 g?" A hospital could use the answer to decide who needs extra monitoring. It doesn't matter why the prediction works, only that it is accurate.
    • Inferential: "In the population of all such pregnancies, how large is the difference in average birth weight between smokers and non-smokers, and how sure can we be?" Here we want a number describing the mechanism, with a margin of error.

  2. Not from this comparison alone. Mothers who smoked may differ from those who didn't in other ways (income, stress, diet, access to care) that also affect birth weight. The 284 g could be partly or even wholly due to those differences. Section 1.5 shows how to think about this. (From many other studies, including some cleverly designed ones, we do know that smoking during pregnancy reduces birth weight. But this dataset alone can't establish it.)

There is a third kind of question, the most ambitious of all.

Definition: Three Kinds of Question

  • A descriptive question asks what the data show: How many fragments contain barium?
  • A predictive question asks what an unseen outcome will be: Will this patient's baby be born underweight?
  • An inferential question asks about the data-generating process: What is the average birth weight in the population, and how sure are we? A causal question is an inferential question about what would happen if we intervened: How much heavier would babies be if mothers stopped smoking?

Example 1.5: What Kind of Question Is It?

Classify each question as descriptive, predictive, inferential, or causal.

  1. What fraction of the 214 glass fragments in fgl came from headlamps?
  2. Will the next email in my inbox be spam?
  3. Do police body cameras reduce complaints against officers?
  4. What is the average lead level in the drinking water of all NYC public schools, based on tests of 60 of them?
  5. Which patients arriving at an emergency room are likely to need intensive care in the next 24 hours?
  6. Would lowering the speed limit to 25 mph reduce pedestrian deaths in NYC?
  1. Descriptive: count and report; no uncertainty about these 214 fragments.
  2. Predictive: a guess about one unseen outcome.
  3. Causal: what happens because of the cameras.
  4. Inferential: from 60 schools to all of them, with a margin of error.
  5. Predictive: it doesn't matter why a patient is at risk, only that we identify them.
  6. Causal: what would happen if we intervened.

Some words are useful clues, though never a substitute for thinking:

clue in the question usually signals
"how many", "what fraction of these" descriptive
"will", "which ... is likely to", "the next" predictive
"all", "in general", "the population", "how sure" inferential
"cause", "effect", "reduce", "if we changed", "would" causal

Question 4 is the subtle one. The 60 tested schools could be described exactly, but the question asks about all schools, and that leap from a part to the whole is what makes it inferential.

Think About It: The Same Data, Different Questions

A hospital has records of 10,000 past emergency-room patients: their symptoms on arrival, their age, which treatment they received, and whether they needed intensive care. Write one descriptive, one predictive and one causal question these records could be used for. Which of your three questions do you think the records can answer most reliably, and which least? Why?

For example: descriptive, "what fraction of these patients needed intensive care?"; predictive, "will the patient who has just arrived need intensive care?"; causal, "does treatment A reduce the need for intensive care compared with treatment B?". The descriptive question is answered exactly by counting. The predictive question can be answered reasonably well if future patients resemble past ones. The causal question is the hardest: doctors chose the treatments, probably giving the stronger treatment to sicker patients, so a comparison of outcomes mixes the treatment's effect with how sick the patients were. Section 1.5 shows why this matters.

Bayesian Inference: Two Ways to Be Uncertain About a Mechanism

In the mystery machine, the true fraction of orange is a fixed number we don't know. There are two schools of thought about how to express our uncertainty about it.

  • The frequentist view (the main thread of this course) treats the parameter as fixed and the data as random. It asks: how would my method behave if I repeated the experiment many times? "The plausible range" means a range produced by a method that captures the truth, say, 95% of the time.
  • The Bayesian view treats our knowledge of the parameter as uncertain and describes it with probabilities. Before spinning, you might believe any fraction is equally likely. Each spin updates that belief, and you can say things like "there is a 90% probability the fraction is between 0.3 and 0.5."

Both are used in science and industry every day, and they often give similar answers. These boxes will show you the Bayesian version of each idea as we go.

Interlude (Optional): Why Should the Future Resemble the Past?

Every prediction and every inference in this course rests on an assumption that is impossible to prove: that the mechanism which produced our data will keep working the same way. The Scottish philosopher David Hume pointed this out in the 1730s and 1740s. We have seen the sun rise every morning, but no amount of past sunrises logically proves it will rise tomorrow. This is the problem of induction.

Bertrand Russell told it as a story about a chicken. Every day of its life, the farmer arrived and fed it, so the chicken grew confident that the farmer's arrival meant food. Then one day the farmer arrived and wrung its neck.

Statistics doesn't solve Hume's problem. It makes it manageable. We assume the mechanism is stable, we say exactly what we are assuming, and we check how badly things go wrong when the assumption fails. And it does fail: in 2020, many forecasting models for travel, shopping and hospital demand broke overnight when the pandemic changed the mechanism behind the data. A model is only as good as the stability of the world it was trained on. Remember the chicken.

Key Takeaways

  • The data-generating process is the mechanism, rule plus noise, behind the data; its unknown numbers are parameters.
  • Inference learns about the mechanism from data, with a statement of uncertainty.
  • Prediction cares whether a guess is accurate; inference cares what the mechanism is; causal inference asks what would happen if we intervened.
  • With more data the plausible range for a parameter narrows, but never to a single point.

1.3 Populations, samples and how we choose them

We can't measure everything

A police department seizes 2,000 small bags of white powder in a raid. To charge the suspects, the lab must show the bags contain an illegal drug. Testing every bag would take weeks. Instead, the lab tests a few dozen and draws a conclusion about all 2,000.

This is the basic move of statistics: look at a part to learn about the whole. Whether it works depends almost entirely on which part we look at.

Definition: Population, Sample, Parameter, Statistic

  • The population is the entire collection of individuals (people, objects, events) we want to learn about. Its members are called units.
  • A sample is the subset of the population we actually observe. Observing every unit is a census.
  • A parameter is a number describing the population, such as the fraction of all 2,000 bags that contain cocaine. It is usually unknown.
  • A statistic is a number computed from a sample, such as the fraction of the tested bags that contain cocaine. We use statistics to estimate parameters.

A memory aid: parameter goes with population, statistic goes with sample.

Choosing the sample

The simplest fair method treats every unit identically.

Definition: Simple Random Sample

A simple random sample (SRS) of size n from a population of N units is chosen so that every possible set of n units is equally likely to be the sample.

Drawing names from a well-shaken hat produces a simple random sample. So does R's sample() function. One consequence is the fairness property we want:

Theorem: Every Unit Has the Same Chance

In a simple random sample of size n from a population of N units, every unit has the same probability, n/N, of being included.

Idea. Count, in two different ways, how many (unit, sample) pairs there are with the unit belonging to the sample.

List every possible sample of size n; say there are M of them, all equally likely. The definition of an SRS treats all units the same way, so by symmetry every unit appears in the same number of these samples; call that number k.

Now count the pairs (unit, sample containing it).

  • Sample by sample: each of the M samples contains n units, giving nM pairs.
  • Unit by unit: each of the N units lies in k samples, giving Nk pairs.

Both counts describe the same collection of pairs, so Nk=nM⇒kM=nN. A particular unit is included exactly when the sample drawn is one of the k samples that contain it. All M samples are equally likely, so the probability of inclusion is the number of samples containing the unit divided by the number of possible samples: Pr(unit is included)=kM=nN.◼∎

A tiny case makes the theorem concrete. Take a population of N=4 units, {A,B,C,D}, and a simple random sample of n=2. The possible samples are {A,B},{A,C},{A,D},{B,C},{B,D},{C,D}, so M=6, each with probability 1/6. Unit A appears in k=3 of them, as does every other unit, so each unit is included with probability 3/6=1/2=n/N. Check the double count: nM=2×6=12 and Nk=4×3=12.

The theorem says every unit has the same chance. That's necessary for a fair sample but not enough on its own: for a list of 20 units, a method that tosses a coin to pick either the first 10 or the last 10 also gives every unit a 50% chance, but it never picks a mix. A simple random sample makes every combination equally likely, which is a much stronger guarantee.

Other designs are often more practical:

  • Stratified sample. Split the population into groups (strata) that differ in important ways, then take a simple random sample from each. A survey of NYC residents might sample separately from each of the five boroughs, so that every borough is represented.
  • Cluster sample. Randomly choose whole groups, then measure everyone in them. A health survey might randomly choose 30 apartment buildings and interview every household in each. Cheaper, but people in the same building tend to be similar, so you learn less per person.
  • Systematic sample. Take every kth unit from a list, starting at a random point. Easy, and usually fine, unless the list has a pattern that repeats every k units.
  • Convenience sample. Take whoever is easy to reach: your friends, the first 50 people through the door, an online poll. Cheap, fast, and often badly misleading.

Definition: Sampling Bias

A sampling method is biased if it systematically favours some parts of the population over others, in a way related to what is being measured, so that its statistics tend to be too high or too low for the parameter. Bias is a property of the method, not of one particular sample, and taking a bigger sample does not fix it.

See this for yourself. A researcher in New York who wants a quick estimate might only collect data from nearby states.

History: The Poll That Ended a Magazine

In 1936 the Literary Digest, a popular American magazine, conducted the largest poll in history. It mailed about 10 million ballots, using lists of its own readers, telephone owners and car registrations, and received about 2.4 million back. It predicted that the Republican Alf Landon would beat President Franklin Roosevelt, with about 57% of the vote.

Roosevelt won with about 61% and carried 46 of the 48 states. Meanwhile, a young pollster named George Gallup predicted a Roosevelt win using a sample of around 50,000.

What went wrong? In the middle of the Great Depression, people who owned phones and cars and subscribed to magazines were richer than average, and richer voters leaned Republican. And of the 10 million, only a quarter replied, and those who replied were not typical of those who didn't. A sample of 2.4 million, collected with a biased method, lost to one fifty times smaller collected with a better one. The Digest closed within two years.

Causal Inference: Who Ends Up in the Sample?

Sampling bias is a causal problem in disguise. Ask: what causes a unit to end up in the sample? In the 1936 poll, wealth caused people to be on the mailing lists, and wealth also affected how they voted. Whenever the thing that puts units into your sample is connected to the thing you're measuring, your sample is biased. Random sampling works because nothing about the unit causes it to be chosen: a random number generator decides. We will draw this as a diagram in Section 1.5.

Example 1.7: Naming the Parts

For each study, identify the population, the sample, the parameter of interest and the statistic used to estimate it. Then say whether the sampling method could be biased, and how.

  1. A crime lab receives 2,000 seized bags. It tests a simple random sample of 40 and finds that 38 contain cocaine.
  2. A software company wants to know the average page-load time its users experience. It measures load times on 500 visits from computers in its own office.

  2. Population: all visits by all users. Sample: 500 visits from the office. Parameter: the average load time over all user visits. Statistic: the average of the 500. Biased: office computers probably have fast connections close to the company's servers, so the sample underestimates what typical users experience. Measuring 5,000 office visits would not help.
  3. Population: all cans of tuna on sale in NYC. Sample: 25 cans. Parameter: the average mercury level per can. Statistic: the average of the 25. This is a two-stage design (random stores, then one can from each). It's reasonable if stores are chosen randomly, but one can per store can't show how much cans vary within a store, and it ignores small shops, corner stores and online sales, which might stock different brands.

1.4 Variables, data and measurement

Once we have a sample, we measure things about each unit. Data almost always arrive as a table: one row per unit, one column per measurement. Statisticians call this shape tidy data, and R stores it as a data frame (Section 0.5).

Definition: Variables and Their Types

A variable is a characteristic recorded for each unit; its value can vary from unit to unit. Variables come in two main types.

  • A categorical variable places each unit in a group.
    • It is nominal if the groups have no natural order (glass type; blood type).
    • It is ordinal if they do (pain on a scale of mild, moderate, severe; a letter grade).
  • A numerical (quantitative) variable is a number for which arithmetic makes sense.
    • It is discrete if its possible values can be listed, usually counts (number of arrests; number of previous premature labours).
    • It is continuous if it can take any value in an interval, usually measurements (birth weight; refractive index; time).

A quick test sorts most variables. Ask two questions in turn.

  1. Does arithmetic make sense? If "the average value" means something, the variable is numerical. If the values are labels (even labels written as numbers, like a jersey number or a ZIP code), it is categorical.
  2. If categorical: is there a natural order? Then ordinal, otherwise nominal. If numerical: can you list the possible values, with gaps between them? Then discrete, otherwise continuous.

The type of a variable decides which summaries and plots make sense (Chapter 2) and which probability models fit it (Chapters 4 and 5). An average ZIP code, or a histogram of blood types, is a sign that someone skipped this step. Getting it right is the first step of every analysis.

Think About It: Measuring the Invisible

A city reports that burglaries fell by 20% after a new policing strategy started. List at least two ways the measurement of burglary could have changed, even if the true number of burglaries didn't. What extra information would you want before believing the 20%?

Interlude (Optional): What Does It Mean to Measure Something?

To measure is to attach numbers to things so that relationships between the numbers mirror relationships between the things. A ruler works because if rod A is longer than rod B, A's number is bigger. The psychologist S. S. Stevens argued in 1946 that this is why the type of variable matters. Some measurements only preserve categories (nominal), some preserve order (ordinal), some preserve differences (temperature in °F: 80° is 10° warmer than 70°, but not "8/7 as hot"), and some preserve ratios (weight: 2 kg really is twice 1 kg). Arithmetic that doesn't respect the structure produces numbers that mean nothing, like the average race code above.

Even the most basic units are human decisions. Since 1983, the metre has been defined as the distance light travels in 1/299,792,458 of a second. We chose to fix the speed of light and measure length with it.

And no measurement is perfect. Every reading of a refractive index, a blood alcohol level or a DNA concentration is the true value plus some measurement error. Forensic labs spend a great deal of effort estimating exactly how large that error is, because a conviction can hinge on whether a reading of 0.081 really means "above 0.08." Measurement error is noise in the data-generating process, and much of this course is about reasoning through it.

Even famous datasets contain mistakes. R's own documentation for USArrests notes a transcription error in Maryland's urban population figure, found decades after the data were published.

1.5 Where data come from: experiments, observation and causes

Association is not causation, and why

In Example 1.4 the babies of smokers were 284 g lighter on average. Does smoking cause lower birth weight? The difficulty is that smokers and non-smokers might differ in other ways. Suppose hardship (low income, stress, poor access to prenatal care) makes a mother more likely to smoke and also lowers birth weight by itself. Then smokers' babies would be lighter on average even if smoking itself did nothing.

The clearest way to think about this is to draw what influences what.

Definition: Causal Diagram (DAG)

A directed acyclic graph (DAG) is a collection of nodes (variables) joined by arrows, with no route that follows the arrows and returns to where it started. In a causal DAG, an arrow A→B means that A directly influences B, and every common cause of two variables in the diagram is also included in it.

A confounder of the relationship between an exposure X and an outcome Y is a common cause of both: a variable with a route of arrows into X, and a route of arrows into Y that does not pass through X.

A confounder creates an association between X and Y that has nothing to do with X causing Y. The diagram shows two routes connecting smoking and birth weight: the direct arrow (the effect we want), and the back route through hardship (confounding). A simple comparison of averages mixes the two together.

A made-up example shows how strong the back route can be. Suppose, in an imaginary town, smoking has no effect at all on birth weight. Hardship lowers the average birth weight by 300 g, and mothers facing hardship are much more likely to smoke:

group smokers others average weight
hardship 60 40 2,900 g
no hardship 20 80 3,200 g

(Each group has 100 mothers; "average weight" is the average birth weight of their babies.)

Within each row, smokers and non-smokers have the same average, because smoking does nothing. Now ignore the rows and compare all smokers with all non-smokers.

  • Smokers: 60 at 2,900 g and 20 at 3,200 g, so their average is 60×2900+20×320080=238,00080=2975 g.
  • Non-smokers: 40 at 2,900 g and 80 at 3,200 g, so their average is 40×2900+80×3200120=372,000120=3100 g.

Smokers' babies appear 125 g lighter, even though smoking does nothing in this town. The whole difference travelled along the back route: smokers are mostly from the hardship group, and that is why their babies are lighter. Comparing within each row (like with like) gives the right answer, zero. The widget lets you build your own version of this and watch the apparent effect appear from nowhere.

When a third variable does the talking

A simulated study of 400 pregnancies, built so that we know the truth. Hardship (low income, stress, poor access to care) makes smoking more likely and lowers birth weight by itself. Smoking also lowers birth weight directly, by the amount you choose. Compare what an observational study would see with the true effect of smoking. Then randomize: let a coin, not hardship, decide who smokes. (Of course, no one could ethically run that experiment on real mothers. That is exactly why this problem is hard.)

Observational studies and experiments

Definition: Observational Study and Experiment

  • In an observational study, the researcher records what happens without deciding who receives which treatment or exposure. The units choose for themselves, or circumstances choose for them.
  • In an experiment, the researcher decides who receives which treatment.
  • In a randomized experiment, the researcher uses a chance device (a coin, a random number generator) to decide. A randomized experiment comparing a treatment group with a control group is called a randomized controlled trial (RCT).

Why does randomization matter so much? Because a coin can't be influenced by anything about the person.

Theorem: Randomization Breaks Confounding

Suppose each unit is assigned to treatment or control by a chance device whose outcome does not depend on any characteristic of the unit. Then treatment assignment is independent of every characteristic the units had before treatment, measured or not. In particular, with large groups, the treatment and control groups have very similar distributions of every such characteristic, and a systematic difference in their outcomes can only be caused by the treatment assignment: by the treatment itself, or by people knowing who was treated. That second possibility is why good trials are blinded.

Let U be any characteristic fixed before treatment (age, income, genes, anything). The chance device decides treatment without looking at U, so for every value u, Pr(treated∣U=u)=Pr(treated). That is exactly what it means for treatment to be independent of U (Chapter 3 makes this precise). Because the device never looks at the unit at all, the same is true for the whole collection of characteristics taken together, not just one at a time. So the treated and untreated groups are random samples from the same population. They can differ only by chance, and chance differences shrink as the groups grow (Chapter 7).

So any confounder's arrow into treatment is cut: in the DAG, the only arrow into "treatment" comes from the coin. Any remaining association between treatment and outcome must flow through the arrow from treatment to outcome, or be due to chance. Chapter 9 shows how to judge whether a difference is larger than chance can explain.∎

Think About It: Why Not Just Ask Permission?

In the randomized design, some parents agreed to the trial knowing their child might get salt water instead of the vaccine. Was it ethical to give 200,000 children a placebo when a promising vaccine existed? What would have been lost if everyone who consented had simply been vaccinated? There is no single right answer here; try to argue both sides.

Against the placebo: if the vaccine worked, some placebo children caught polio who might have been protected. In this trial, the placebo group's rate (71 per 100,000) against the vaccinated group's (28) suggests that about 86 children in a group of 200,000 got paralytic polio who might not have. For the placebo: nobody knew in 1954 whether the vaccine worked or was even safe, and the observed-control design, as we saw, gave a muddied answer. A clear answer meant the vaccine could be confidently given to millions of children the following year, and that a harmful vaccine would have been detected. Modern ethics rules allow placebo controls only when there is genuine uncertainty about which treatment is better (called equipoise) and no proven treatment is being withheld.

Causal Inference: Drawing the Diagram First

Here is the habit this course will build. Before looking at any data, draw the DAG. List the exposure and the outcome, then ask: what else causes the exposure? What else causes the outcome? Anything that causes both is a confounder, and a fair comparison must deal with it, either by randomizing (cutting its arrow) or by comparing like with like (for example, comparing smokers and non-smokers within the same income level). Some arrows shouldn't be adjusted for at all: if smoking lowers birth weight by damaging the placenta, holding placental health fixed would hide the very effect you want to measure. We'll keep drawing these diagrams in every chapter.

History: From Scurvy to Streptomycin

In 1747 the Scottish naval surgeon James Lind took twelve sailors suffering from scurvy aboard HMS Salisbury and divided them into six pairs, giving each pair a different remedy: cider, vinegar, sea water, oranges and lemons, and others. The pair given citrus fruit recovered dramatically. It's often called one of the first controlled trials, though with two patients per group it was a tiny one.

Randomization came much later. Ronald Fisher argued for it in agricultural experiments in the 1920s and 1930s, randomly assigning fertilizers to plots of land. In 1948 the British Medical Research Council published a trial, designed by the statistician Austin Bradford Hill, in which tuberculosis patients were randomly allocated to the new antibiotic streptomycin plus bed rest, or to bed rest alone. It is often called the first modern randomized controlled trial. Today almost no new drug is approved without one.

Interlude (Optional): Randomized Trials: The Gold Standard and Its Limits

Randomized controlled trials are called the gold standard because they answer causal questions without needing to know, or even guess, the confounders. But they have limits.

  • Ethics. We can't randomly assign people to smoke, or to live near a polluting factory, or to be arrested. Many of the most important questions in toxicology and criminal justice can only be studied observationally.
  • Who takes part. Trial volunteers are often healthier or more motivated than the patients who will later use the treatment, so results may not carry over.
  • Compliance. People assigned to a treatment may not take it.

So scientists also look for natural experiments, where chance or an arbitrary rule assigns the "treatment" for them: a lottery for school places, a policy that starts on a particular date, a birthday that falls just before or after a cut-off. A whole field, causal inference, is devoted to drawing causal conclusions from data that weren't randomized, using diagrams like the ones in this section. The Book of Why by Pearl and Mackenzie is a readable introduction.

Key Takeaways

  • An association between X and Y can come from X causing Y, or from a confounder causing both.
  • A causal DAG draws what influences what. A confounder is a common cause of exposure and outcome.

  • Randomization makes the treatment independent of every pre-existing characteristic, so it removes confounding, even by things nobody measured.

1.6 Independence and IID data

Almost every method in this course assumes that each observation brings new information. If you measure the refractive index of the same glass fragment five times, you learn a lot about that fragment and the measuring instrument, but very little about glass in general. Five fragments from five different windows tell you far more.

Here is the idea in everyday language; Chapter 3 makes it precise with probability.

Definition: Independence and IID (Informal)

  • Observations are independent if learning the values of some of them doesn't change what we expect from the others.
  • They are identically distributed if each one is produced by the same mechanism.
  • Observations that are both are called IID ("independent and identically distributed"). A simple random sample from a large population gives (approximately) IID observations, and the word random sample in later chapters will mean exactly this.

Independence is surprisingly hard to fake. People asked to write down "random" coin flips almost always switch between H and T too often, because a run of five heads feels non-random. Independent flips don't remember what came before, so they produce longer runs than our intuition expects. Try it.

Can you fake a coin?

Without flipping anything, invent a "random" sequence of 50 coin flips by pressing the H and T buttons (or the H and T keys while a button is focused). Then compare your sequence with 5,000 sequences from a genuinely fair, independent coin. Two features give fakers away: how often the sequence switches between H and T, and the longest run of the same face.

This isn't just a party trick. Auditors and forensic accountants use the same idea to spot invented numbers in expense reports and fabricated data in scientific papers: made-up data are usually too regular.

Why are long runs so common? Each flip has a 1 in 2 chance of matching the one before it, so once a flip has landed, the chance that the next four all match it (making a run of five) is 12×12×12×12=116. That is unlikely at any one place, but in 50 flips there are 46 places where a run of five could start. Simulation shows that about 4 out of every 5 sequences of 50 fair flips contain a run of five or more identical faces. Your intuition judges each run on its own ("five in a row, how unlikely!") instead of counting all the places one could have happened. We will be able to compute these chances properly in Chapter 3.

Think About It: When Is the Same Thing Measured Twice?

A study of a new reading program measures 400 children: 20 children in each of 20 classrooms. A second study measures 400 children, each from a different school. Both report "n = 400". Key Takeaways

  • Independent observations don't inform each other; identically distributed observations come from the same mechanism; together they are IID.

1.7 The map of the course

We can now see the whole course in one picture. There is a population, or more generally a data-generating process, that we want to understand. We observe a sample of data from it.

  • Describing the data (Chapter 2): summaries and pictures of what we actually observed.
  • Probability (Chapters 3–7) reasons forwards: if we knew the mechanism, what kinds of data would it produce, and how much would they vary from sample to sample?
  • Inference (Chapters 8–9) reasons backwards: having seen the data, what can we say about the mechanism, and how sure can we be?
  • Prediction uses what we've learned to guess the next case.

Backwards reasoning is only possible because we first understand forwards reasoning. To judge whether 38 cocaine-positive bags out of 40 is consistent with "at least 90% of all 2,000 bags are cocaine", we need to know what samples from such a population typically look like. That is why the course spends five chapters on probability before it returns to data.

Interlude (Optional): The Two Cultures

In 2001 the statistician Leo Breiman published a famous essay, Statistical Modeling: The Two Cultures. He described two communities working with data.

  • The data-modelling culture (traditional statistics) writes down a model of the mechanism, with interpretable parameters, and uses data to learn those parameters. Its goal is inference: understanding.
  • The algorithmic culture (machine learning) treats the mechanism as a black box and searches for whatever function predicts best on new data. Its goal is prediction.

Breiman, who worked in both, argued that statisticians had been too slow to adopt the second culture. He was right: neural networks, recommendation engines and language models are triumphs of prediction. But the story has a sequel. In 2008 Google launched Google Flu Trends, which predicted flu levels from what people typed into its search engine, faster than official surveillance. It tracked ordinary flu seasons well at first, though it missed the 2009 swine-flu pandemic. Then in the 2012–13 season it predicted more than twice as many flu-related doctor visits as actually happened. The search patterns had changed (media coverage, changes to the search engine itself), and a model with no understanding of the mechanism couldn't notice. Lazer and colleagues described the failure in Science in 2014: "The Parable of Google Flu: Traps in Big Data Analysis".

The lesson isn't that one culture wins. Prediction without understanding breaks when the world changes (remember the chicken). Understanding without good prediction is hard to test. Modern statistics and machine learning use both, and this course gives you the foundations of each.

Key Takeaways

  • Probability goes from mechanism to data; inference goes from data back to mechanism; prediction goes from data to the next case.
  • We learn probability first because we can't judge data without knowing what chance alone can produce.
