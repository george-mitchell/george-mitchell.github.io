Chapter 2
Describing data

Lectures 3–4 · centre, spread, shape, and pictures that tell the truth

Before anyone can predict from data or infer anything about the mechanism behind them, someone has to look at the data. That sounds trivial. It isn't. A table of 300 numbers tells the human eye almost nothing, and a single summary number can hide exactly the thing that matters. An average depth of three feet doesn't mean you can wade across the river.

This chapter gives you the tools for looking: numbers that summarize where data sit (the centre), how much they vary (the spread), and what pattern they make (the shape), and the pictures that show all three at once. Along the way we'll meet a geyser that keeps two timetables, a physicist who measured the speed of light slightly wrong, and a university that seemed to discriminate against women until someone looked more closely. Every named dataset is real and built into R, so you can explore them yourself.

Every summary in this chapter comes with three things:

  1. a way to compute it by hand, step by step, so that you know exactly what it is (and can do it in an exam);
  2. the R code that computes it, so that you can use it on real datasets with hundreds of values;
  3. its strengths and weaknesses, because no single summary is best for every job.

The by-hand answers and the R answers always agree. If yours don't, that's a sign to look again at one of the steps.

Describing data is not an end in itself: it is the first step of both of the course's big goals. For prediction, a summary of past data is the raw material of every forecast: the centre of a distribution is our best single guess for the next value (Section 2.2 makes this precise), and the spread tells us how far off that guess is likely to be. For inference, the pictures and summaries of a sample are our first clues about the mechanism that produced it: two peaks hint at two hidden groups, a difference between boxplots hints at a real effect, and a pattern that reverses when the data are split warns us that something else is at work. Keep both questions in mind as you read: what would I predict for the next case? and what does this suggest about the process behind the data?

Think About It: What Would You Want to Know?

A friend tells you, "the average commute time in my city is 30 minutes." What else would you want to know before deciding whether you'd enjoy living there? Try to name three different things. By the end of the chapter you'll have a word, a number and a picture for each of them.

Typical answers: How much does it vary? (Is it always 30, or sometimes 10 and sometimes 90? That's spread.) Is the average dragged up by a few people with very long commutes? (That's shape, especially skewness, and the difference between the mean and the median.) Are there two groups, such as drivers and cyclists, with very different times? (That's bimodality, and comparing groups with side-by-side boxplots.) What about my particular route? (That's position: where one value sits within the distribution.)

2.1 Distributions: the shape of data

A geyser with two timetables

Old Faithful, in Yellowstone National Park, is the most famous geyser in the world. Every hour or two it shoots water dozens of metres into the air, and crowds gather to watch. Park rangers post a prediction of the next eruption so visitors know when to arrive. How do they predict it?

The pattern of values in a dataset (which values occur, and how often) is its distribution (Section 1.4). A histogram is the standard picture of the distribution of a numerical variable.

Drawing a histogram

A histogram is built from a frequency table: a list of intervals and the number of observations in each.

Definition: Frequency Table and Histogram

Divide the range of a numerical variable into adjacent intervals of equal width, called bins or classes. The frequency of a bin is the number of observations in it, and its relative frequency is the frequency divided by the total number of observations n.

A histogram draws, for each bin, a bar whose base is the bin and whose height is its frequency (or relative frequency). The bars touch, because the bins cover the number line without gaps.

In a density histogram, each bar's height is density=relative frequencybin width, so that the area of each bar is its relative frequency and the total area of all the bars is 1.

The bars of a histogram touch, unlike the bars of a bar chart for categories (Section 2.5), because a numerical variable runs continuously along the number line. A gap in a histogram means "no observations here", which is information.

Idea: Algorithm: A Histogram by Hand

  1. Find the range. Identify the smallest and largest observations.
  2. Choose the bins. Pick a convenient bin width (5 to 15 bins is usual for small datasets) and a starting point at or just below the minimum. Write down every bin as an interval.
  3. Decide the endpoint rule. A value that lands exactly on a boundary must go in only one bin. In these notes we include the left endpoint: the bin [a,b) contains values with a≤x<b. Say which rule you used.
  4. Tally. Go through the data once, making a tally mark in the bin of each observation. Check that the counts add up to n.
  5. Draw the axes. The variable goes on the horizontal axis, with the bin boundaries marked; frequency (or density) goes on the vertical axis, starting at 0.
  6. Draw the bars. One bar per bin, as wide as the bin, as tall as its count, touching its neighbours. Label both axes, with units.

In the first of his five experiments in 1879, Albert Michelson made 20 measurements of the speed of light (more about him in Example 2.8). Recorded as the speed in km/s minus 299,000, they were

Shape. The distribution has a single peak in [900,1000), and a tail stretching out to the left, towards the lower values. It is unimodal and left-skewed (these words are defined below).

Why the endpoint rule matters. R's default is the opposite rule, (a,b]. With right = TRUE, the 900 moves down a bin and the three 1000s move down too, giving counts 1, 2, 5, 11, 1: a noticeably different picture from the same data. With only 20 values, several of which sit exactly on bin boundaries, small choices change the histogram. That's worth remembering whenever you read one.

A histogram depends on two choices you make: the bin width and the starting point. Too wide and every detail is smoothed away; too narrow and the picture is all noise. Try it on Old Faithful and three other datasets from this chapter.

Describing a distribution

When you describe a distribution, describe three things:

  • shape: how many peaks, whether it is symmetric or skewed, and whether there are gaps or unusual values;
  • centre: where a typical value lies;
  • spread: how much the values vary.

Statisticians use a small vocabulary for shape. It's worth learning exactly, because "skewed to the right" is easy to get backwards.

Definition: Words for Shape

  • Modality. A mode of a histogram is a peak: a bar (or cluster of bars) taller than its neighbours. A distribution with one clear peak is unimodal, with two is bimodal, and with more is multimodal. One with no peak at all, where every bin has about the same height, is uniform.
  • Symmetry. A distribution is symmetric if its left and right halves are roughly mirror images.
  • Skewness. A distribution is right-skewed (or positively skewed) if it has a long tail stretching to the right, towards large values, and left-skewed (or negatively skewed) if the long tail stretches to the left. The direction of skew is the direction of the long tail, not of the peak.
  • Gaps and outliers. A gap is a stretch with no observations; an outlier is an observation far from the rest (Section 2.4 gives a rule).

Explore the shapes below. Pay attention to where the mean and median sit for each one: the relationship between them is the quickest numerical clue to the shape.

Most quantities that can't go below zero but occasionally get very large (incomes, waiting times, pollution levels, drug concentrations) are right-skewed. Left skew is rarer, and usually appears when there is a natural upper limit that most values crowd against (scores on an easy test), or when a few values come out unusually low (Michelson's measurements).

Watch Out: Naming the Skew

Students often name the skew after the side where the peak is. It's the other way round: name it after the tail. A histogram with a tall peak on the left and a long tail to the right is right-skewed. A quick check: in a right-skewed distribution, the mean is usually to the right of the median, pulled along by the tail.

Think About It: What Made the Shape?

A histogram's shape is a clue about the mechanism behind the data. For each shape, suggest a mechanism that could produce it: (a) the waiting times at a doctor's surgery are right-skewed; (b) the ages of people at a children's birthday party are bimodal; (c) scores on a very easy quiz are left-skewed. Then say how the shape would affect your prediction for the next person.

(a) Most patients are seen quickly, but now and then a complicated case or an emergency holds everyone up, creating a long right tail; for a prediction, the median wait is more typical than the mean, but you should allow for a long wait. (b) Two groups, children and the adults who brought them; a single "average age" predicts nobody, so first predict which group the next guest is in. (c) Most students get nearly full marks, and a few who didn't prepare score low; the best guess is high, and the uncertainty is mostly on the low side. In each case, reasoning about the mechanism (inference) improves the prediction.

Key Takeaways

  • Always draw the data before summarizing them.
  • A histogram is built from a frequency table: choose bins, decide the endpoint rule, tally, draw touching bars.
  • Describe a distribution by its shape, centre and spread.
  • Right-skewed means a long tail to the right. Two peaks often signal two groups.
  • A histogram's appearance depends on the bins: try several, and trust only the features that survive.

2.2 Centre: what is a typical value?

Mean and median

Suppose we have observations x1,x2,…,xn. The subscript i in xi just labels the observations in the order they were recorded. When we sort them from smallest to largest, we write x(1)≤x(2)≤⋯≤x(n): the brackets around the subscript mean "the ith smallest". There are two classic ways to describe the centre.

Definition: Sample Mean

The sample mean is the ordinary average: x‾=x1+x2+⋯+xnn=1n∑i=1nxi. In R: mean(x).

Definition: Sample Median

The sample median x̃ is the middle value of the sorted data.

  • If n is odd, it is the observation in position n+12: x̃=x((n+1)/2).
  • If n is even, there are two middle observations, in positions n2 and n2+1, and the median is their average: x̃=12(x(n/2)+x(n/2+1)).

In R: median(x).

The two measure "typical" in different ways. The mean is the balance point of the data: put an equal weight at each observation on a see-saw, and the mean is where it balances. A value far from the others sits at the end of a long lever, so it has a lot of influence. The median is the halfway point: half the observations are at or below it and half at or above. It only cares about the order of the values, not how far out the extreme ones are.

The mode

Definition: Mode

The mode of a dataset is its most frequent value. A dataset can have more than one mode (if several values tie for most frequent), and for a numerical variable recorded with many decimal places, every value may occur only once, so the mode is useful mainly for categorical data and for discrete counts. For a histogram, we speak of the modal bin or of peaks (Section 2.1).

For the insect counts in Example 2.3(b), the value 14 occurs three times and nothing else occurs more than twice, so the mode is 14. For glass type in fgl, the counts are WinF 70, WinNF 76, Veh 17, Con 13, Tabl 9 and Head 29, so the modal type is WinNF (building window, non-float). The mode is the only measure of centre that makes sense for a nominal variable: there is no "average glass type" and no "middle glass type". Resistance and the trimmed mean

Definition: Resistant Summary

A summary is resistant (or robust) if changing a few observations, even to wildly wrong values, can change it only a little. The median is resistant; the mean is not.

To see the difference, imagine changing the largest value in a dataset to a billion. The mean shoots up towards a billion. The median doesn't move at all, because the largest value is still the largest, and the middle of the sorted list is unchanged.

Is there a compromise between the two? Yes: throw away the most extreme values at both ends, and average what is left.

Definition: Trimmed Mean

The α-trimmed mean, for 0≤α<0.5, is computed in three steps.

  1. Sort the data.
  2. Let k=⌊nα⌋ (that is, nα rounded down to a whole number). Delete the k smallest and the k largest observations.
  3. Average the n−2k observations that remain.

In R: mean(x, trim = alpha). A "10% trimmed mean" has α=0.1.

Trimmed means are used where outliers are a real worry and the mean is still wanted: Olympic diving and gymnastics drop the highest and lowest judges' scores before averaging, which is a trimmed mean that stops one biased judge deciding a medal.

Example 2.5: A Typo in the Lab Notebook

A toxicologist records five blood alcohol readings (g/dL) from repeated tests of one sample: 0.081, 0.079, 0.083, 0.080, and 0.82, where the last should have been 0.082. Compute the mean, the median and the 20% trimmed mean, by hand and in R.

Mean. The five readings add to 0.081+0.079+0.083+0.080+0.82=1.143, so x‾=1.1435=0.2286.

Median. Sorted: 0.079, 0.080, 0.081, 0.083, 0.82. The middle (third) value is 0.081.

20% trimmed mean. Here nα=5×0.2=1, so k=1: delete one value from each end of the sorted list (0.079 and 0.82) and average the remaining three: 0.080+0.081+0.0833=0.2443≈0.0813.

R

One misplaced decimal point drags the mean to 0.229, almost three times the real level, and far above the legal driving limit of 0.08. The median (0.081) and trimmed mean (0.0813) barely notice. This is why it's wise to compute both the mean and the median: if they disagree badly, look for skewness or errors. (And never simply delete an observation because it is inconvenient: find out why it is unusual. Here, the lab notebook showed a transcription error.)

The mean lands in empty space, where no reading is: it describes nothing that happened. That's a good visual test for any summary: does it sit where the data are?

The trimmed mean is not one summary but a whole family, with the mean and the median at its two ends.

  • Trim 0%: nothing is removed, and the trimmed mean is the ordinary mean.
  • Trim a little: the most extreme values go, and the result moves away from the mean.
  • Trim almost 50% from each end: only the middle one or two values remain, and their average is the median. (R treats trim = 0.5 as the median.)

So choosing a trimming fraction is choosing how much to trust the extremes. The widget below makes the journey visible on the ozone data.

Why the mean and median are "best" guesses

There is a deeper way to think about centre, and it connects directly to Chapter 1's airline. Suppose you must summarize the data with a single number c, and every data value charges you a penalty depending on how far it is from c. Which c makes the total penalty smallest? The answer depends on the penalty rule. Try it before reading the theorem.

One number to stand for all the data: which guess costs least?

Theorem: Mean, Median and Mode as Best Guesses

For observations x1,…,xn:

  1. The total squared distance ∑i=1n(xi−c)2 is smallest when c=x‾, the mean.
  2. The total absolute distance ∑i=1n|xi−c| is smallest when c is a median.
  3. The number of misses, #{i:xi≠c}, is smallest when c is a mode.
  4. The deviations from the mean always add to zero: ∑i=1n(xi−x‾)=0.

Part 4 (needed for part 1). Split the sum and use ∑ixi=nx‾: ∑i=1n(xi−x‾)=∑i=1nxi−nx‾=nx‾−nx‾=0.

Part 1. Let g(c)=∑i(xi−c)2. Differentiate with respect to c, using the chain rule on each term: g′(c)=∑i=1n2(xi−c)(−1)=−2(∑i=1nxi−nc)=−2n(x‾−c). This is zero only at c=x‾. Since g″(c)=2n>0, the graph of g is a parabola opening upwards, so c=x‾ is its minimum.

A proof without calculus. Write xi−c=(xi−x‾)+(x‾−c) and expand the square: ∑i=1n(xi−c)2=∑i=1n(xi−x‾)2+2(x‾−c)∑i=1n(xi−x‾)+n(x‾−c)2=∑i=1n(xi−x‾)2+n(x‾−c)2.∑i=1n(xi−c)2=∑i=1n(xi−x‾)2+2(x‾−c)∑i=1n(xi−x‾)+n(x‾−c)2=∑i=1n(xi−x‾)2+n(x‾−c)2. The middle term vanished by part 4. The first term doesn't depend on c, and the second is never negative and is zero exactly when c=x‾.

Part 2. Imagine sliding c to the right by a tiny amount δ, not passing any data point. Every observation to the right of c gets δ closer, and every observation at or to the left of c gets δ further away. Write L for the number of observations at or to the left of c, and R for the number to the right. Then the total absolute distance changes by δ×(L−R). If more observations lie to the right of c (R>L), this is negative and moving right helps; if more lie to the left, moving left helps. As c moves right, the bracket can only increase, so the total first decreases, then (possibly) stays flat, then increases. The minimum is reached where neither direction helps, which is exactly when at most half the observations lie strictly on either side of c: the middle value when n is odd, and any point between the two middle values (including their average) when n is even. These are the medians.

Part 3. If c is not one of the data values, every observation is a miss. If c equals a value that occurs m times, the number of misses is n−m. This is smallest when m is as large as possible, that is, when c is the most frequent value. ◼∎

So the mean is the best single guess if big errors are especially costly (squared penalty: an error of 10 costs 100 times as much as an error of 1), the median is the best guess if every unit of error costs the same (absolute penalty), and the mode is the best guess if only an exact hit counts. This is the same idea as Chapter 1's airline: the best prediction depends on the cost of being wrong.

Interlude (Optional): Loss Functions: The Hidden Cost Behind Every Guess

What is a loss function? A loss function L(y,c) is a rule that says how bad it is to guess c when the truth turns out to be y. It is zero for a perfect guess and grows as the guess gets worse. The penalties in the widget above are three loss functions:

loss function formula what it punishes best guess
squared error (y−c)2 big misses, very heavily the mean
absolute error |y−c| every unit of error equally the median
zero–one 0 if c=y, else 1 being wrong at all, however close the mode

So the three familiar measures of centre are not three unrelated recipes. Each is the answer to the same question, "which single guess has the smallest total loss?", for a different loss function.

You use loss functions all the time. You just don't write them down.

  • Leaving for a train. Arriving 10 minutes early costs you 10 minutes of waiting; arriving 1 minute late costs you the train. Your loss is lopsided, so you don't aim for the average journey time: you leave early, aiming for a high percentile of the journey time instead. (With a lopsided loss, the best guess is a quantile, which we define in Section 2.3.)
  • Guessing how many people will come to a party. If you'll be embarrassed by running out of food but don't mind leftovers, you over-cater, again a lopsided loss.
  • A multiple-choice exam. A nearly right answer scores the same as a wildly wrong one. That's zero–one loss, so the best strategy is to pick the single most likely option (the mode), not some "average" of the options.
  • A weather forecast. Squared error punishes a forecaster who is off by 10 degrees a hundred times as much as one who is off by 1, so forecasters judged this way avoid big misses above all.

Different loss functions disagree because they care about different things. Squared loss is terrified of large errors, which is why it is dragged around by outliers: it will move the guess a long way to avoid one huge miss. Absolute loss is calmer: one far-away point costs only its distance, so it can't drag the median far. Zero–one loss doesn't care about distance at all.

Machine learning is loss minimization. When a machine-learning model is "trained", what actually happens is that a computer searches for the settings that make the average loss over a training dataset as small as possible.

  • A model predicting house prices is usually trained with squared loss, so it learns something like a mean price for houses with given features.
  • A model that must not be swayed by a few absurd listings might use absolute loss instead, learning a median.
  • A spam filter or image classifier is trained with a loss that rewards putting high probability on the correct category (called cross-entropy, related to the entropy in Section 2.6).
  • A model forecasting electricity demand, where a blackout is far worse than spare capacity, uses a lopsided "quantile loss".

Choosing the loss is choosing what the model cares about, and that is a human decision, not a mathematical one. Chapter 8 returns to squared-error loss when it judges estimators by their mean squared error.

Why it matters: whenever someone reports "the best estimate", ask "best according to which loss?".

Bayesian Inference: The Same Tools Describe Beliefs

In Bayesian statistics, what you know about an unknown quantity, say the true blood alcohol level, is itself described by a distribution: the posterior. To report a single best guess, Bayesians use exactly the tools of this section: the posterior mean (best under squared-error loss), the posterior median (best under absolute-error loss), or the posterior mode (best under zero–one loss), with the theorem above telling them which to use. And to report uncertainty, they describe the posterior's spread.

Key Takeaways

  • Mean x‾ = balance point; median = middle of the sorted data (average the two middle values when n is even); mode = most common value.
  • For right-skewed data, usually mean > median. Report the one that answers your question.
  • The median and trimmed mean are resistant to extreme values; the mean is not. Trimmed means run from the mean (0%) to the median (50%).
  • The mean minimizes squared loss, the median absolute loss, the mode zero–one loss. The best guess depends on the cost of being wrong.

2.3 Spread: how much do the data vary?

Two drug-testing instruments both give the right answer on average. One gives readings within 1% of the truth; the other is sometimes 20% off. Their means are identical; only their spread tells them apart. In science, medicine and forensics, spread is often the more important number.

The range

Definition: Range

The range of a dataset is the largest observation minus the smallest: x(n)−x(1).

For the toxin concentrations of Example 2.3, the range is 15.3−9.8=5.5 ng/mL. The range is easy to compute and to explain, but it depends on only two observations, the most extreme ones, so a single outlier changes it completely, and it tends to grow as you collect more data. (R's range(x) returns the minimum and maximum; diff(range(x)) gives their difference.)

Variance and standard deviation

A better measure of spread uses every observation: how far, typically, is an observation from the mean? The distances from the mean, xi−x‾, are called deviations. We can't just average them, because they always add to zero (part 4 of the theorem in Section 2.2): the positive and negative ones cancel. So we square them first, making them all positive, average the squares, and then take a square root at the end to get back to the original units.

Definition: Sample Variance and Standard Deviation

The sample variance of x1,…,xn is s2=1n−1∑i=1n(xi−x‾)2. The sample standard deviation is its square root, s=s2. It is measured in the same units as the data. In R: var(x) and sd(x).

Idea: Algorithm: Standard Deviation by Hand

  1. Compute the mean x‾.
  2. For each observation, compute the deviation xi−x‾. (Check: they add to 0.)
  3. Square each deviation.
  4. Add up the squares.
  5. Divide by n−1. This is the variance s2.
  6. Take the square root. This is the standard deviation s.

Theorem: Chebyshev's Inequality for Data

For any dataset and any k>1, the fraction of observations more than k standard deviations from the mean is less than 1/k2. In particular, more than 75% of any dataset lies within 2 standard deviations of its mean, and more than 8/9 (about 88.9%) within 3.

Suppose m of the n observations satisfy |xi−x‾|>ks. Each of them contributes more than k2s2 to the sum of squared deviations, and the other terms are non-negative, so (n−1)s2=∑i=1n(xi−x‾)2>mk2s2. Dividing both sides by nk2s2 gives mn<n−1n⋅1k2<1k2. (If s=0 all observations equal the mean and there is nothing to prove.) ◼∎

Chebyshev's guarantee is weak (75% instead of the rule of thumb's 95%), but it never fails. That's the trade-off between assuming something about the shape and assuming nothing.

Quantiles and quartiles

The standard deviation squares deviations, so it is even less resistant than the mean: one extreme value can inflate it enormously. Resistant measures of spread are built from quantiles instead.

Definition: Quantiles, Percentiles and Quartiles

For 0<p<1, the p-quantile (or 100pth percentile) of a dataset is a value with roughly a fraction p of the observations below it and 1−p above it. The first quartile Q1 is the 0.25-quantile, the median is the 0.5-quantile, and the third quartile Q3 is the 0.75-quantile.

For small datasets "roughly" needs a precise rule, and different books and programs use slightly different ones. These notes use R's default rule:

  1. Sort the data: x(1)≤x(2)≤⋯≤x(n).
  2. Compute the position h=1+(n−1)p.
  3. If h is a whole number, the quantile is x(h). Otherwise, write h=j+f with j a whole number and 0<f<1, and move a fraction f of the way from x(j) to x(j+1): Q(p)=x(j)+f(x(j+1)−x(j)).

In R: quantile(x, p). With p=0.5 this rule always gives the median.

The rule spreads the n sorted values evenly from "0% of the way along" (the minimum, position 1) to "100% of the way along" (the maximum, position n). The 25% point is then a quarter of the way along, at position 1+(n−1)/4, and if that falls between two observations we interpolate.

Definition: IQR and MAD

  • The interquartile range is IQR=Q3−Q1: the width of the middle half of the data. In R: IQR(x).
  • The median absolute deviation (MAD) is the median of the distances from the median, |xi−x̃|. In R: mad(x, constant = 1).

R's plain mad(x) multiplies the MAD by 1.4826. That constant makes the MAD comparable to the standard deviation for bell-shaped data, so that the two can be compared directly. Always say which version you are reporting.

Think About It: Spread as the Price of a Prediction

A big spread doesn't mean the data are wrong; it means a single number can't tell you much about the next case, and you should look for other information (season, weather fronts) that explains some of the variation.

History: Who Named the Standard Deviation, and Should We Rename It?

The term standard deviation was coined by the English statistician Karl Pearson in a lecture in 1893, published in 1894; he also introduced the word histogram. The word variance came later, from Ronald Fisher, in a 1918 paper on how traits are passed from parents to children. Both men were brilliant mathematicians, and both were committed eugenicists. In 1911 Pearson became the first Galton Professor of Eugenics at University College London, and Fisher held the same chair from 1933. Much of their work on heredity was driven by eugenic aims: encouraging some groups of people to have more children and discouraging others. The history boxes in Sections 4.6 and 5.3 tell more of the story.

In Bernoulli's Fallacy (2021), the mathematician Aubrey Clayton argues that statistics should rethink some of the words it inherited from this period, partly because of where they came from and partly because they mislead. "Deviation" sounds judgmental, as if differing from the average were a fault. He suggests calling the standard deviation the uncertainty (and its reciprocal the precision), and calling the variance simply the second central moment. He makes similar proposals for "random variable", "correlation", "regression" and "statistically significant", which later chapters mention as they arise.

These notes keep the standard names "standard deviation" and "variance", because renaming them causes real problems:

  • every textbook, scientific paper and piece of software you will meet uses them (R's functions are sd() and var()), and a statistically literate reader has to recognize them;
  • Clayton's replacements already mean other things: "uncertainty" is used for the margin of error on a measurement (Chapter 5) and for interval estimates (Chapter 8), so using it for a spread of data would cause confusion.

Still, the words are not neutral descriptions handed down by nature. Particular people chose them for particular purposes. A deviation from the mean is just a difference, never a defect.

Key Takeaways

  • Range = max − min: simple but fragile.
  • Variance s2 averages squared deviations (dividing by n−1); the standard deviation s is its square root, in the data's units.
  • For bell-shaped data, about 68% / 95% lie within 1 / 2 SDs of the mean. Chebyshev: more than 1−1/k2 within k SDs, for any data.
  • Quantiles by R's rule: position h=1+(n−1)p, then interpolate. IQR and MAD are resistant measures of spread.
  • Random error scatters measurements; systematic error (bias) shifts them all. Averaging reduces only the first.

2.4 Position, outliers and boxplots

Is a birth weight of 2,500 g unusual? Is a glass fragment's refractive index consistent with window glass? Questions like these ask about one observation's position within a distribution. Quantiles (Section 2.3) answer one version: a baby "on the 10th percentile" for weight is lighter than 90% of babies of the same age. z-scores answer another.

z-scores

Definition: Standardized Value (z-Score)

The z-score of an observation x is its distance from the mean in units of standard deviations: z=x−x‾s. A z-score of 2 means "two standard deviations above the mean"; −1.5 means "one and a half standard deviations below".

z-scores let us compare observations measured on different scales: is a student who scored 85 on an exam with mean 70 and SD 10 (z=1.5) further above average than one who scored 620 on an exam with mean 500 and SD 100 (z=1.2)? Yes. And z-scores don't depend on the units: converting grams to pounds changes x, x‾ and s by the same factor, which cancels. Here is the general fact.

Theorem: Changing Units

If every observation is transformed to yi=a+bxi (for constants a and b), then y‾=a+bx‾andsy=|b|sx. In words: adding a constant shifts the centre but not the spread; multiplying by b multiplies both by b (the spread by |b|, since spread can't be negative).

The mean. Using the sum rules from Section 0.4, y‾=1n∑i=1n(a+bxi)=1n(na+b∑i=1nxi)=a+bx‾.

The standard deviation. Each deviation becomes yi−y‾=(a+bxi)−(a+bx‾)=b(xi−x‾), so the constant a cancels. Squaring and adding, ∑i=1n(yi−y‾)2=b2∑i=1n(xi−x‾)2. Divide by n−1 to get sy2=b2sx2, and take square roots: sy=|b|sx. ◼∎

For example, converting Celsius to Fahrenheit (F=32+1.8C) multiplies the mean by 1.8 and then adds 32, but only multiplies the standard deviation by 1.8: shifting every temperature up by 32 degrees doesn't make them any more spread out. The theorem also shows that z-scores always have mean 0 and standard deviation 1: the z-score is y=a+bx with b=1/s and a=−x‾/s, so its mean is −x‾/s+x‾/s=0 and its SD is s/s=1.

Outliers

Definition: The 1.5 × IQR Rule

An observation is flagged as a possible outlier if it lies more than 1.5×IQR below Q1 or above Q3. The values lower fence=Q1−1.5IQR,upper fence=Q3+1.5IQR are called the fences.

Why 1.5? It's a convention, chosen by John Tukey because it works well in practice: for bell-shaped data, it flags fewer than 1 observation in 100, so a flagged point is genuinely unusual, while still catching real outliers.

An outlier isn't automatically a mistake. It may be a data-entry error (Example 2.5), a measurement under unusual conditions, or the most interesting observation in the dataset. The rule only says: look at this one.

Boxplots

A boxplot (or box-and-whisker plot) draws the five-number summary and the outliers in one compact picture.

Definition: Five-Number Summary and Boxplot

The five-number summary of a dataset is (minimum, Q1, median, Q3, maximum).

A boxplot draws a box from Q1 to Q3 with a line at the median; whiskers from the box out to the most extreme observations that lie inside the fences; and every observation beyond the fences as an individual point.

Idea: Algorithm: A Boxplot by Hand

  1. Sort the data.
  2. Median. Find the middle value; draw a line there.
  3. Quartiles. Find Q1 and Q3; draw a box from Q1 to Q3, so that the box contains the middle half of the data.
  4. Fences. Compute IQR=Q3−Q1, then the fences Q1−1.5IQR and Q3+1.5IQR. (Don't draw them; they are for checking.)
  5. Whiskers. Draw a line from the box down to the smallest observation inside the fences, and up to the largest observation inside the fences.
  6. Outliers. Plot every observation beyond a fence as a separate point.
  7. Label the axis with the variable and its units.

What a boxplot can hide

A boxplot compresses a whole dataset into five numbers plus outliers. That makes it compact, and it also means very different datasets can have identical boxplots.

Side-by-side boxplots

Where boxplots really shine is in comparing a numerical variable across groups. Placed side by side on a common axis, they let you compare centres (the median lines), spreads (the box lengths), skewness (whether the median sits in the middle of its box, and whether one whisker is much longer) and outliers, for many groups at once.

Think About It: When Is an Outlier Evidence?

In a study of 30 forensic labs, one lab's error rate is far above the upper fence. Give two different explanations for the outlier, one that makes it a problem with the data and one that makes it a genuine signal about the labs. What extra information would help you decide?

Key Takeaways

  • A z-score z=(x−x‾)/s measures position in standard deviations; it doesn't depend on units.
  • Under y=a+bx: the mean becomes a+bx‾, the SD becomes |b|s.
  • The 1.5 × IQR rule flags possible outliers. Investigate them; don't just delete them.
  • A boxplot draws the five-number summary and outliers. Build it by hand: sort, median, quartiles, fences, whiskers, outliers.
  • Identical boxplots can hide very different shapes. Boxplots are at their best side by side, comparing groups.

Is it bell-shaped? The Gaussian Q-Q plot

Many methods later in the course assume data come from a bell-shaped (Gaussian) distribution. A histogram is a poor judge of this, especially for small samples, because its appearance depends so much on the bins. A better tool is the Gaussian quantile–quantile (Q-Q) plot. (Most books and software call the bell curve the normal distribution, and this plot a normal Q-Q plot; R's function is qqnorm(). These notes use the name Gaussian, for reasons explained in Section 5.3.)

The idea is simple. Sort the data. Then ask: if the data really came from a bell-shaped distribution, where would the smallest value be expected to sit? The second smallest? The median? A Q-Q plot puts these expected positions (from the Gaussian distribution, Chapter 5) on the horizontal axis and the actual sorted data on the vertical axis. If the data are bell-shaped, actual and expected agree up to a change of units, and the points lie close to a straight line. Curves and bends show how they depart:

  • points curving upwards at the right end (and often at the left): the largest values are too large, a sign of right skew;
  • points curving downwards at the left end: the smallest values are too small, left skew;
  • an S shape, with both ends pulling away from the line in opposite directions: heavy tails (more extreme values at both ends than a bell would produce).

Honest and dishonest graphs

A graph is an argument, and it can mislead as easily as words. The most common trick is with the axis.

How to make a difference look bigger

The bars show the average birth weight of babies born to mothers who did and didn't smoke (Baystate Medical Center, 1986: 3,056 g and 2,772 g). Move the start of the vertical axis up from zero. The numbers never change, but the picture does. The lie factor compares the size of the effect shown in the graphic with the size of the effect in the data; an honest bar chart has a lie factor of 1.

Watch Out: Checklist for an Honest Graph

  • Bars start at zero. Bar length is read as size.
  • Label the axes, with units.
  • Don't hide the variation. A bar of averages hides the individual observations; show them, or at least their spread.
  • Compare like with like. Totals from groups of different sizes should usually be rates (per 100,000 people, per day).
  • Don't let decoration distort. 3-D bars and pie charts make sizes hard to judge accurately.

Think About It: Who Made This Graph, and Why?

A company's annual report shows a bar chart of profits over five years with the vertical axis starting at $9.5 million; profits rose from $9.6 to $10.1 million. Who benefits from this choice of axis? Now imagine a news article about rising crime using a line graph whose axis starts at zero, making a 40% rise look gentle. Is starting a line graph at zero always the honest choice?

The company's bars exaggerate a 5% rise into what looks like a six-fold one (bars of height 0.1 and 0.6 above the axis), which flatters management. Line graphs are different: their message is the change over time, read from the slope, not the length of anything, so an axis that doesn't start at zero can be honest, and starting at zero can hide a real and important change. The honest choice depends on what the picture is meant to compare. A good test: would the people who made the graph be happy for you to see the same data plotted another reasonable way?

History: The Inventors of Statistical Graphics

The Scottish engineer and economist William Playfair invented the line graph and the bar chart in his Commercial and Political Atlas of 1786, to make England's trade figures visible at a glance, and the pie chart in 1801.

In 1858, two years after returning from the Crimean War, Florence Nightingale used "rose" diagrams (polar area charts) to show that far more British soldiers had died of preventable diseases in army hospitals than of wounds. Her graphics helped persuade the government to reform military sanitation, and she became the first woman elected a Fellow of the Royal Statistical Society.

A century later the American statistician John Tukey urged statisticians to explore data visually before testing anything, and invented the boxplot along the way; his book Exploratory Data Analysis (1977) gave the approach its name.

2.6 Two categorical variables and Simpson's paradox

Contingency tables

When both variables are categorical, we summarize them with a contingency table (a two-way table of counts): one row for each category of one variable, one column for each category of the other, and in each cell the number of units with that combination. Chapter 3 uses one for the Titanic. To compare groups, convert the counts to row or column percentages, so that groups of different sizes can be compared fairly.

Definition: Simpson's Paradox

Simpson's paradox occurs when an association between two variables within each of several groups reverses (or disappears) when the groups are combined. It happens when a third variable is related both to the grouping and to the outcome, so that the groups being compared are spread very differently across its levels.

Causal Inference: Which Table Should You Believe?

Simpson's paradox isn't a paradox of arithmetic: both tables are correct. It's a question of which comparison answers your question, and that depends on the causal structure. Draw the diagram:

Sex influences which department a person applies to, and the department strongly influences admission. If the question is "do departments treat men and women differently?", we compare within departments, holding department fixed, and find no evidence against women. If the question were "how does being a woman affect your overall chance of admission, all things included?", the pooled numbers describe that total effect, which runs through department choice. The data can't tell us which question to ask. The diagram can.

The same pattern turns up everywhere: a hospital with worse survival rates overall that is better for every type of patient (because it takes the sickest patients), or a treatment that looks harmful overall but helps in every subgroup. Before pooling data across groups, ask whether the groups differ in ways that also affect the outcome.

Source: P. J. Bickel, E. A. Hammel and J. W. O'Connell (1975), "Sex bias in graduate admissions: data from Berkeley", Science 187, 398–404.

Interlude (Optional): How Spread Out Is a Categorical Variable?

Standard deviations need numbers. How can we measure the spread, or unpredictability, of a categorical variable like glass type? If almost every fragment were window glass, guessing a fragment's type would be easy; if all six types were equally common, it would be hard.

In 1948 Claude Shannon defined the entropy of a categorical distribution with proportions p1,…,pk: H=−∑j=1kpjlog2pjbits. It is 0 when one category has everything (no uncertainty), and as large as possible, log2k, when all k categories are equally common. You can think of it as the average number of yes/no questions needed to identify a randomly chosen category, if you ask cleverly. For a fair coin, H=−(12log212+12log212)=1 bit: one yes/no question.

R
p <- table(MASS::fgl$type) / nrow(MASS::fgl)   # proportions of the six glass types
-sum(p * log2(p))                               # entropy, in bits
log2(6)                                         # the maximum for six types
output
[1] 2.176534
[1] 2.584963

The six glass types have entropy 2.18 bits, below the maximum of 2.58 because window glass is much more common than the rest. Entropy is the foundation of information theory, data compression, and the way decision-tree algorithms in machine learning choose which question to ask first. It is also behind the cross-entropy loss used to train classifiers (Section 2.2). Chapter 4 returns to it.

Why it matters: spread isn't only about numbers; unpredictability can be measured for any variable.

Hospital A treated 1,000 patients and 90% survived. Hospital B treated 1,000 patients and 80% survived. But hospital B takes most of the region's severe trauma cases.

Can you conclude hospital A gives better care?

Remember Berkeley. Could the hospitals differ in which patients they treat?

Equal totals don't mean equal mixes of patients. What third variable affects both which hospital and survival?

Severity affects both the hospital a patient goes to and their chance of survival: a confounder. A fair comparison looks at survival within severity groups. It's quite possible that B does better for both mild and severe cases, and looks worse overall only because it treats so many severe ones.

Think About It: Pooling in Your Own Life

A college reports that its average class size fell from 30 to 26 over five years. But most students say their classes feel bigger than ever. Could both be true? Think about who is being averaged.

Yes. The college averages over classes; students experience the average over students. Suppose there are many small seminars and a few huge lectures: most classes are small, but most students sit in the big ones, because that's where the students are. If the college added many small seminars while the big lectures grew, the average class size would fall while the typical student's class grew. It's the same trap as the average of the states in Example 1.8, and a cousin of Simpson's paradox: always ask what the units of an average are.

Key Takeaways

  • A contingency table summarizes two categorical variables; compare groups using row or column percentages.
  • Simpson's paradox: a comparison can reverse when groups are pooled, because a third variable is tied to both.
  • Which comparison is right depends on the causal question. Draw the DAG before pooling.

Prediction and inference in this chapter

Looking back, every tool in this chapter serves the course's two goals.

  • Prediction. The mean, median and mode are the best single predictions under squared, absolute and zero–one loss. The standard deviation and IQR measure how wrong such a prediction is likely to be. Scatterplots show when one variable can help predict another (temperature and ozone).
  • Inference. Pictures and summaries are where we first notice that a mechanism might be at work: two peaks in Old Faithful's eruptions pointed to two eruption types; side-by-side boxplots suggested some insecticides work better than others; a systematic gap between Michelson's results and the truth revealed bias in his apparatus. Simpson's paradox showed that a pattern in pooled data can mislead about the mechanism, which is why inference needs the causal thinking of Chapter 1.

What we can't yet do is say how sure we should be: whether the difference between two boxplots could be a fluke of these particular samples. That needs probability, which is the subject of the next three chapters.
