Lectures 11–16 built the two quantum attacks on cryptography: Grover's quadratic speedup for search, and Shor's polynomial-time algorithms for factoring and discrete logarithms. This lecture, the end of Block II, asks what they mean in practice. By the end you should be able to sort a cryptographic primitive into broken, weakened or believed resistant and say why; list the quantities a resource estimate depends on and read one critically; explain harvest-now-decrypt-later with Mosca's inequality; and describe the NIST post-quantum standards and the migration timeline at the level a policy memo needs.

1 — Broken, weakened, or resistant

Two lessons. First, for public-key schemes based on factoring or discrete logs, no key size helps: Shor's cost grows polynomially, so doubling the key multiplies the attack cost by about 8, not by \(2^{\text{something}}\). The only fix is a different problem. Second, for symmetric schemes the fix is a parameter change: 256-bit keys and 384-bit hashes restore comfortable margins (Lecture 12). 2 — Anatomy of a resource estimate

definition 17.1 — cryptographically relevant quantum computer

A cryptographically relevant quantum computer (CRQC) is a quantum computer able to break a cryptographic primitive in practical use, such as RSA-2048 or a 256-bit elliptic curve, in a time short enough to matter (hours to months). Q-Day is the (unknown) date on which the first one exists.

Today's machines are nowhere near this: the largest gate-based devices have on the order of a hundred to a thousand physical qubits, with two-qubit error rates around \(10^{-3}\) at best, and they run small error-corrected experiments, not long computations. Every claim about Q-Day therefore rests on a resource estimate: a calculation of what a hypothetical machine would need. An estimate has two layers, and you should always know which numbers come from which.

Logical layer (the algorithm, as in Lectures 15–16):

  • the number of logical qubits, the error-corrected qubits the algorithm uses;
  • the number of non-Clifford gates (Toffoli or \(T\) gates, Lecture 7), which are the expensive ones on an error-corrected machine, and their depth.

Physical layer (the machine and its error-correcting code, Lectures 21–23):

  • the physical error rate \(p\) of each gate and measurement;
  • the code and its distance \(d\), which set how many physical qubits each logical qubit costs and how small its error is;
  • the cycle time (one round of error detection) and the reaction time (how fast classical decoding can feed back into the circuit, which limits how fast \(T\) gates can run in sequence);
  • the connectivity (nearest neighbours on a 2-D grid, or long-range links);
  • the number of magic-state factories, which produce the \(T\) or Toffoli states, and the routing space between everything.

Tick the quantities that belong to the physical layer of a resource estimate.

  • the two-qubit gate error rate
  • the Toffoli countThe Toffoli count is a property of the algorithm's circuit: it is the same on any error-corrected machine.

  • the number of logical qubitsLogical qubits are what the algorithm uses; how many physical qubits each one costs is the physical layer's question.

The logical layer is fixed once the algorithm and its circuit are fixed. The physical layer describes the machine and the code running underneath.
Error rate, distance and reaction time describe the hardware and its error correction. Toffoli count and logical qubits come from the algorithm (Lectures 15–16).

For the surface code, a useful rule of thumb (Lecture 23 makes it precise) is that each logical qubit costs about \(2d^2\) physical qubits and fails per cycle with probability about \(0.1\,(p/p_{\text{th}})^{(d+1)/2}\), with threshold \(p_{\text{th}}\approx1\%\). The distance must make the whole computation succeed: logical qubits times time steps times the per-step error must stay below, say, 10%. The second code box in Section 8 runs these numbers for 1,000 logical qubits and \(10^9\) steps: at \(p=10^{-3}\) it needs \(d=23\) and about a million physical qubits for the data alone, and at \(p=10^{-4}\) only about a quarter as many. This is not any paper's method, but it shows the structure: the physical error rate enters exponentially, through the distance.

Use the rule of thumb with distance \(d=17\) and \(p=10^{-3}\). How many physical qubits does each logical qubit cost, and what is \(\log_{10}\) of its failure probability per cycle?

Physical qubits: \(2d^2\). Failure: \(0.1\,(p/p_{\text{th}})^{(d+1)/2}\) with \(p/p_{\text{th}}=10^{-3}/10^{-2}\).
\(2\cdot17^2=578\), and \(0.1\cdot(0.1)^{9}=10^{-10}\). Each step of 2 in the distance buys a factor of 10 here, at a quadratic cost in qubits.

3 — Case study: twenty million qubits, then one million

The most cited estimate for RSA-2048 is Gidney and Ekerå (2019, published in Quantum in 2021). Its assumptions are stated up front, which is the first thing to look for:

  • physical gate error rate \(10^{-3}\) (0.1%), uniform across the device;
  • surface-code cycle time of 1 μs and a classical reaction time of 10 μs;
  • a square grid of qubits with nearest-neighbour connections only.

Under these assumptions they estimated 20 million noisy qubits for 8 hours, down from the hundreds of millions to a billion of earlier estimates. Most of the savings came from the arithmetic: windowed modular exponentiation (looking up several exponent bits at once), oblivious carry runways (cutting long carry chains into short parallel pieces), coset representations of modular integers, and a careful layout of the surface code on the chip.

In 2025 Gidney revised the estimate, keeping the same hardware assumptions: fewer than one million noisy qubits, in less than a week. HW9 asks you to read the paper's abstract, introduction and conclusion and identify the three main ideas behind the reduction. Two observations need no reading:

  • The headline is a trade, not only a saving: 20 times fewer qubits, about 20 times longer runtime. Their space–time volumes are almost equal, about \(1.6\times10^8\) and \(1.7\times10^8\) qubit-hours.
  • Both are upper bounds on cost under stated assumptions. They say nothing about when hardware meeting those assumptions will exist.

A hypothetical design needs 4 million physical qubits running for 2 days. What is its space–time volume, in units of \(10^8\) qubit-hours?

Qubit-hours are qubits times hours; 2 days is 48 hours.
\(4\times10^6\cdot48=1.92\times10^8\) qubit-hours: about the same volume as both Gidney estimates, just spent with a different shape.

4 — Reading new claims critically

Since 2025 new estimates arrive every few months, many as preprints with press releases. Two from 2026 illustrate the questions to ask.

  • Pinnacle (Webster et al., Iceberg Quantum, February 2026, preprint): RSA-2048 with "fewer than one hundred thousand physical qubits, given a physical error rate of \(10^{-3}\), code cycle time of 1 microsecond and a reaction time of 10 microseconds", using quantum LDPC codes instead of the surface code. The error and timing assumptions match Gidney's; the connectivity does not, since LDPC codes need long-range couplings that a nearest-neighbour grid lacks. The runtime is longer, around a month near 100,000 qubits by secondary accounts of the paper's trade-off curve.
  • Elliptic curves (Babbush et al., Google Quantum AI with collaborators, March 2026, preprint): 256-bit elliptic-curve discrete logs with fewer than 1,200 logical qubits and fewer than \(9\times10^7\) Toffolis, "in minutes using fewer than half a million physical qubits" at error rate \(10^{-3}\) with planar connectivity. The circuits were not published; a zero-knowledge proof certifies that they exist.
idea — five questions for any quantum-attack headline
  1. What is attacked? RSA-2048 and a 256-bit curve are different targets; a "broken" toy instance such as factoring 15 or 21 says nothing.
  2. Which assumptions? Physical error rate, cycle and reaction times, connectivity. Are they the field's standard ones (\(10^{-3}\), 1 μs, 10 μs, planar), or something no device has shown?
  3. How long? A qubit count without a runtime is half an answer; a huge runtime can make a small machine useless.
  4. What is counted? Logical or physical qubits? Data only, or factories and routing too?
  5. Who checked it? Peer-reviewed, preprint, or press release? Are the circuits public, checkable by simulation on small instances?

Pinnacle claims RSA-2048 with fewer than 100,000 physical qubits, against Gidney's one million. Which of the five questions exposes the main difference in assumptions?

  • Which assumptions: the physical error rateBoth assume \(10^{-3}\).
  • Which assumptions: the cycle and reaction timesBoth assume 1 μs cycles and 10 μs reaction time.
  • Which assumptions: the connectivity
  • What is attackedBoth target RSA-2048.
Compare the quoted assumptions line by line. What do quantum LDPC codes need that a nearest-neighbour grid does not provide?
LDPC codes need long-range couplings, while Gidney assumes a planar nearest-neighbour grid. (Question 3, the runtime, is the other one to ask: about a month.)

Applying these questions does not mean dismissing the claims. The trend in the figure is real and steep: four orders of magnitude in fifteen years, mostly from better algorithms and codes rather than better hardware. A sensible reading is that the hardware bar is coming down to meet the hardware, and that the date is uncertain.

5 — Harvest now, decrypt later

An adversary can record encrypted traffic today and store it until a CRQC exists. Key exchange is the vulnerable step: once the shared key is recovered from a recorded RSA or Diffie–Hellman handshake, every message protected by that key can be read, years later. Michele Mosca turned this into a simple inequality.

proposition 17.1 — Mosca's inequality

Let \(x\) be the number of years the data must remain secret, \(y\) the number of years needed to migrate the system to quantum-safe cryptography, and \(z\) the number of years until a CRQC exists. If

$$x+y\gt z,$$

then some data encrypted by the system will still need protecting when it can be decrypted.

Proof. Until the migration ends, \(y\) years from now, the system keeps producing quantum-vulnerable ciphertexts. The last of them must stay secret until \(y+x\) years from now. If \(z\lt x+y\), it is exposed for the remaining \(x+y-z\) years (assuming it was recorded).

A company's trade secrets must stay confidential for \(x=12\) years, and its migration will take \(y=5\) years. If a CRQC appears in \(z=10\) years, for how many years is the last vulnerable ciphertext exposed?

By the proof of Proposition 17.1, the last vulnerable ciphertext must stay secret until \(x+y\) years from now, and it can be read from year \(z\) on.
\(x+y-z=12+5-10=7\) years. Since \(17\gt10\), the inequality holds: this company is already late.
example 17.1 — sealed court records

A court system encrypts sealed records (juvenile records, protected witnesses) that must stay confidential for 25 years. Migrating its document systems, vendors and archives will take 7 years. For which Q-Day dates is it already late?

\(x+y=25+7=32\). If a CRQC exists within 32 years, that is, by about 2058, then records sealed near the end of the migration can be decrypted while they are still supposed to be sealed, provided someone recorded them in transit or copied the encrypted archive. Every estimate in the figure of Section 3 makes 32 years look long. The inequality does not require knowing \(z\); it only requires believing \(z\) might be less than 32.

Mosca's inequality

Choose a record type or set the three numbers yourself. The verdict compares \(x+y\) with \(z\). For public-sector systems, the migration time \(y\) is often the number you control.

Harvest-now-decrypt-later affects confidentiality, so key exchange and encryption must move first. Signatures are different: a forged signature is only useful while the signature is being checked, so a signature scheme can be replaced up to Q-Day itself. The exceptions are long-lived trust anchors, such as firmware signing keys and root certificates burned into devices that will still be in service when a CRQC exists; those need early attention too.

6 — The migration

NIST ran an open competition from 2016. Its first three post-quantum standards were published in August 2024, and further selections followed.

The timeline. NIST's draft transition plan, IR 8547 (initial public draft, November 2024), proposes that quantum-vulnerable public-key algorithms such as RSA, finite-field and elliptic-curve Diffie–Hellman, ECDSA and EdDSA be deprecated after 2030 and disallowed after 2035 in U.S. federal systems. NIST's SP 800-227 (final, September 2025) gives recommendations for using key-encapsulation mechanisms. Deployment is already widespread for key exchange: major browsers and content-delivery networks negotiate a hybrid of X25519 and ML-KEM-768 by default, and messaging apps such as Signal and iMessage have added post-quantum key agreement.

Hybrid key exchange runs a classical and a post-quantum key exchange together and derives the session key from both, so an attacker must break both. It protects against a quantum computer (through ML-KEM) and against an unexpected classical break of the young post-quantum scheme (through X25519). Crypto-agility means building systems so that algorithms can be replaced by configuration rather than by redesign; the main lesson of the RSA-to-ECC and SHA-1 transitions was how long it takes without it.

The session key is derived from both shared secrets. What does the attacker still not know after breaking one component?
Both secrets feed the key derivation, so the attacker needs both: a quantum computer gets X25519 but not ML-KEM, and a surprise classical break of ML-KEM leaves X25519 standing.
crypto connection — PQC or QKD?

Post-quantum cryptography (PQC) is ordinary software that runs on today's computers and is believed hard for quantum ones. Quantum key distribution (QKD, Lecture 20) uses quantum states to distribute keys with security based on physics, but needs special hardware and dedicated links, and does not provide signatures. Government agencies in the U.S., U.K. and France currently recommend PQC as the primary migration path. Lecture 20 explains what QKD does guarantee.

7 — What a lattice scheme looks like

ML-KEM's security rests on learning with errors (LWE; Regev, 2005). Pick a prime modulus \(q\), a secret vector \(s\in\mathbb Z_q^n\), and publish many noisy linear equations:

$$b=As+e\pmod q ,$$

where \(A\) is a public random \(m\times n\) matrix and \(e\) is a vector of small random errors. Without \(e\) this is linear algebra: Gaussian elimination recovers \(s\) in polynomial time. With \(e\), elimination mixes the errors together until they fill the whole range mod \(q\), and the best known algorithms, classical or quantum, take time exponential in \(n\). ML-KEM uses a structured version (Module-LWE, with \(n=256\)-coefficient polynomials and \(q=3329\)) for efficiency.

A one-dimensional LWE toy: \(q=7\), a secret \(s\in\mathbb Z_7\), and one equation \(b=3s+e\pmod 7\) with \(b=5\) and an unknown error \(e\in\{-1,0,1\}\). How many values of \(s\) are consistent with this equation?

For each possible error, solve \(3s\equiv5-e\pmod7\); the inverse of 3 mod 7 is 5.
\(3s\in\{4,5,6\}\) gives \(s\in\{6,4,2\}\): three candidates, where without the error there would be exactly one (\(s=4\)). Errors multiply the possibilities, and more equations only help if you can untangle the errors.
example 17.2 — noise ruins elimination

The first code box in Section 8 takes \(q=17\), \(n=3\), six equations, and errors in \(\{-1,0,1\}\). What happens?

Without errors, elimination returns the secret \((5,11,2)\) and the three extra equations are consistent. With errors, elimination returns \((3,15,3)\), which is wrong, and the extra equations become inconsistent. Brute force over all \(17^3=4913\) candidates, keeping the one whose residuals are smallest, does recover \((5,11,2)\). That works here because \(17^3\) is tiny; for ML-KEM the analogous search space has well over \(2^{100}\) elements, and the clever (lattice-reduction) attacks are still exponential.

The connection to Lecture 16: Regev's reduction relates such lattice problems to the dihedral hidden subgroup problem, where Fourier sampling has not led to an efficient algorithm. LWE is the problem behind most of the new standards because it has survived that line of attack, has worst-case to average-case reductions, and is fast in practice.

8 — Try it in code

tool — learning with errors, in miniature (numpy) 
import itertools
import numpy as np

q, n, m = 17, 3, 6                                   # modulus, secret length, number of equations
rng = np.random.default_rng(17)
A = rng.integers(0, q, size=(m, n))                  # public random matrix
s = np.array([5, 11, 2])                             # the secret
e = rng.integers(-1, 2, size=m)                      # small errors in {-1, 0, 1}
b = (A @ s + e) % q                                  # public: (A, b)

def solve_mod(A, b, q):
    """Gauss-Jordan elimination mod a prime q; returns (solution from the first n pivots, leftover residuals)"""
    M = np.concatenate([A % q, (b % q)[:, None]], axis=1).astype(int); rows, cols = M.shape; r = 0
    for c in range(cols - 1):
        piv = next((i for i in range(r, rows) if M[i, c] % q), None)
        if piv is None: continue
        M[[r, piv]] = M[[piv, r]]; M[r] = M[r] * pow(int(M[r, c]), -1, q) % q
        for i in range(rows):
            if i != r and M[i, c]: M[i] = (M[i] - M[i, c] * M[r]) % q
        r += 1
    return M[:cols - 1, -1], M[cols - 1:, -1]

sol, res = solve_mod(A, (A @ s) % q, q)
print("without errors: elimination gives", sol, " leftover equations consistent:", not res.any())
sol, res = solve_mod(A, b, q)
print("with errors:    elimination gives", sol, " leftover residuals:", res, "(inconsistent)")

centered = lambda v: (v + q // 2) % q - q // 2      # represent residues in -8..8
best = min(itertools.product(range(q), repeat=n), key=lambda c: np.abs(centered(A @ np.array(c) - b)).sum())
print(f"brute force over all {q**n} candidates finds", list(best), "(the secret is", s.tolist(), ")")
without errors: elimination gives [ 5 11  2]  leftover equations consistent: True
with errors:    elimination gives [ 3 15  3]  leftover residuals: [5 9 4] (inconsistent)
brute force over all 4913 candidates finds [5, 11, 2] (the secret is [5, 11, 2] )

Example 17.2. The errors are tiny (\(\pm1\)) but elimination amplifies them: each row operation multiplies and adds them, and after a few steps they are as large as the numbers themselves.

tool — a back-of-envelope surface-code budget 
# A back-of-envelope surface-code budget (illustrative rule of thumb; real estimates are far more detailed)
def distance(p, p_th=1e-2, target=1e-13):
    """smallest odd d with logical error per qubit per step 0.1 (p/p_th)^((d+1)/2) <= target"""
    d = 3
    while 0.1 * (p / p_th) ** ((d + 1) / 2) > target * (1 + 1e-9):
        d += 2
    return d

L, D = 1000, 1e9                       # logical qubits, logical time steps
target = 0.1 / (L * D)                 # allow a 10% chance that anything fails
for p in (1e-3, 3e-4, 1e-4):
    d = distance(p, target=target)
    print(f"physical error {p:.0e}: distance {d:2d}, about {2 * d * d:5d} physical per logical, "
          f"{2 * d * d * L / 1e6:.2f} million physical qubits (data only, no magic-state factories)")
physical error 1e-03: distance 23, about  1058 physical per logical, 1.06 million physical qubits (data only, no magic-state factories)
physical error 3e-04: distance 15, about   450 physical per logical, 0.45 million physical qubits (data only, no magic-state factories)
physical error 1e-04: distance 11, about   242 physical per logical, 0.24 million physical qubits (data only, no magic-state factories)

Lowering the physical error rate tenfold, from \(10^{-3}\) to \(10^{-4}\), halves the distance and cuts the footprint by more than a factor of four. That is why hardware papers report error rates as prominently as qubit counts. The same rule is what the footprint widget in Section 2 uses.

9 — Summary and exercises

summary
  • Factoring and discrete-log schemes (RSA, Diffie–Hellman, all elliptic-curve cryptography) are broken by Shor at any key size; symmetric ciphers and hashes are weakened by Grover and fixed by larger parameters; lattice, hash-based and code-based schemes are believed resistant.
  • A resource estimate has a logical layer (qubits, Toffoli or \(T\) count) and a physical layer (error rate, code distance, cycle and reaction time, connectivity, factories). Always read the assumptions and the runtime with the qubit count.
  • RSA-2048: 20 million qubits for 8 hours (2019), under a million for under a week (2025), at \(10^{-3}\) error, 1 μs cycles, 10 μs reaction, planar connectivity. Elliptic curves are cheaper. Newer claims use different codes and connectivity and are preprints.
  • Mosca: if \(x+y\gt z\), you are already late. Harvest-now-decrypt-later makes key exchange the first priority.
  • NIST: ML-KEM, ML-DSA, SLH-DSA (2024), FN-DSA and HQC to follow; deprecation of vulnerable algorithms after 2030 and disallowance after 2035 in the draft plan. Deploy hybrids and design for crypto-agility.

  5. An agency can migrate only one thing this year: its TLS key exchange or its document-signing system. Which should it choose, and when would the answer change?
  6. Why do deployed systems use hybrid X25519 + ML-KEM key exchange rather than ML-KEM alone?
  1. \(x+y=60\): any Q-Day within 60 years exposes some records. Long-lived health, legal and government records are the clearest case for migrating early.
  2. X25519: elliptic-curve Diffie–Hellman, broken by Shor. SHA3-512 preimages: Grover gives about \(2^{256}\) idealised, still far out of reach: weakened, not threatened. AES-192: about \(2^{96}\) idealised Grover iterations, and far more in practice: weakened but acceptable. ML-DSA-65: lattice signature, believed resistant. SLH-DSA: security from hash functions only, believed resistant.
  3. \(2\times10^7\times8=1.6\times10^8\) and \(10^6\times168=1.68\times10^8\) qubit-hours. The total "volume" is about the same; the 2025 design spends it on a smaller, longer-running machine. It shows a trade-off, not by itself a cheaper attack, though a machine with fewer qubits is much easier to build. It says nothing about when such a machine will exist.
  4. Target per step \(0.1/(10^3\cdot10^9)=10^{-13}\); \(0.1\cdot0.01^{(d+1)/2}\le10^{-13}\) needs \((d+1)/2\ge6\), so \(d=11\), \(2\cdot121=242\) physical per logical, about 242,000 data qubits.
  5. Key exchange: recorded handshakes can be decrypted later (harvest now, decrypt later), whereas a signature only has to resist forgery while it is being checked. The answer changes for signatures that must remain trustworthy for many years or cannot be updated later, such as firmware signing keys in long-lived devices and roots of trust.
  6. ML-KEM is young; a classical cryptanalytic break or an implementation flaw is possible. A hybrid stays secure if either component is, so it costs a little bandwidth and loses nothing. Agencies differ on how long to keep it: some, such as Germany's BSI and France's ANSSI, recommend hybrids during the transition, while the U.S. NSA's CNSA 2.0 suite allows ML-KEM on its own.
