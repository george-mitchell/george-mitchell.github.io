1 — Why this course, and why now

Quantum computing is one of the rare technologies whose most famous application is destructive. In 1994 Peter Shor showed that a large enough quantum computer could factor integers and compute discrete logarithms efficiently. Those are exactly the two problems that RSA, Diffie–Hellman and elliptic-curve cryptography depend on. For thirty years this was a theorem about machines that did not exist. Three recent developments have turned it into a planning problem.

crypto connection — three headlines, 2024–2026
  1. The replacements are standardized. In August 2024 NIST published its first three post-quantum standards: FIPS 203 (ML-KEM, for key exchange), FIPS 204 (ML-DSA, signatures) and FIPS 205 (SLH-DSA, hash-based signatures). Governments and companies are now migrating.
  2. Error correction crossed a threshold. Google's Willow processor ran surface-code memories whose logical error rate fell as the code grew: each increase in code distance by 2 cut the error rate by a factor of about 2.14. The 101-qubit, distance-7 logical qubit outlived the best physical qubit on the chip by a factor of about 2.4.
  3. The estimated cost of breaking RSA keeps falling. In 2019 Gidney and Ekerå estimated that factoring a 2048-bit RSA modulus needs about 20 million noisy qubits for 8 hours. In 2025 Gidney lowered that to under a million noisy qubits for under a week, with the same hardware assumptions.

The vertical axis spans four orders of magnitude. None of these machines exists yet: today's largest devices have hundreds to a few thousand physical qubits. What matters is the trend, and the fact that most of the drop came from better algorithms and better error correction, not from waiting for hardware.

idea — how to read a headline number

Every resource estimate rests on assumptions: the error rate of a single gate (usually 0.1%), the time for one round of error correction (usually a microsecond), which qubits can interact, and how long you are willing to wait. Fewer qubits usually means a longer runtime. So when a number makes the news, ask four questions. What error rate? What connectivity? What runtime? Peer-reviewed, or a preprint? We will read these papers properly in Lecture 17.

Why hurry, if the machine does not exist? Because an adversary can record encrypted traffic today and decrypt it once one does. This is called harvest now, decrypt later. Court records, medical files and financial archives must stay secret for decades, so for them the clock is already running. This course teaches both sides of that story: the algorithms that break today's cryptography, and the error correction that decides when.

A hospital's records must stay confidential for 25 years, and no cryptographically relevant quantum computer exists yet. An adversary who copies the encrypted records off the network today puts them at risk .

What matters is when the data is recorded, compared with how long it must stay secret.
Harvest now, decrypt later: recorded ciphertext can be decrypted whenever a machine arrives, so for long-lived secrets the clock is already running.
history — forty-five years in five lines

1981. Richard Feynman argues that simulating nature efficiently needs a computer that is itself quantum. 1985. David Deutsch defines a universal quantum computer. 1994–96. Shor's factoring algorithm, then Lov Grover's search algorithm, show what such a machine could do. 2016. IBM puts a five-qubit processor on the cloud, and anyone can run a circuit. 2024. NIST standardizes the cryptography meant to survive it all. The theory came first, by decades, and engineering has been catching up ever since.

2 — Bits as vectors

A bit is in state 0 or state 1. It will pay off, almost immediately, to write these states as column vectors:

$$0\;\longleftrightarrow\; e_0=\begin{pmatrix}1\\[2pt] 0\end{pmatrix},\qquad 1\;\longleftrightarrow\; e_1=\begin{pmatrix}0\\[2pt] 1\end{pmatrix}.$$

A deterministic operation on a bit is then a matrix that sends basis vectors to basis vectors. The NOT operation swaps them:

$$\mathrm{NOT}=\begin{pmatrix}0 & 1\\[2pt] 1 & 0\end{pmatrix},\qquad \mathrm{NOT}\,e_0=e_1,\qquad \mathrm{NOT}\,e_1=e_0.$$

There are exactly four deterministic one-bit operations: the identity, NOT, "set to 0" and "set to 1". All four are matrices whose columns are basis vectors. Writing a bit this way looks like overkill. What it buys us is that the next step, adding randomness, needs no new ideas at all.

3 — Probabilistic bits

Suppose you do not know the value of a bit, but you do know the odds: it is 0 with probability \(p_0\) and 1 with probability \(p_1\). Stack the odds into a vector.

definition 1.1 — probabilistic state

A probabilistic state of a bit is a vector

$$p=\begin{pmatrix}p_0\\[2pt] p_1\end{pmatrix}=p_0\,e_0+p_1\,e_1,\qquad p_0,\;p_1\ge 0,\qquad p_0+p_1=1.$$

The last condition says the 1-norm \(\lVert p\rVert_1=|p_0|+|p_1|\) equals 1.

If a random process takes state \(j\) to state \(i\) with probability \(M_{ij}\), then by the law of total probability the new odds are \(p'_i=\sum_j M_{ij}\,p_j\). That is exactly the rule for multiplying a matrix by a vector: \(p'=Mp\).

definition 1.2 — stochastic matrix

A square matrix \(M\) is stochastic if all its entries are nonnegative and every column sums to 1. Column \(j\) is the probability distribution of the output when the input is \(j\). Stochastic matrices are exactly the matrices that turn probabilistic states into probabilistic states.

Tick every stochastic matrix (Definition 1.2).

  • \(\begin{pmatrix}0.5&0.3\\ 0.5&0.7\end{pmatrix}\)
  • \(\begin{pmatrix}0.7&0.3\\ 0.4&0.6\end{pmatrix}\)Here the rows sum to 1, but the columns sum to 1.1 and 0.9. Column \(j\) is the output distribution for input \(j\), so it is the columns that must sum to 1.
  • \(\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\ 0&0\end{pmatrix}\)
  • \(\begin{pmatrix}1.2&0\\ -0.2&1\end{pmatrix}\)The columns do sum to 1, but an entry is negative, and a probability cannot be negative.
  • \(\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\ 1&0\end{pmatrix}\)
Check two things for each matrix: no negative entries, and each column adds up to 1.
The first, third ("set to 0") and fifth (NOT) qualify. Deterministic operations are stochastic matrices whose columns happen to be basis vectors.
example 1.1 — a noisy memory cell

A memory cell holds a bit, but at every clock tick it flips with probability 0.1. The bit starts at 0. What are the odds after one, two and three ticks?

Column \(j\) of \(M\) describes what happens to state \(j\): stay with probability 0.9, flip with probability 0.1. So

$$M=\begin{pmatrix}0.9 & 0.1\\[2pt] 0.1 & 0.9\end{pmatrix},\qquad p^{(0)}=\begin{pmatrix}1\\[2pt] 0\end{pmatrix}.$$

One tick:

$$p^{(1)}=Mp^{(0)}=\begin{pmatrix}0.9\\[2pt] 0.1\end{pmatrix}.$$

Two ticks:

$$p^{(2)}=Mp^{(1)}=\begin{pmatrix}0.9(0.9)+0.1(0.1)\\[2pt] 0.1(0.9)+0.9(0.1)\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}0.82\\[2pt] 0.18\end{pmatrix}.$$

Three ticks:

$$p^{(3)}=Mp^{(2)}=\begin{pmatrix}0.756\\[2pt] 0.244\end{pmatrix}.$$

The chance of reading 0 decays 1, 0.9, 0.82, 0.756, … toward \(\tfrac12\). After enough ticks the cell is a fair coin, and all memory of the starting value is gone.

proposition 1.1 — a noisy bit forgets exponentially fast

A bit that flips with probability \(q\) at each tick and starts at 0 reads 0 after \(k\) ticks with probability

$$P^{(k)}(0)=\frac12+\frac12\,(1-2q)^k .$$

The matrix is

$$M=\begin{pmatrix}1-q & q\\[2pt] q & 1-q\end{pmatrix}.$$

Check directly that

$$M\begin{pmatrix}1\\[2pt] 1\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}1\\[2pt] 1\end{pmatrix},\qquad M\begin{pmatrix}1\\[2pt] -1\end{pmatrix}=(1-2q)\begin{pmatrix}1\\[2pt] -1\end{pmatrix},$$

so these are eigenvectors with eigenvalues \(1\) and \(1-2q\). Write the starting state in this basis and apply \(M\) \(k\) times:

$$e_0=\frac12\begin{pmatrix}1\\[2pt] 1\end{pmatrix}+\frac12\begin{pmatrix}1\\[2pt] -1\end{pmatrix} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad M^k e_0=\frac12\begin{pmatrix}1\\[2pt] 1\end{pmatrix}+\frac{(1-2q)^k}{2}\begin{pmatrix}1\\[2pt] -1\end{pmatrix}.$$

The top entry is \(\tfrac12+\tfrac12(1-2q)^k\). With \(q=0.1\) this is \(\tfrac12+\tfrac12(0.8)^k\), which gives 0.9, 0.82 and 0.756 as before.

A cell flips with probability \(q=0.25\) at each tick and starts at 0. What is the chance of reading 0 after three ticks?

Use Proposition 1.1: first compute \(1-2q\), then raise it to the number of ticks.
\(1-2q=0.5\), so \(P^{(3)}(0)=\tfrac12+\tfrac12(0.5)^3=\tfrac12+\tfrac1{16}=0.5625\).

Noisy-bit explorer

Set the flip probability and step the cell forward. Try \(q=0.5\) (a fair coin after one tick), \(q=1\) (that is just NOT), and \(q=0.9\). Why does the last one zig-zag?

idea — the norm that is kept

Stochastic matrices keep the 1-norm of a probabilistic state, but not its ordinary length. After one tick, \(\lVert p^{(1)}\rVert_1=0.9+0.1=1\), while \(\lVert p^{(1)}\rVert_2=\sqrt{0.81+0.01}=\sqrt{0.82}\approx0.906\). The whole of quantum computing grows out of swapping the roles of these two norms. Hold on to that sentence until Section 6.

The fair coin \(F=\tfrac12\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\ 1&1\end{pmatrix}\) sends \(e_0\) to \((\tfrac12,\tfrac12)\). Its 1-norm and its 2-norm .

\(\lVert p\rVert_1=|p_0|+|p_1|\) and \(\lVert p\rVert_2=\sqrt{p_0^2+p_1^2}\).
The 1-norm stays 1, as it must for a stochastic matrix; the 2-norm falls from 1 to \(\sqrt{1/2}\approx0.707\). Stochastic matrices keep the first norm only.

4 — Reading a bit is sampling

You cannot look at a probabilistic state. When you read the bit you see one value, 0 or 1, and never the vector \(p\). To learn \(p_0\) you prepare the same state many times, read it each time, and estimate. In MAT301 language each reading is a Bernoulli\((p_0)\) trial, and after \(n\) readings the sample proportion \(\hat p\) gives the approximate 95% confidence interval

$$\hat p\;\pm\;1.96\sqrt{\frac{\hat p\,(1-\hat p)}{n}} .$$

After \(n=400\) readings the sample proportion is \(\hat p=0.5\). What is the half-width of the 95% interval?

Plug into \(1.96\sqrt{\hat p(1-\hat p)/n}\): here \(\hat p(1-\hat p)=0.25\).
\(\sqrt{0.25/400}=0.025\), so the half-width is \(1.96\times0.025=0.049\), and the interval is about \((0.451,\,0.549)\).
example 1.2 — how many readings?

A device should produce 0 with probability 0.9. (a) With \(n=100\) readings and \(\hat p=0.9\), what is the 95% interval? (b) What goes wrong with \(n=10\)? (c) How many readings guarantee a half-width of at most 0.01, whatever \(p_0\) is?

(a) The half-width is \(1.96\sqrt{0.9\cdot 0.1/100}=0.0588\), so the interval is about \((0.841,\;0.959)\).

(b) With \(n=10\) the half-width is \(0.186\), which gives \((0.714,\;1.086)\): an interval for a probability that runs past 1. The normal approximation is poor when \(n\) is small or \(p\) is near 0 or 1. The Wilson interval stays inside \([0,1]\); here it is \((0.596,\;0.982)\).

(c) Since \(p(1-p)\le\tfrac14\), we need \(1.96\sqrt{0.25/n}\le 0.01\), that is, \(n\ge 1.96^2(0.25)/0.01^2=9604\).

Sampling a bit

Pick the true chance of reading 0, then add readings. The gold trace is your estimate; the shaded band is the 95% band around the truth. Watch the normal interval misbehave for small \(n\).

To cut the half-width of a 95% interval in half, you need about readings.

The half-width is proportional to \(1/\sqrt n\). What must \(\sqrt n\) do?
\(\sqrt n\) must double, so \(n\) must be multiplied by 4. Precision is expensive: every extra decimal place costs a factor of 100 in shots.

5 — Two bits and the tensor product

Two bits have four possible values, 00, 01, 10, 11, so a probabilistic state of two bits is a vector in \(\mathbb R^4\) with nonnegative entries adding up to 1:

$$p=\begin{pmatrix}p_{00}\\[2pt] p_{01}\\[2pt] p_{10}\\[2pt] p_{11}\end{pmatrix}.$$

If the bits are independent, with odds \(a=(a_0,a_1)\) for the left bit and \(b=(b_0,b_1)\) for the right, then \(p_{ij}=a_i\,b_j\). This way of combining two vectors has a name.

definition 1.3 — tensor product of vectors $$\begin{pmatrix}a_0\\[2pt] a_1\end{pmatrix}\otimes\begin{pmatrix}b_0\\[2pt] b_1\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}a_0b_0\\[2pt] a_0b_1\\[2pt] a_1b_0\\[2pt] a_1b_1\end{pmatrix}.$$

Entry \(ij\) is \(a_i b_j\), and the first factor labels the left bit.

The left bit has odds \(a=(0.7,\,0.3)\) and the right bit, independently, \(b=(0.2,\,0.8)\). Find the entries \(p_{01}\) and \(p_{10}\) of \(a\otimes b\).

Entry \(ij\) is \(a_i\,b_j\), and the first index belongs to the left bit: \(p_{01}=a_0\,b_1\).
\(p_{01}=0.7\times0.8=0.56\) and \(p_{10}=0.3\times0.2=0.06\). Swapping the order of the factors would swap these two entries.
example 1.3 — independent and correlated

(a) The left bit is the noisy cell after one tick, \(a=(0.9,\,0.1)\); the right bit is an independent biased coin, \(b=(0.6,\,0.4)\). Find the joint odds. (b) Two sealed envelopes hold the same random bit, so \(p=(\tfrac12,\,0,\,0,\,\tfrac12)\). Is this a tensor product of two one-bit states?

(a) Multiply entry by entry:

$$\begin{pmatrix}0.9\\[2pt] 0.1\end{pmatrix}\otimes\begin{pmatrix}0.6\\[2pt] 0.4\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}0.54\\[2pt] 0.36\\[2pt] 0.06\\[2pt] 0.04\end{pmatrix}.$$

(b) If \(p=a\otimes b\), then \(p_{00}\,p_{11}=a_0b_0a_1b_1=p_{01}\,p_{10}\). Here \(p_{00}p_{11}=\tfrac14\) but \(p_{01}p_{10}=0\), so no: the bits are correlated. In general a two-bit distribution is a product exactly when the table

$$\begin{pmatrix}p_{00} & p_{01}\\[2pt] p_{10} & p_{11}\end{pmatrix}$$

has determinant zero.

Keep part (b) in mind. In Lecture 5 the same determinant test will detect entanglement, the quantum cousin of correlation, and in Lab 2 you will measure that it is strictly stronger than anything two envelopes can do.

idea — the exponential wall

\(n\) bits need a vector of \(2^n\) probabilities. A classical computer never stores it: it simulates randomness by following one random path at a time. For quantum states that shortcut fails, because of interference (next section), and this is both why quantum computers are hard to simulate and why they might be powerful.

6 — The quantum leap: amplitudes and interference

Here is the whole course in one sentence: replace nonnegative probabilities that add up to 1 with complex amplitudes whose squared sizes add up to 1.

definition 1.4 (preview, made precise in lecture 3) — qubit state

A qubit state is a vector

$$\psi=\begin{pmatrix}\alpha\\[2pt] \beta\end{pmatrix}\in\mathbb C^2\quad\text{with}\quad |\alpha|^2+|\beta|^2=1 ,$$

that is, with 2-norm equal to 1. Reading it gives 0 with probability \(|\alpha|^2\) and 1 with probability \(|\beta|^2\). We will soon write it \(|\psi\rangle=\alpha|0\rangle+\beta|1\rangle\).

A qubit is in the state \(\psi=\Big(\tfrac{1+i}{\sqrt3},\ \tfrac{i}{\sqrt3}\Big)\). With what probability does reading it give 0?

The probability is \(|\alpha|^2\), the squared size of the amplitude, not \(\alpha^2\). For \(1+i\), \(|1+i|^2=1^2+1^2\).
\(|1+i|^2=2\), so \(P(0)=\tfrac23\) and \(P(1)=|i|^2/3=\tfrac13\); the two add to 1, as Definition 1.4 requires.

Operations must now preserve the 2-norm instead of the 1-norm. The matrices that do this are called unitary (Lecture 2), and they replace stochastic matrices. The most important one is the Hadamard gate

$$H=\frac{1}{\sqrt2}\begin{pmatrix}1 & 1\\[2pt] 1 & -1\end{pmatrix}.$$
example 1.4 — same odds, different states

Compare \(\psi_1=(0.6,\;0.8)\) and \(\psi_2=(0.6,\;-0.8)\). Do they read the same? Are they the same?

Both have \(0.6^2+0.8^2=0.36+0.64=1\), and reading either gives 0 with probability 0.36 and 1 with probability 0.64. Read directly, they cannot be told apart. But they are different vectors, and one Hadamard separates them completely:

$$H\psi_1=\frac{1}{\sqrt2}\begin{pmatrix}1.4\\[2pt] -0.2\end{pmatrix}\approx\begin{pmatrix}0.990\\[2pt] -0.141\end{pmatrix},$$ $$H\psi_2=\frac{1}{\sqrt2}\begin{pmatrix}-0.2\\[2pt] 1.4\end{pmatrix}\approx\begin{pmatrix}-0.141\\[2pt] 0.990\end{pmatrix}.$$

Now \(\psi_1\) reads 0 with probability 0.98 and \(\psi_2\) reads 0 with probability 0.02. The sign was invisible, but it decided the outcome.

Interference: the one thing probabilities cannot do

Apply a "fair coin" twice to a bit that starts at 0. Classically the fair coin is the stochastic matrix

$$F=\frac12\begin{pmatrix}1 & 1\\[2pt] 1 & 1\end{pmatrix},\qquad F^2e_0=\begin{pmatrix}\tfrac12\\[4pt] \tfrac12\end{pmatrix}:$$

randomizing twice is still random. The quantum version is \(H\). One application gives \(He_0=(\tfrac{1}{\sqrt2},\tfrac{1}{\sqrt2})\), which reads 0 or 1 with probability \(\tfrac12\) each, exactly like the coin. The second application gives

$$H^2e_0=\frac12\begin{pmatrix}1+1\\[2pt] 1-1\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}1\\[2pt] 0\end{pmatrix}.$$

The output is 0 with certainty. The figure shows why. Each path from input to output carries the product of the amplitudes along it. The two paths into 1 carry \(+\tfrac12\) and \(-\tfrac12\) and cancel, while the two paths into 0 reinforce. Probabilities, being nonnegative, can only pile up. Amplitudes can also cancel. Every algorithm in this course, from Deutsch–Jozsa to Grover to Shor, works by arranging for the wrong answers to cancel.

The state \(\big(\tfrac1{\sqrt2},\,-\tfrac1{\sqrt2}\big)\) reads 0 or 1 with probability \(\tfrac12\) each, just like a fair coin. Apply \(H\) once and then read it. What do you see?

  • 0 or 1 with probability \(\tfrac12\) eachThat is what the classical coin \(F\) would do. Track the amplitudes instead: the two paths into one of the outcomes cancel.
  • 0 with certaintyCheck the sign: the paths into 0 carry \(+\tfrac12\) and \(-\tfrac12\).
  • 1 with certainty
  • 0 and 1 at the same timeA reading always gives one outcome; the question is only how likely each is.
Multiply: \(H\big(\tfrac1{\sqrt2},-\tfrac1{\sqrt2}\big)=\tfrac12\big(1-1,\ 1+1\big)\).
The amplitudes into 0 cancel and those into 1 reinforce, giving \((0,1)\). A minus sign that reading could not see has decided the outcome.

Step through it yourself

Choose the coin or the Hadamard gate, then step. Watch what happens at the last step on the bottom row.

idea — a misconception worth killing early

"A qubit is 0 and 1 at the same time, so a quantum computer tries every answer in parallel." This is misleading. A qubit in the state \(He_0\) gives one random answer when read, just like a coin. The power comes from interference: steering amplitudes so that when you finally do read, the answer you want is the likely one. No cancellation, no speedup.

history — what is a qubit, physically?

Any two-level quantum system will do, and the mathematics is identical for all of them. Superconducting circuits (IBM, Google) are tiny electrical loops cooled to near absolute zero: fast gates, nearest-neighbour wiring; your labs run on these. Trapped ions (Quantinuum, IonQ) are single charged atoms held by electric fields and steered by lasers: very accurate, slower. Neutral atoms (QuEra, Atom Computing, Pasqal) sit in arrays of laser "tweezers" that can be rearranged mid-computation. Photons (PsiQuantum, Xanadu) travel at room temperature and suit networking. This course treats them all through one abstraction: vectors, unitaries and measurements.

7 — Try it in code

tool — the noisy bit and a confidence interval (numpy) 
import numpy as np

M = np.array([[0.9, 0.1],
              [0.1, 0.9]])      # each column sums to 1
p = np.array([1.0, 0.0])        # start: definitely 0

for k in range(1, 4):
    p = M @ p
    print(k, p.round(4))

rng = np.random.default_rng(seed=1)
n = 1000
reads_zero = rng.random(n) < 0.9    # n reads of a bit with P(0) = 0.9
p_hat = reads_zero.mean()
half = 1.96 * np.sqrt(p_hat * (1 - p_hat) / n)
print(f"p_hat = {p_hat:.3f}, 95% CI = ({p_hat - half:.3f}, {p_hat + half:.3f})")

2 [0.82 0.18]
3 [0.756 0.244]

8 — Summary, glossary, exercises

summary
  • Deterministic states of a bit are basis vectors. Probabilistic states are nonnegative vectors with 1-norm 1, and random steps are stochastic matrices.
  • Reading a state is sampling. Estimates carry error bars that shrink like \(1/\sqrt n\).
  • Independent bits combine by the tensor product. Correlated bits do not factor, and a determinant detects it.
  • Quantum states use amplitudes with 2-norm 1. Amplitudes can be negative (or complex), so paths can cancel. This interference is the source of every quantum speedup.
  • 2024–26: post-quantum standards published, error correction below threshold, and RSA-2048 estimates down by orders of magnitude.

Glossary for 2026

physical qubit
One actual two-level device on a chip, with its own error rate.
logical qubit
A qubit encoded across many physical qubits by an error-correcting code, so that it is far more reliable than any of its parts (Lectures 21–23).
below threshold
Physical errors are rare enough that making the code bigger makes the logical qubit better. Willow (2024) was the first clear surface-code demonstration.
fault-tolerant quantum computer
A machine that computes on logical qubits, correcting errors throughout. None exists yet at useful scale.
utility / advantage
"Utility": results useful to scientists that are hard to get by brute-force classical simulation. "Advantage": a verified speedup over the best classical method on a task that matters. The second bar is much higher (Lecture 24).
CRQC
A cryptographically relevant quantum computer: one able to run Shor's algorithm against real key sizes.
PQC
Post-quantum cryptography: ordinary algorithms, such as ML-KEM and ML-DSA, believed secure against quantum attack. It runs on today's computers.
QKD
Quantum key distribution: using quantum states themselves to agree on a secret key (Lecture 20). A different approach from PQC.
harvest now, decrypt later
Recording encrypted data today to decrypt once a CRQC exists. It is the reason migration is urgent now.

  4. \(n\ge1.96^2(0.25)/0.02^2=2401\). For \(p_0\approx0.9\): \(1.96^2(0.09)/0.02^2=864.4\), so 865 readings.
  5. For \(q=0.9\), \(1-2q=-0.8\) is negative, so \((1-2q)^k\) alternates in sign and the odds overshoot \(\tfrac12\) on alternate ticks while still converging. For \(q=0.1\) we need \(\tfrac12(0.8)^k\le0.01\), i.e. \(0.8^k\le0.02\), so \(k\ge\ln(0.02)/\ln(0.8)\approx17.5\): the first such tick is \(k=18\).
