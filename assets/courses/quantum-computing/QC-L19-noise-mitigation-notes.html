1 — Relaxation and dephasing: T1 and T2

A superconducting qubit is a tiny electrical circuit at about 10 millikelvin, and \(|1\rangle\) is its excited state. Left alone it loses energy to its surroundings and drops to \(|0\rangle\); and even when it keeps its energy, slow fluctuations in its frequency scramble the relative phase between \(|0\rangle\) and \(|1\rangle\). The two processes are measured separately.

definition 19.1 — T1 and T2

The relaxation time \(T_1\) and the dephasing time \(T_2\) are the time constants in

$$P\big(\text{still }|1\rangle\text{ after time }t\big)=e^{-t/T_1},\qquad |\rho_{01}(t)|=|\rho_{01}(0)|\,e^{-t/T_2}.$$

The first is amplitude damping (Lecture 18) with \(\gamma(t)=1-e^{-t/T_1}\). The second is the decay of the coherence, the off-diagonal entry; its size \(|\rho_{01}|\) is half the length of the Bloch vector's \(xy\) part.

A qubit has \(T_1=80\) μs and \(T_2=50\) μs. After it waits 40 μs, what fraction of its \(|1\rangle\) population survives, and what fraction of its coherence \(|\rho_{01}|\)?

Each quantity decays with its own time constant: \(e^{-t/T_1}\) for the population, \(e^{-t/T_2}\) for the coherence.
\(e^{-40/80}\approx0.607\) and \(e^{-40/50}\approx0.449\). With \(T_2\lt T_1\), the coherence goes first.

Relaxation destroys coherence too: a qubit that has decayed to \(|0\rangle\) has no relative phase left. Separating the two effects gives

$$\frac1{T_2}=\frac1{2T_1}+\frac1{T_\varphi},$$

where \(T_\varphi\) is the time constant of pure dephasing (frequency noise). Since \(1/T_\varphi\ge0\), always \(T_2\le2T_1\). The factor 2 is because coherence is an amplitude (it decays like \(\sqrt{e^{-t/T_1}}\)) while \(T_1\) describes a probability.

Suppose a qubit has no pure dephasing at all (\(1/T_\varphi=0\)), so relaxation is the only source of decoherence. Then \(T_2=\) .

Set \(1/T_\varphi=0\) in \(1/T_2=1/(2T_1)+1/T_\varphi\).
\(1/T_2=1/(2T_1)\), so \(T_2=2T_1\): the upper limit. Any frequency noise only makes \(T_2\) shorter.

These are exponential decay models of exactly the kind fitted in an introductory statistics course: an experiment prepares \(|1\rangle\), waits \(t\), measures, repeats a few thousand times for each of 30 or so values of \(t\), and fits \(e^{-t/T_1}\) to the fraction still in \(|1\rangle\). \(T_2\) is measured the same way with a superposition (a Ramsey experiment, which measures a slightly shorter \(T_2^*\), or an echo experiment, which measures \(T_2\); calibration tables report the echo value).

example 19.1 — how long is long?

A qubit has \(T_1=150\) μs and \(T_2=90\) μs. (a) A circuit keeps it waiting for 10 μs. What fraction of its \(|1\rangle\) population and of its coherence survives? (b) What is \(T_\varphi\)? (c) Two-qubit gates take about 84 ns and single-qubit gates about 24 ns on the device of Section 2. How many layers of "one two-qubit gate plus one single-qubit gate" fit in 10 μs?

(a) \(e^{-10/150}\approx0.936\) and \(e^{-10/90}\approx0.895\): about 6% of the population and 11% of the coherence are lost. (b) \(1/T_\varphi=1/90-1/300\), so \(T_\varphi\approx128.6\) μs. (c) One layer takes \(84+24=108\) ns, so 10 μs is about 92 layers, and 100 layers take 10.8 μs. A circuit of depth 100 already loses about a tenth of its coherence to idling alone, before counting any gate errors.

2 — The other errors, and reading a calibration table

Decay during idling is only part of the story. A device's calibration data, refreshed every day or so, lists for each qubit and each coupled pair:

  • Gate errors. The average infidelity of each native gate, measured by randomized benchmarking: random sequences of gates of increasing length whose ideal product is the identity, with the survival probability fitted to an exponential in the length. On IBM's Heron processors (2025–26) single-qubit errors are around \(2\times10^{-4}\) and two-qubit errors around \(2\)–\(4\times10^{-3}\), with the best pairs below \(10^{-3}\) (trapped-ion machines reach \(10^{-3}\) or better); the dominant term in most circuits.
  • Readout error. The probability of reading the wrong value: \(P(1\mid0)\) and \(P(0\mid1)\), typically of order 1%, often unequal because \(|1\rangle\) can decay during the 1–2 μs measurement.
  • Crosstalk. A gate on one pair disturbing neighbouring qubits; not captured by per-gate numbers.
  • Leakage. The physical circuit has more than two levels; a gate can leave the qubit in \(|2\rangle\), outside the computational space. Leakage is especially harmful for error correction, which assumes errors are Paulis on qubits.

The code in Section 8 prints part of the calibration snapshot that Qiskit ships for the 156-qubit IBM device ibm_fez: median \(T_1\approx145\) μs, median \(T_2\approx88\) μs, median readout error about 0.76%, and median two-qubit (CZ) error about 0.39%, with a wide spread between qubits (qubit 0 has \(T_1\) under 50 μs; seven couplers are marked unusable with error 1). These are a snapshot from February 2025, not current values; in class we pull the live numbers instead, and they will be different.

A crude but useful rule: if a circuit has \(G\) two-qubit gates of error \(\varepsilon\), the probability that none of them fails is about \((1-\varepsilon)^G\approx e^{-\varepsilon G}\). At \(\varepsilon=0.004\) that is 67% for 100 gates and 2% for 1,000. Everything in the rest of this lecture is about doing better than this rule suggests without full error correction.

A circuit has 500 two-qubit gates, each with error \(\varepsilon=0.003\). Estimate the probability that none of them fails.

\((1-\varepsilon)^G\approx e^{-\varepsilon G}\), and \(\varepsilon G\) here is 1.5.
\(e^{-1.5}\approx0.22\) (and \(0.997^{500}\approx0.223\)): most runs of this circuit contain at least one gate error.

3 — Transpilation: what the compiler does to your circuit

The circuits we wrote in Blocks I and II used any gate on any pair of qubits. Hardware offers a small basis of native gates (on IBM's Heron devices: \(R_Z(\theta)\), \(\sqrt X\), \(X\), and \(CZ\), plus optional fractional two-qubit gates) and a fixed coupling map, the graph of pairs that can interact. On IBM's Heron devices it is a heavy-hex lattice, in which every qubit has at most three neighbours (IBM's newer Nighthawk chips use a square lattice with four). Transpilation turns an abstract circuit into one the device can run, in stages:

  1. Layout: choose which physical qubit holds each circuit qubit, preferring well-calibrated qubits that are close together.
  2. Routing: when a two-qubit gate acts on qubits that are not neighbours, insert SWAPs to bring them together. Each SWAP costs three CNOTs (Lecture 7).
  3. Translation: rewrite every gate in the basis (for example \(\text{CNOT}=(I\otimes H)\,CZ\,(I\otimes H)\), and \(H\) into \(R_Z\) and \(\sqrt X\)).
  4. Optimization: cancel and merge gates; higher optimization_level works harder.

Routing is why a good layout matters, and why algorithms whose interactions are local in space (like the surface code of Lecture 23) suit this hardware. It also means that a circuit's depth and gate count on paper are lower bounds: always report the counts after transpilation.

Six qubits sit on a line, 0–1–2–3–4–5. A circuit asks for one CNOT with control 0 and target 5. If the router moves the control along the line until it is next to the target, how many CNOTs does the routed circuit contain?

The control must move from position 0 to position 4, one SWAP per step, and each SWAP costs three CNOTs. Then add the CNOT itself.
Four SWAPs, \(4\cdot3=12\) CNOTs, plus the original one: 13. One gate on paper, thirteen on the device.

4 — Readout-error mitigation

Readout errors act on the classical outcome after the quantum part is over, so they can be described by a stochastic matrix, like the Markov chains of HW1.

definition 19.2 — confusion matrix

For one qubit with \(e_0=P(\text{read }1\mid0)\) and \(e_1=P(\text{read }0\mid1)\), the confusion matrix \(A\) has columns indexed by the true value and rows by the value read:

$$A=\begin{pmatrix}1-e_0&e_1\\ e_0&1-e_1\end{pmatrix},\qquad \vec p_{\text{obs}}=A\,\vec p_{\text{true}} .$$

It is estimated by preparing \(|0\rangle\) and \(|1\rangle\) many times and recording what is read. For \(n\) qubits with independent readout errors, the full \(2^n\times2^n\) matrix is \(A_{n-1}\otimes\cdots\otimes A_0\).

A qubit has \(e_0=0.03\) and \(e_1=0.06\), and its true distribution is \(p_{\text{true}}=(0.8,\,0.2)\). With what frequency is 1 read?

Use the second row of \(A\): \(p_{\text{obs}}(1)=e_0\,p_{\text{true}}(0)+(1-e_1)\,p_{\text{true}}(1)\).
\(0.03\cdot0.8+0.94\cdot0.2=0.024+0.188=0.212\): true 0s misread as 1 add more than true 1s misread as 0 take away.
proposition 19.1 — inverting one qubit's readout

If \(e_0+e_1\ne1\), then \(A\) is invertible and the corrected probability of reading 1 is

$$p_{\text{true}}(1)=\frac{p_{\text{obs}}(1)-e_0}{1-e_0-e_1}.$$

\(\det A=(1-e_0)(1-e_1)-e_0e_1=1-e_0-e_1\). The second row of \(\vec p_{\text{obs}}=A\vec p_{\text{true}}\) reads \(p_{\text{obs}}(1)=e_0\,p_{\text{true}}(0)+(1-e_1)\,p_{\text{true}}(1)\). Substitute \(p_{\text{true}}(0)=1-p_{\text{true}}(1)\): \(p_{\text{obs}}(1)=e_0+(1-e_0-e_1)\,p_{\text{true}}(1)\), and solve.

example 19.2 — correcting a measured probability

Readout errors are \(e_0=0.02\) and \(e_1=0.05\). (a) An experiment reads 1 with frequency 0.30. What is the mitigated estimate? (b) Another reads 1 with frequency 0.015. What goes wrong?

(a) \((0.30-0.02)/(1-0.07)=0.28/0.93\approx0.301\). The correction is small here because the two errors nearly cancel near 0.3: \(|1\rangle\) results lost to \(e_1\) are replaced by \(|0\rangle\) results misread as 1.

(b) \((0.015-0.02)/0.93\approx-0.0054\): a negative "probability". The observed frequency is itself random, and when the true value is near 0, shot noise can put \(p_{\text{obs}}(1)\) below \(e_0\). The inverse of a stochastic matrix is not stochastic. In practice one projects the result back to the nearest probability vector (here: 0) or uses a constrained least-squares fit.

The cost of readout mitigation is mild for few qubits: the variance of the estimate is multiplied by about \(1/(1-e_0-e_1)^2\) per qubit. The difficulty is size: a \(2^n\times2^n\) matrix cannot even be stored for \(n=50\). Practical methods assume a tensor-product (or nearest-neighbour) structure, or mitigate only expectation values of Pauli observables, where each qubit's correction is a single factor. 5 — Zero-noise extrapolation

Gate errors cannot be undone after the fact the way readout errors can, because they act in the middle of the computation. Zero-noise extrapolation (ZNE) takes a different route: we cannot reduce the noise, but we can increase it in a controlled way, measure how the answer changes, and extrapolate back to zero noise.

definition 19.3 — zero-noise extrapolation by gate folding

Let \(E(\lambda)\) be the measured expectation value of an observable when the noise is scaled by a factor \(\lambda\ge1\), with \(E(1)\) the ordinary noisy run. Gate folding replaces a gate \(G\) by \(G\,G^\dagger G\): the same unitary, three times the noise. Folding every gate \(k\) times gives \(\lambda=2k+1\in\{1,3,5,\dots\}\). ZNE fits a model to \(E(\lambda_1),\dots,E(\lambda_m)\) and reports the fitted value at \(\lambda=0\).

The answer depends on the model. A linear fit assumes \(E(\lambda)\approx a+b\lambda\). Richardson extrapolation passes a polynomial of degree \(m-1\) through all \(m\) points. An exponential fit assumes \(E(\lambda)\approx a\,e^{-b\lambda}\), which is what a depolarizing channel after every gate produces: each use shrinks the Bloch vector, and an observable's expectation value, by a fixed factor. Each extrapolated value is a fixed linear combination \(\sum_iw_iE(\lambda_i)\) of the measurements (for the linear and polynomial fits), with \(\sum_iw_i=1\).

A ZNE experiment measures \(E(1)=0.60\) and \(E(3)=0.45\). What is the linear extrapolation to \(\lambda=0\)?

The slope per unit of \(\lambda\) is \((0.45-0.60)/2\). Going from \(\lambda=1\) back to \(\lambda=0\) is one unit.
The slope is \(-0.075\), so \(E(0)=0.60+0.075=0.675\). (The exponential model would give \(0.60/\sqrt{0.75}\approx0.693\): the model matters.)
example 19.3 — ZNE by hand

An observable whose noiseless value is unknown is measured at \(\lambda=1\) and \(\lambda=3\), giving \(E(1)=0.80\) and \(E(3)=0.56\). Extrapolate to \(\lambda=0\) with a straight line and with an exponential.

Linear: the slope is \((0.56-0.80)/2=-0.12\) per unit of \(\lambda\), so \(E(0)=0.80+0.12=0.92\). Exponential: \(E(3)/E(1)=0.7=e^{-2b}\), so each unit of \(\lambda\) multiplies by \(\sqrt{0.7}\approx0.837\), and \(E(0)=0.80/\sqrt{0.7}\approx0.956\). Two reasonable models differ by 0.036, more than the shot noise of a typical run. Choosing the model is the main source of systematic error in ZNE.

proposition 19.2 — the price of extrapolation

If the \(E(\lambda_i)\) are independent with standard deviations \(\sigma_i\), an extrapolated value \(\sum_iw_iE(\lambda_i)\) has standard deviation \(\sqrt{\sum_iw_i^2\sigma_i^2}\). For \(\lambda=1,3,5\), the Richardson weights are \(\big(\tfrac{15}8,-\tfrac54,\tfrac38\big)\) and the least-squares-line weights are \(\big(\tfrac{13}{12},\tfrac13,-\tfrac5{12}\big)\). With equal \(\sigma\), the variance is multiplied by \(\sum w_i^2\approx5.22\) and \(1.46\) respectively.

The variance formula is the variance of a linear combination of independent variables. Richardson: the quadratic through the three points evaluated at 0 is \(\sum_iE(\lambda_i)\ell_i(0)\) with the Lagrange basis \(\ell_i(0)=\prod_{j\ne i}\frac{0-\lambda_j}{\lambda_i-\lambda_j}\): \(\ell_1(0)=\frac{(-3)(-5)}{(-2)(-4)}=\frac{15}8\), \(\ell_3(0)=\frac{(-1)(-5)}{(2)(-2)}=-\frac54\), \(\ell_5(0)=\frac{(-1)(-3)}{(4)(2)}=\frac38\). Then \(\sum w_i^2=\frac{225+100+9}{64}=\frac{334}{64}\approx5.22\). The least-squares line through \((\lambda_i,E_i)\) has intercept \(\bar E-\bar\lambda\,\hat b\) with \(\hat b=\sum(\lambda_i-\bar\lambda)E_i/\sum(\lambda_i-\bar\lambda)^2\), \(\bar\lambda=3\), \(\sum(\lambda_i-3)^2=8\), so \(w_i=\frac13-\frac{3(\lambda_i-3)}8\): \(\frac{13}{12},\frac13,-\frac5{12}\), with \(\sum w_i^2=\frac{169+16+25}{144}\approx1.46\).

Compared with simply reporting the raw noisy value \(E(1)\), the Richardson extrapolation from \(\lambda=1,3,5\) (with the same shots per point) has

  • smaller bias and smaller varianceExtrapolation is not free: the weights \(\tfrac{15}8,-\tfrac54,\tfrac38\) amplify the shot noise of each point.
  • smaller bias, but larger variance
  • larger bias, but smaller varianceThe raw value is the biased one: it is the noisy answer, with no attempt to remove the noise.
  • the same variance, since it uses three times as many shotsThe weights enter squared: \(\sum w_i^2\approx5.22\), much more than 1, and more shots at the folded points do not cancel that.
Proposition 19.2: what is \(\sum w_i^2\) for Richardson, and what is it for the raw value (one weight, equal to 1)?
Richardson removes the noise exactly if \(E(\lambda)\) is quadratic, so its bias is small, but its variance is about 5.2 times that of one raw point. Bias is traded for variance.

In the figure, the raw value has standard error 0.006, the linear extrapolation 0.009 and the Richardson extrapolation 0.017 (the folded runs are also noisier, since their \(E\) is smaller). To keep the same precision, Richardson needs about \(0.017^2/0.006^2\approx8\) times as many shots. The more aggressive the model, the smaller the bias and the larger the variance: the usual statistical trade-off.

6 — Twirling: making errors simple

Not all errors are random. A gate that is miscalibrated by a small angle \(\varepsilon\) applies \(R_X(\varepsilon)\) every time, a coherent error. Coherent errors add up in amplitude: after \(n\) repetitions the state has rotated by \(n\varepsilon\), and the error probability is \(\sin^2(n\varepsilon/2)\), which grows like \(n^2\varepsilon^2\). A stochastic error that flips the qubit with probability \(p\) each time grows only like \(np\).

Pauli twirling converts the first kind into the second. Before each noisy gate apply a random Pauli \(P\), and after it the Pauli that undoes \(P\) (for a Clifford gate this is another Pauli, which can be merged into neighbouring gates, so the ideal circuit is unchanged). Averaged over the random choices, the error becomes a Pauli channel: a mixture \(\sum_Pq_PP\rho P\). For the over-rotation, \(\tfrac14\sum_PPR_X(\varepsilon)P\,\rho\,PR_X(\varepsilon)^\dagger P=\cos^2\tfrac\varepsilon2\,\rho+\sin^2\tfrac\varepsilon2\,X\rho X\) (checked numerically in verify_L19.py), a bit flip with \(p=\sin^2(\varepsilon/2)\).

For small total errors, doubling the number of repetitions \(n\) multiplies the error probability of a coherent over-rotation by about and that of the twirled (stochastic) error by about .

Coherent: \(\sin^2(n\varepsilon/2)\approx n^2\varepsilon^2/4\). Stochastic: about \(np\).
The coherent probability grows like \(n^2\), so it quadruples; the Pauli error grows like \(n\), so it doubles. That gap is what twirling buys.
example 19.4 — coherent versus twirled

A gate over-rotates by \(\varepsilon=0.02\) rad and is applied 100 times to \(|0\rangle\). Compare the probability of reading 1 with and without twirling.

Coherent: \(\sin^2(100\cdot0.02/2)=\sin^2(1)\approx0.708\). Twirled: each gate is a bit flip with \(p=\sin^2(0.01)\approx10^{-4}\); after \(n\) uses the flip probability is \(\tfrac12\big(1-(1-2p)^n\big)\approx0.0099\). Twirling did not make the gate better on average (the average fidelity of one gate is unchanged); it made the errors stop adding coherently. It also makes the noise the kind that ZNE's models and error correction's analysis assume. This is also called randomized compiling (Wallman and Emerson, 2016).

7 — Mitigation versus correction, and the "utility" experiments

Every mitigation method trades shots for bias. The most powerful one, probabilistic error cancellation, writes the inverse of each gate's noise channel as a combination of physical operations with some negative coefficients, and samples circuits from it; the price is a factor \(\gamma^2\) more shots per gate, where \(\gamma\gt1\) grows with the gate's error. Over a circuit of \(N\) gates the shot count is multiplied by \(\gamma^{2N}\). With \(\gamma=1.002\) (an illustrative value for a gate with error of order \(10^{-3}\)), that is a factor of 54 for \(N=1{,}000\) gates and about \(2\times10^{17}\) for \(N=10{,}000\). ZNE's variance grows more slowly, but its bias grows with the circuit size instead.

With \(\gamma=1.002\) per gate, by what factor does probabilistic error cancellation multiply the number of shots for a circuit of \(N=500\) gates?

The factor is \(\gamma^{2N}\), and \(\gamma^{2N}=e^{2N\ln\gamma}\approx e^{2N(\gamma-1)}\).
\(1.002^{1000}\approx e^{2}\approx7.4\). Ten times more gates would give \(e^{20}\approx5\times10^8\): the cost is exponential in \(N\).
caution — mitigation does not scale

Mitigation estimates expectation values; it never produces a less noisy quantum state, and its cost grows exponentially with the total error \(\varepsilon N\) of the circuit. It extends what noisy devices can do by a constant factor. It cannot run Shor's algorithm on RSA-2048, which needs billions of gates (Lecture 17). That requires error correction, in which the cost grows only polylogarithmically with the circuit size, as long as the physical error rate is below a threshold (Lectures 21–23).

Between these two regimes sits the current "utility" era. In 2023, IBM researchers (Kim et al., Nature 618, 500) ran the time evolution of a 127-spin Ising model on a 127-qubit device, with up to 60 layers of two-qubit gates (2,880 CNOTs), and used ZNE to obtain expectation values that agreed with exactly solvable cases and that, they argued, were beyond brute-force classical simulation. Within weeks, several groups reproduced the results classically with tensor-network and Pauli-propagation methods, which exploited the specific structure of the problem. Both halves of the story matter: mitigated experiments at the 100-qubit scale really do produce trustworthy numbers, and "beyond classical simulation" claims for them are a moving target. Lecture 24 returns to how to read such claims.

tool — readout mitigation by hand, and a noisy simulator from calibration data 
import numpy as np
from qiskit import QuantumCircuit, transpile
from qiskit_aer import AerSimulator
from qiskit_ibm_runtime.fake_provider import FakeFez

# readout mitigation by hand for one qubit (Example 19.2) and for a pair (A tensor A)
e0, e1 = 0.02, 0.05                                   # P(read 1 | 0), P(read 0 | 1)
A = np.array([[1 - e0, e1], [e0, 1 - e1]])
print("one qubit:  observed P(1) = 0.30 -> mitigated", np.round(np.linalg.solve(A, [0.70, 0.30])[1], 4))
seen = np.kron(A, A) @ [0.5, 0, 0, 0.5]               # a perfect Bell pair read through noisy detectors
print("Bell pair:  seen", np.round(seen, 4), "-> mitigated", np.round(np.linalg.solve(np.kron(A, A), seen), 4) + 0.0)

# a 3-qubit GHZ state on a noisy simulator built from the FakeFez calibration snapshot
ghz = QuantumCircuit(3); ghz.h(0); ghz.cx(0, 1); ghz.cx(1, 2); ghz.measure_all()
for name, sim in (("ideal", AerSimulator(seed_simulator=19)), ("noisy", AerSimulator.from_backend(FakeFez(), seed_simulator=19))):
    tq = transpile(ghz, sim, optimization_level=1, seed_transpiler=19, initial_layout=[2, 3, 4] if name == "noisy" else None)
    counts = sim.run(tq, shots=4000).result().get_counts()
    good = (counts.get("000", 0) + counts.get("111", 0)) / 4000
    print(f"{name}: P(000 or 111) = {good:.4f}; other outcomes {sorted(k for k in counts if k not in ('000', '111'))}")

9 — Summary, exercises and Lab 5

summary
  • \(T_1\): population of \(|1\rangle\) decays as \(e^{-t/T_1}\) (amplitude damping). \(T_2\): coherence decays as \(e^{-t/T_2}\); \(1/T_2=1/(2T_1)+1/T_\varphi\), so \(T_2\le2T_1\).
  • Calibration data list gate, readout and two-qubit errors; two-qubit gates dominate. A rough success estimate is \((1-\varepsilon)^G\).
  • Transpilation chooses a layout, routes with SWAPs (3 CNOTs each), translates to the native basis and optimizes. Report gate counts after transpilation.
  • Readout mitigation inverts the confusion matrix: \(p_{\text{true}}(1)=(p_{\text{obs}}(1)-e_0)/(1-e_0-e_1)\) for one qubit; the result can be negative and must be projected back.
  • ZNE folds gates to scale the noise by \(\lambda=1,3,5\) and extrapolates to \(\lambda=0\). The model choice sets the bias; the extrapolation weights inflate the variance (by 5.2 for Richardson at 1, 3, 5).
  • Twirling turns coherent errors (which add in amplitude) into Pauli errors (which add in probability).
  • Mitigation costs grow exponentially with circuit size; only error correction scales.
