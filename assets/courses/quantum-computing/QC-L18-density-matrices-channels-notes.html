Every result in Blocks I and II assumed perfect gates and perfectly known states. Real devices are noisy (Lecture 17's resource estimates were all about that), and we often know a state only statistically. This lecture introduces the language for both situations. By the end you should be able to build the density matrix of an ensemble and use it to predict measurements, decide whether a state is pure, locate it in the Bloch ball, compute a reduced state with the partial trace, apply a channel given its Kraus operators, and compute the trace distance and fidelity of two qubit states.

1 — Two reasons vectors are not enough

Classical ignorance. A source prepares \(|0\rangle\) with probability \(\tfrac34\) and \(|{-i}\rangle\) with probability \(\tfrac14\), and does not tell us which. No single vector \(|\psi\rangle\) describes what we hold: the probability of each outcome of any measurement is an average over the two possibilities, and that average is not the Born rule for any one state.

Entanglement. Alice holds one qubit of an entangled pair (Lecture 5). Her qubit on its own has no state vector at all, since the pair is not a product. Yet she can measure it, and her statistics must be described by something that refers to her qubit only.

Both situations are handled by one object, and the second turns out to be a special case of the first.

2 — Density matrices

definition 18.1 — density matrix of an ensemble

An ensemble is a list of states \(|\psi_k\rangle\) prepared with probabilities \(p_k\). Its density matrix is

$$\rho=\sum_kp_k\,|\psi_k\rangle\langle\psi_k| .$$

A pure state \(|\psi\rangle\) has density matrix \(|\psi\rangle\langle\psi|\). For \(|\psi\rangle=\alpha|0\rangle+\beta|1\rangle\) this is the matrix \(\begin{pmatrix}|\alpha|^2&\alpha\bar\beta\\ \bar\alpha\beta&|\beta|^2\end{pmatrix}\).

The point of \(\rho\) is that it predicts every measurement. If we measure in an orthonormal basis \(\{|b\rangle\}\), the outcome \(b\) has probability \(\sum_kp_k|\langle b|\psi_k\rangle|^2\) (average over the ensemble, Born rule within each member), and \(|\langle b|\psi_k\rangle|^2=\langle b|\psi_k\rangle\langle\psi_k|b\rangle\). Summing,

$$P(b)=\langle b|\rho|b\rangle=\operatorname{tr}\big(\rho\,|b\rangle\langle b|\big).$$

After a gate \(U\), each member becomes \(U|\psi_k\rangle\), so \(\rho\mapsto U\rho U^\dagger\). Since all predictions go through \(\rho\), two ensembles with the same density matrix cannot be told apart by any experiment: \(\rho\) is the state.

A source prepares \(|1\rangle\) with probability 0.6 and \(|{+i}\rangle\) with probability 0.4. The qubit is measured in the Y basis. What is the probability of the outcome \({+i}\)?

Average over the ensemble, Born rule within each member: \(P({+i})=0.6\,|\langle{+i}|1\rangle|^2+0.4\,|\langle{+i}|{+i}\rangle|^2\).
\(0.6\cdot\tfrac12+0.4\cdot1=0.7\). The same number is \(\langle{+i}|\rho|{+i}\rangle\) for the ensemble's \(\rho\).
proposition 18.1 — what a density matrix looks like

Every density matrix \(\rho\) is (a) Hermitian, (b) positive semidefinite, \(\langle v|\rho|v\rangle\ge0\) for all \(v\), and (c) of trace 1. Conversely, every matrix with (a)–(c) is the density matrix of some ensemble, for example of its eigenvectors weighted by its eigenvalues. Moreover \(\operatorname{tr}\rho^2\le1\), with equality exactly when \(\rho\) is pure.

(a) Each \(|\psi\rangle\langle\psi|\) is Hermitian and real combinations of Hermitian matrices are Hermitian. (b) \(\langle v|\rho|v\rangle=\sum_kp_k|\langle v|\psi_k\rangle|^2\ge0\). (c) \(\operatorname{tr}|\psi\rangle\langle\psi|=\langle\psi|\psi\rangle=1\), and the \(p_k\) sum to 1. Conversely, by the spectral theorem (Lecture 2) a Hermitian \(\rho\) equals \(\sum_j\lambda_j|e_j\rangle\langle e_j|\) with orthonormal \(|e_j\rangle\); (b) makes \(\lambda_j\ge0\) and (c) makes them sum to 1, so they are probabilities. Finally \(\operatorname{tr}\rho^2=\sum_j\lambda_j^2\le\sum_j\lambda_j=1\), with equality iff one \(\lambda_j\) is 1 and the rest 0, that is, iff \(\rho=|e_j\rangle\langle e_j|\) is pure.

The quantity \(\operatorname{tr}\rho^2\) is the purity. For a qubit it ranges from 1 (pure) down to \(\tfrac12\), reached only by \(I/2\), the maximally mixed state: a fair coin between \(|0\rangle\) and \(|1\rangle\), and equally between any two orthogonal states.

3 — The Bloch ball

proposition 18.2 — every qubit state is a point of the ball

Every qubit density matrix can be written uniquely as

$$\rho=\tfrac12\big(I+xX+yY+zZ\big),$$ $$\vec r=(x,y,z)=\big(\operatorname{tr}\rho X,\ \operatorname{tr}\rho Y,\ \operatorname{tr}\rho Z\big),$$ $$|\vec r|\le1 .$$

The state is pure exactly when \(|\vec r|=1\) (on the sphere, matching Definition 3.3), its purity is \(\tfrac12(1+|\vec r|^2)\), and measuring along a unit vector \(\vec n\) gives \(+\) with probability \(\tfrac12(1+\vec r\cdot\vec n)\). The density matrix of an ensemble has the probability-weighted average of the members' Bloch vectors.

\(I,X,Y,Z\) form a basis of the \(2\times2\) Hermitian matrices, the coefficients are real for Hermitian \(\rho\), and \(\operatorname{tr}\rho=1\) fixes the coefficient of \(I\) at \(\tfrac12\). Since \(\operatorname{tr}(PQ)=2\delta_{PQ}\) for Paulis, \(\operatorname{tr}\rho X=x\), and so on. The eigenvalues of \(\vec r\cdot\vec\sigma\) are \(\pm|\vec r|\), so those of \(\rho\) are \(\tfrac12(1\pm|\vec r|)\): positivity is \(|\vec r|\le1\), and purity \(\sum\lambda^2=\tfrac12(1+|\vec r|^2)\). The projector onto \(+\vec n\) is \(\tfrac12(I+\vec n\cdot\vec\sigma)\), and \(\operatorname{tr}\big(\rho\,\tfrac12(I+\vec n\cdot\vec\sigma)\big)=\tfrac12(1+\vec r\cdot\vec n)\). Finally \(\rho\mapsto\vec r\) is linear, so mixtures average Bloch vectors.

Find the Bloch vector and the purity of \(\rho=\begin{pmatrix}0.7&0.2\\ 0.2&0.3\end{pmatrix}\).

Expand \(\tfrac12(I+xX+yY+zZ)\): the diagonal is \(\tfrac12(1\pm z)\) and \(\rho_{10}=\tfrac12(x+iy)\). Then purity \(=\tfrac12(1+|\vec r|^2)\).
\(z=0.7-0.3=0.4\), \(x=2\operatorname{Re}\rho_{10}=0.4\), \(y=0\); \(|\vec r|^2=0.32\) and the purity is \(0.66\): a point inside the ball.

Example 18.1 has \(\vec r=\tfrac34(0,0,1)+\tfrac14(0,-1,0)=(0,-\tfrac14,\tfrac34)\), of length \(\sqrt{10}/4\approx0.79\): inside the ball, on the chord from \(|0\rangle\) to \(|{-i}\rangle\), a quarter of the way along. Its purity \(\tfrac12(1+\tfrac{10}{16})=\tfrac{13}{16}\) agrees with the direct computation.

4 — The partial trace

Now the second situation. Let two qubits be in a state \(\rho_{AB}\) (pure or not), with \(A\) the left factor and \(B\) the right. What state describes \(A\) alone?

definition 18.2 — partial trace

The reduced state of \(A\) is \(\rho_A=\operatorname{tr}_B\rho_{AB}\), defined on product operators by \(\operatorname{tr}_B(M\otimes N)=M\operatorname{tr}(N)\) and extended linearly. In components, \((\rho_A)_{ij}=\sum_k(\rho_{AB})_{ik,jk}\): sum over the matching indices of \(B\). For a pure two-qubit state with coefficient matrix \(C\) (Definition 5.6, rows indexed by \(A\)), \(\rho_A=CC^\dagger\) and \(\rho_B=(C^\dagger C)^{\mathsf T}\).

Two qubits are in \(\tfrac{\sqrt3}{2}|00\rangle+\tfrac12|11\rangle\) (kets \(|q_1q_0\rangle\)). Find the probability that measuring \(q_1\) alone gives 0, and the purity of \(\rho_{q_1}\).

The coefficient matrix is \(C=\mathrm{diag}(\tfrac{\sqrt3}2,\tfrac12)\) with rows indexed by \(q_1\); then \(\rho_{q_1}=CC^\dagger\).
\(\rho_{q_1}=\mathrm{diag}(\tfrac34,\tfrac14)\), so \(P(0)=\tfrac34\) and the purity is \(\tfrac9{16}+\tfrac1{16}=\tfrac58\): the state is entangled, so its halves are mixed.
proposition 18.3 — the reduced state is the right one

For every observable or measurement on \(A\) alone, \(\operatorname{tr}\big(\rho_{AB}(M\otimes I)\big)=\operatorname{tr}(\rho_AM)\). So \(\rho_A\) predicts everything that can be learned by experiments on \(A\), and nothing done to \(B\) alone (any gate, any measurement whose result is not communicated) changes it.

The first identity is the definition, checked on products: \(\operatorname{tr}\big((P\otimes Q)(M\otimes I)\big)=\operatorname{tr}(PM)\operatorname{tr}(Q)=\operatorname{tr}\big(\operatorname{tr}_B(P\otimes Q)M\big)\), then linearity. For the second, a gate \(I\otimes U\) on \(B\) changes \(\rho_{AB}\) to \((I\otimes U)\rho_{AB}(I\otimes U^\dagger)\), and the cyclicity of the trace over \(B\) removes \(U\). A measurement on \(B\) with unknown outcome replaces \(\rho_{AB}\) by \(\sum_b(I\otimes\Pi_b)\rho_{AB}(I\otimes\Pi_b)\), and \(\sum_b\Pi_b=I\) gives the same \(\rho_A\).

In the same state \(\tfrac{\sqrt3}{2}|00\rangle+\tfrac12|11\rangle\), Bob measures \(q_0\) in the X basis and does not tell Alice the outcome. Alice's probability of getting 0 from \(q_1\) is now .

Proposition 18.3: a measurement on \(B\) whose result is not communicated does not change \(\rho_A\).
\(\rho_{q_1}\) is unchanged, so \(P(0)=\tfrac34\) still. Only when Bob sends his outcome can Alice condition on it, and that message travels no faster than light.
example 18.2 — half of a partly entangled pair

Let \(|\psi\rangle=\tfrac1{\sqrt3}\big(|00\rangle+|01\rangle+|11\rangle\big)\), kets \(|q_1q_0\rangle\). Find the reduced state of each qubit, its purity and its eigenvalues.

The coefficient matrix is \(C=\tfrac1{\sqrt3}\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\ 0&1\end{pmatrix}\) (rows: \(q_1\)). Then

$$\rho_{q_1}=CC^\dagger=\frac13\begin{pmatrix}2&1\\[2pt] 1&1\end{pmatrix},\qquad \rho_{q_0}=(C^\dagger C)^{\mathsf T}=\frac13\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\[2pt] 1&2\end{pmatrix}.$$

Both have trace 1, determinant \(\tfrac19\) and purity \(\tfrac{4+1+1+1}{9}=\tfrac79\), and eigenvalues \(\tfrac{3\pm\sqrt5}{6}\approx0.873,\ 0.127\). These are the squares of the Schmidt coefficients of \(|\psi\rangle\) (Lecture 5): for a pure state of two parts, the two reduced states always have the same eigenvalues, and they are pure exactly when the state is a product. The less pure the halves, the more entangled the whole.

No signalling, made precise. Proposition 18.3 is the reason entanglement cannot transmit information. In teleportation (Lecture 8), before Alice's two bits arrive, Bob's qubit is in one of four states \(P|\psi\rangle\), \(P\in\{I,X,Z,XZ\}\), each with probability \(\tfrac14\), so his density matrix is \(\tfrac14\sum_PP\rho P^\dagger\). This average is \(I/2\) for every qubit state (HW10 Problem 1 asks you to show it): Bob holds a maximally mixed qubit, independent of \(|\psi\rangle\), until the classical bits tell him which correction to apply.

5 — Quantum channels

Noise is anything that happens to a qubit that is not a known unitary: a random gate error, leakage of information to the environment, energy loss. The most general physically allowed transformation of a state has a simple form.

definition 18.3 — quantum channel (Kraus form)

A channel is a map

$$\Phi(\rho)=\sum_kK_k\,\rho\,K_k^\dagger\qquad\text{with}\qquad \sum_kK_k^\dagger K_k=I .$$

The matrices \(K_k\) are its Kraus operators. The condition on the right guarantees that \(\Phi(\rho)\) has trace 1; \(\Phi(\rho)\) is automatically Hermitian and positive semidefinite.

One way to read the formula: with probability \(\operatorname{tr}(K_k\rho K_k^\dagger)\) the event \(k\) happens and the state becomes \(K_k\rho K_k^\dagger\) (normalised), and we do not learn which event happened. A unitary is a channel with one Kraus operator. "Measure in the standard basis and forget the result" has Kraus operators \(|0\rangle\langle0|\) and \(|1\rangle\langle1|\), and it kills the coherences.

Four channels describe most single-qubit noise. Each sends the Bloch ball into itself, and since \(\Phi\) is linear, it acts on Bloch vectors as an affine map \(\vec r\mapsto M\vec r+\vec c\): the ball becomes an ellipsoid.

  • Bit flip with probability \(p\): Kraus operators \(\sqrt{1-p}\,I\) and \(\sqrt p\,X\). Since \(X\) negates the \(y\) and \(z\) components (Lecture 4), the Bloch vector becomes \((x,\ (1-2p)y,\ (1-2p)z)\).
  • Phase flip (dephasing) with probability \(p\): \(\sqrt{1-p}\,I\) and \(\sqrt p\,Z\). It maps \(\vec r\) to \(((1-2p)x,\ (1-2p)y,\ z)\): populations survive, coherences decay. This is the \(T_2\) process of Lecture 19.
  • Depolarizing with parameter \(p\) (the convention used in this course): Kraus operators \(\sqrt{1-3p/4}\,I\) and \(\tfrac{\sqrt p}{2}X\), \(\tfrac{\sqrt p}{2}Y\), \(\tfrac{\sqrt p}{2}Z\), "a random Pauli error". HW10 asks you to find its effect on the Bloch vector; the figure shows the answer's shape.
  • Amplitude damping with parameter \(\gamma\): spontaneous decay from \(|1\rangle\) to \(|0\rangle\) with probability \(\gamma\), the energy-loss (\(T_1\)) process of superconducting qubits. Unlike the others it is not a mixture of unitaries, and it moves the ball toward the north pole. HW10 asks you to write its Kraus operators and find its fixed point.

The phase-flip channel with \(p=0.2\) acts on \(|+\rangle\). The result is then measured in the X basis. What is \(P(+)\)?

\(|+\rangle\) has \(\vec r=(1,0,0)\), and the phase flip multiplies \(x\) by \(1-2p\). Then \(P(+)=\tfrac12(1+x)\) (Proposition 18.2).
\(x=1-0.4=0.6\), so \(P(+)=0.8\). Before the channel the outcome \(+\) was certain; dephasing destroys exactly this kind of information.

idea — noise is entanglement with the environment

Every channel arises by letting the qubit interact unitarily with an environment that starts in a fixed state and then forgetting (tracing out) the environment; this is Stinespring's theorem. The simplest case: a CNOT from the qubit onto an environment qubit in \(|0\rangle\) copies the \(Z\) value into the environment. Tracing out the environment leaves the qubit with its populations intact and its coherences gone: the phase-flip channel with \(p=\tfrac12\). The second code box in Section 7 does exactly this. Decoherence is not the qubit "getting tired"; it is information about the qubit leaking into things we do not control, which is why isolating qubits is the central engineering problem.

6 — How far apart are two states?

definition 18.4 — trace distance and fidelity

The trace distance is \(D(\rho,\sigma)=\tfrac12\sum_j|\lambda_j|\), the eigenvalues \(\lambda_j\) being those of \(\rho-\sigma\). The fidelity is \(F(\rho,\sigma)=\big(\operatorname{tr}\sqrt{\sqrt\rho\,\sigma\sqrt\rho}\big)^2\); if \(\sigma=|\psi\rangle\langle\psi|\) is pure, \(F=\langle\psi|\rho|\psi\rangle\).

proposition 18.4 — distances for qubits, and what they mean

For qubit states with Bloch vectors \(\vec r\) and \(\vec s\):

$$D(\rho,\sigma)=\tfrac12|\vec r-\vec s|,\qquad F(\rho,\sigma)=\tfrac12\Big(1+\vec r\cdot\vec s+\sqrt{(1-|\vec r|^2)(1-|\vec s|^2)}\Big).$$

Operationally: given one copy of a state that is \(\rho\) or \(\sigma\) with equal probability, the best possible measurement guesses correctly with probability \(\tfrac12+\tfrac12D(\rho,\sigma)\) (Helstrom). No channel can increase the trace distance: \(D(\Phi(\rho),\Phi(\sigma))\le D(\rho,\sigma)\).

Proof sketch. \(\rho-\sigma=\tfrac12(\vec r-\vec s)\cdot\vec\sigma\) has eigenvalues \(\pm\tfrac12|\vec r-\vec s|\). The fidelity formula is a \(2\times2\) computation (checked numerically in verify_L18.py). For Helstrom's bound and contractivity, see Watrous Ch. 9 or Nielsen–Chuang §9.2.

Let \(\rho=\mathrm{diag}(\tfrac14,\tfrac34)\) and \(\sigma=|1\rangle\langle1|\). Find \(D(\rho,\sigma)\) and the best probability of guessing correctly, from one copy, which of the two (equally likely) you hold.

Bloch vectors: \(\vec r=(0,0,-\tfrac12)\) and \(\vec s=(0,0,-1)\). Use \(D=\tfrac12|\vec r-\vec s|\) and Helstrom's \(\tfrac12+\tfrac12D\).
\(D=\tfrac12\cdot\tfrac12=\tfrac14\), and the best guess is right with probability \(\tfrac12+\tfrac18=\tfrac58\): the two states are hard to tell apart.
example 18.3 — how far is the shuffled source from |0⟩?

Compute \(D\) and \(F\) between Example 18.1's \(\rho\) and \(|0\rangle\langle0|\), and \(D\) between \(\rho\) and \(I/2\).

\(\vec r=(0,-\tfrac14,\tfrac34)\) and \(\vec s=(0,0,1)\): \(D=\tfrac12\big|(0,-\tfrac14,-\tfrac14)\big|=\tfrac{\sqrt2}{8}\approx0.177\). Since \(|0\rangle\) is pure, \(F=\langle0|\rho|0\rangle=\tfrac78\). So the best single-shot test tells \(\rho\) from \(|0\rangle\) only with probability \(\tfrac12+\tfrac12\cdot0.177\approx0.588\). From the centre: \(D(\rho,I/2)=\tfrac12|\vec r|=\tfrac{\sqrt{10}}8\approx0.395\).

Both quantities are used constantly from here on: fidelity is how hardware papers report the quality of a gate or a prepared state (Lecture 19), and trace distance is how cryptographers bound what an eavesdropper can learn (Lecture 20).

tool — density matrices and partial traces (qiskit.quantum_info, tested with qiskit 2.5)

zero = Statevector([1, 0])
minus_i = Statevector([1, -1j]) / np.sqrt(2)
rho = 0.75 * DensityMatrix(zero) + 0.25 * DensityMatrix(minus_i)      # Example 18.1

tool — channels, fidelity, and decoherence by an environment 
import numpy as np
from qiskit import QuantumCircuit
from qiskit.quantum_info import DensityMatrix, Statevector, Kraus, SparsePauliOp, partial_trace, state_fidelity

I2 = np.eye(2); X = np.array([[0, 1], [1, 0]]); Z = np.diag([1, -1])
p = 0.3
bit_flip = Kraus([np.sqrt(1 - p) * I2, np.sqrt(p) * X])
phase_flip = Kraus([np.sqrt(1 - p) * I2, np.sqrt(p) * Z])
bloch = lambda r: [round(float(r.expectation_value(SparsePauliOp(P)).real), 3) + 0.0 for P in "XYZ"]

plus_i = DensityMatrix(Statevector([1, 1j]) / np.sqrt(2))              # Bloch vector (0, 1, 0)
for name, ch in (("bit flip", bit_flip), ("phase flip", phase_flip)):
    out = plus_i.evolve(ch)
    print(f"{name:10s}: Bloch {bloch(out)}, fidelity with |+i> {state_fidelity(out, plus_i):.3f}")

# decoherence as entanglement with an environment: CNOT from the qubit (q0) onto a fresh environment qubit (q1)
qc = QuantumCircuit(2)
qc.ry(2 * np.arccos(np.sqrt(0.8)), 0)                               # system: sqrt(0.8)|0> + sqrt(0.2)|1>, Bloch (0.8, 0, 0.6)
qc.cx(0, 1)                                                          # the environment "learns" the Z value
sys = partial_trace(Statevector.from_instruction(qc), [1])           # forget the environment
print("system after the environment looked:", np.round(sys.data.real, 3).tolist(), " Bloch", bloch(sys))
bit flip  : Bloch [0.0, 0.4, 0.0], fidelity with |+i> 0.700
phase flip: Bloch [0.0, 0.4, 0.0], fidelity with |+i> 0.700
system after the environment looked: [[0.8, 0.0], [0.0, 0.2]]  Bloch [0.0, 0.0, 0.6]

Both flips shorten the \(y\) component of \(|{+i}\rangle\) from 1 to \(1-2p=0.4\), so the fidelity drops to \(\tfrac12(1+0.4)=0.7\). The last line is the idea box of Section 5: after the CNOT, the qubit's Bloch vector \((0.8,0,0.6)\) has lost its \(x\) component, exactly as a phase flip with \(p=\tfrac12\) would do.

8 — Summary and exercises

summary
  • A density matrix \(\rho=\sum p_k|\psi_k\rangle\langle\psi_k|\) predicts every measurement, \(P(b)=\langle b|\rho|b\rangle\). Density matrices are exactly the Hermitian, positive semidefinite, trace-1 matrices; \(\rho\) is pure iff \(\operatorname{tr}\rho^2=1\). Ensembles with the same \(\rho\) are indistinguishable.
  • Qubit states fill the Bloch ball: \(\rho=\tfrac12(I+\vec r\cdot\vec\sigma)\), \(|\vec r|\le1\), pure on the sphere, \(I/2\) at the centre.
  • The partial trace gives the state of a subsystem; it predicts all local statistics and is unaffected by anything done elsewhere. For pure states of two parts, the reduced states' eigenvalues are the squared Schmidt coefficients.
  • Channels: \(\Phi(\rho)=\sum K\rho K^\dagger\) with \(\sum K^\dagger K=I\). Bit flip, phase flip, depolarizing and amplitude damping deform the ball into ellipsoids; every channel is a unitary interaction with an environment that is then forgotten.
  • For qubits, \(D=\tfrac12|\vec r-\vec s|\), the best distinguishing probability is \(\tfrac12(1+D)\), channels never increase \(D\), and \(F=\langle\psi|\rho|\psi\rangle\) against a pure state.
